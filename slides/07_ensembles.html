Ensemble methods – idea!

Class exercise: how much does an Apple AirTag cost? Give independent answers.

Each guess is noisy, but the average of the class is usually close to the real price (39 €).

Averaging reduces variance (if independent):

$$\mathrm{Var}\big(\hat f_{\text{bag}}(\bx)\big)=\frac1M\mathrm{Var}\big(f(\bx)\big)$$

With correlation $\rho$ between the models:

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar h)=\rho\,\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2$$

More models never hurt, but the floor $\rho\sigma^2$ stays: to go lower, make the models less correlated.

Ensemble methods – Why averaging works (proof)

$B$ models at a fixed point $\bx$, each with variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$. Their average is $\bar h=\frac1B\sum_bh_b$.

1 · The variance of a sum is the sum of all variances plus all covariances: $B$ variances and $B(B-1)$ covariances $\rho\sigma^2$.

$$\mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_bh_b\Big)=B\sigma^2+B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2$$

2 · Divide by $B^2$ (the $\frac1B$ inside the average, squared):

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar h)=\frac{\sigma^2}{B}+\frac{B-1}{B}\rho\sigma^2=\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2\ \ \blacksquare$$
  • The bias doesn't change, so average low-bias models: deep trees.
  • As $B\to\infty$ the second term vanishes: more trees never overfit, the error levels off.

We only have one training set. To get "different" ones, draw $m$ examples with replacement.

Each draw misses a given example with probability $1-\frac1m$; there are $m$ draws:

$$P(\text{left out})=\Big(1-\frac1m\Big)^m\xrightarrow{m\to\infty}e^{-1}\approx0.368$$

For m = 10 it is $0.9^{10}=0.349$. Each sample holds ~63 % of the distinct examples. The rest are out-of-bag (OOB): predict each point with only the trees that never saw it, and you get a nearly free estimate of the test error.

Ensemble methods – Bagging

Bootstrap aggregating:

  • Train K classifiers on K bootstrap sub-samples of the dataset.
  • Aggregate the output: average (regression) or vote / average the probabilities (classification).
  • A good, stable global model.

Works for any unstable model: deep trees are the classic case. A stable model like linear regression gains almost nothing.

Ensemble methods – Random Forest

Random Forests contain N different decision "weaker" trees.

  • Step 1: divide the dataset into N (bootstrap) subsets.
  • Step 2: train (in parallel) the decision trees on the selected data points and a random subset of the features at every split.
  • Step 3: for new data points, find the prediction of each decision tree.
  • Step 4: ensemble the results from all trees (majority vote).

Many ways to ensemble: average (regression), majority vote, or mix in probability.

Why the random features? Bagged trees are still correlated: a strong feature ends up at the top of almost every tree. Random features lower $\rho$, and the variance floor with it.

Random Forest – Review

  • Pros:
    • They reduce the overfitting risks of decision trees.
    • Very good accuracy on classification tasks.
    • Very used!
    • Work well on non-linear data.
    • Robust to outliers (many trees will not learn them).
  • Cons:
    • Slower training.
    • Less interpretable than a decision tree.
    • Not so suitable for very sparse features.

Boosting

Bagging: train a bunch of individual models in a parallel way. Each model is trained on a random subset of the data.

Boosting: train a bunch of individual models in a sequential way. Each individual model learns from the mistakes made by the previous model.

$$F_M(\bx)=F_0(\bx)+\nu\sum_{t=1}^Mh_t(\bx)$$

Many weak, shallow trees added one after another.

AdaBoost

Weak learners

Usually decision stumps: trees with a single split.

Weights on data points

Every example starts with equal weight. After each learner, the weights of the misclassified examples are increased: the next learner focuses on the hard ones.

Weights on models

Each learner gets a vote from its error: $\alpha_t=\frac12\log\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}$. Final prediction: a weighted vote.

Gradient Boosting

Gradient Boosting learns from the mistake, the residual error, directly, rather than updating the weights of data points.

  1. Start with a constant: $F_0=\bar y$.
  2. Compute the residuals $r^{(i)}=y^{(i)}-F(\bx^{(i)})$.
  3. Fit a small tree $h_t$ to the residuals.
  4. Update $F\leftarrow F+\nu\,h_t$ and repeat from 2.

For any loss, the residual is replaced by the negative gradient $-\partial\ell/\partial F$: gradient descent in function space. For log-loss it is $y-p$.

XGBoost

  • Extremely efficient and powerful for tabular data.
  • Used in many Kaggle-winning solutions.
  • Not so good for formatted data (sequences, images, …).
  • For those cases deep learning performs the best!

For squared loss, the value XGBoost puts in a leaf is roughly

$$w=\frac{\sum_{i\in\text{leaf}}r^{(i)}}{N_{\text{leaf}}+\lambda}$$

the mean residual, shrunk towards 0 by reg_lambda. Splits that gain less than gamma are not made.
