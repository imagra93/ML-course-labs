Regularization

$$J(\bth)+\lambda\,R(\bth)$$

Regularization – Why?

Three problems that all show up as large, unstable parameters:

  1. Overfitting: many features (or a high polynomial degree) relative to $m$. The model fits the noise; the $\theta_j$ become huge with alternating signs.
  2. Collinearity: correlated features make $\bX^\top\bX$ nearly singular. Small changes in the data change $\bth$ a lot.
  3. Separable classes in logistic regression: $\norm{\bth}\to\infty$.

Regularization accepts a little more training error (bias) in exchange for a more stable model (less variance).

Regularization – The idea in one picture

The degree-15 curve on the previous slide ($\lambda=0$) wiggles to reach the noisy points. Written as a plain polynomial $\theta_0+\theta_1t+\dots+\theta_{15}t^{15}$ (with $t=2x-1$), its coefficients are

$$\bth\approx(0,\ -4,\ 3,\ 90,\ -70,\ -1\,267,\ 373,\ 8\,302,\ \dots)\quad\text{up to }\approx49\,000$$

Wiggles need huge coefficients with alternating signs. A smooth curve has small ones. So: make large coefficients expensive.

$$\text{minimize}\quad\underbrace{J(\bth)}_{\text{fit the data}}\ +\ \lambda\cdot\underbrace{\text{size}(\bth)}_{\text{a tax on big }\theta\text{'s}}$$
  • $\lambda$ is the tax rate. $\lambda=0$: no tax, plain least squares. $\lambda\to\infty$: any non-zero $\theta_j$ is ruinous, all go to 0, a flat line.
  • In between, the model gives up a little training error to keep $\bth$ small. Less wiggle, better on new data.

Two ways to measure "size":

Namesize$(\bth)$
L2 · Ridge$\sum_{j=1}^n\theta_j^2$
L1 · Lasso$\sum_{j=1}^n|\theta_j|$

Squares punish big coefficients hard and small ones hardly at all. Absolute values punish every coefficient at the same rate, even tiny ones. That difference is the whole story of Ridge vs Lasso.

Bias–variance: a bit more bias (worse fit on train) for a lot less variance (stability on new data).

Regularization – The regularized cost

$$J_{\text{reg}}(\bth)=\underbrace{J(\bth)}_{\text{fit the data}}+\underbrace{\lambda\,R(\bth)}_{\text{keep }\bth\text{ small}},\qquad\lambda\ge0$$
NamePenalty $R(\bth)$Effect
L2 · Ridge · weight decay$\sum_{j=1}^n\theta_j^2=\norm{\bth}_2^2$shrinks all coefficients smoothly
L1 · Lasso$\sum_{j=1}^n|\theta_j|=\norm{\bth}_1$sets some exactly to 0 (feature selection)
Elastic Neta mix of bothsparse and stable with correlated features

Two rules that always apply:

  • Do not penalize the intercept $\theta_0$ (the sums start at $j=1$). It only sets the average level of $y$; shrinking it would bias every prediction.
  • Standardize the features first. The size of $\theta_j$ depends on the units of $x_j$: income in € vs k€ changes it 1000×. Without scaling, the tax is arbitrary.

$\lambda$ is a hyperparameter, chosen by cross-validation (later slide). scikit-learn often writes $C=1/\lambda$: large $C$ = weak regularization.

Regularization – L2 (Ridge): the gradient, slowly

We write the L2 penalty with a $\frac{1}{2m}$ in front, only so that the derivative comes out clean:

$$J_{\text{reg}}(\bth)=J(\bth)+\frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^n\theta_j^2$$

Step 1 · derivative of the penalty with respect to one $\theta_j$. Only the $j$-th term of the sum contains $\theta_j$; the others are constants:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_{k=1}^n\theta_k^2\Big)=\frac{\lambda}{2m}\cdot2\theta_j=\frac{\lambda}{m}\,\theta_j$$

Step 2 · add it to the data gradient (whatever $J$ is: least squares or cross-entropy):

$$\frac{\partial J_{\text{reg}}}{\partial\theta_j}=\frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_j}+\frac{\lambda}{m}\,\theta_j\qquad(j\ge1;\ \text{for }\theta_0\text{ nothing changes})$$

Step 3 · plug into gradient descent and regroup the $\theta_j$ terms:

$$\theta_j:=\theta_j-\alpha\Big(\frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_j}+\frac{\lambda}{m}\theta_j\Big)$$ $$=\hl{\theta_j\Big(1-\frac{\alpha\lambda}{m}\Big)}-\alpha\frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_j}$$

Weight decay. Every step first multiplies $\theta_j$ by a number slightly below 1 (e.g. $\alpha=0.1$, $\lambda=1$, $m=100$: by 0.999), then takes the usual gradient step. Coefficients that the data does not keep pushing up slowly decay towards 0.

This is exactly what weight_decay does in neural-network optimizers.

Regularization – L2 (Ridge): the closed form, slowly

For linear regression, $J=\frac1{2m}\norm{\bX\bth-\by}^2$ and its gradient is (chapter 02) $\ \nabla J=\frac1m\bX^\top(\bX\bth-\by)$.

Step 1 · the penalty gradient as a matrix. Step 1 of the previous slide for all $j$ at once, with a 0 for the intercept:

$$\nabla\Big(\frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_{j\ge1}\theta_j^2\Big)=\frac{\lambda}{m}\bL\bth,\qquad\bL=\mathrm{diag}(0,1,\dots,1)$$

Step 2 · set the total gradient to zero and drop the common $\frac1m$:

$$\bX^\top(\bX\bth-\by)+\lambda\bL\bth=\bzero$$

Step 3 · collect the $\bth$ terms on the left, the rest on the right:

$$\bX^\top\bX\bth+\lambda\bL\bth=\bX^\top\by\ \Rightarrow\ (\bX^\top\bX+\lambda\bL)\,\bth=\bX^\top\by$$
$$\bth^*_{\text{ridge}}=(\bX^\top\bX+\lambda\bL)^{-1}\bX^\top\by$$

Compare with the normal equation $(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\by$: Ridge just adds $\lambda$ to the diagonal of $\bX^\top\bX$ (except the intercept entry).

One standardized feature ($\bX^\top\bX=m$, no intercept). Let $z=\bX^\top\by/m$ be the OLS coefficient:

$$(m+\lambda)\,\theta=\bX^\top\by=m\,z\ \Rightarrow\ \theta_{\text{ridge}}=\frac{z}{1+\lambda/m}$$

Ridge divides. Smaller than OLS, but never exactly zero.

The inverse always exists for $\lambda\gt0$, even with duplicated features or $n\gt m$: for $\bv\ne\bzero$, $\ \bv^\top(\bX^\top\bX+\lambda\bL)\bv=\norm{\bX\bv}^2+\lambda\sum_{j\ge1}v_j^2\gt0$ (if all $v_{j\ge1}=0$ then $\bX\bv=v_0\mathbf 1\ne\bzero$). Positive definite → a unique minimum.

Regularization – L1 (Lasso): why exact zeros?

Same one-feature setting: OLS coefficient $z$, and (up to a constant) the cost is a parabola centred at $z$ plus the L1 tax:

$$g(\theta)=\tfrac12(\theta-z)^2+\lambda|\theta|$$

$|\theta|$ has a kink at 0: slope $+\lambda$ on the right, $-\lambda$ on the left, no derivative at 0. So we look at the three cases separately.

If $\theta\gt0$: $\ g'=\theta-z+\lambda=0\Rightarrow\theta=z-\lambda$, valid only if $z\gt\lambda$.

If $\theta\lt0$: $\ g'=\theta-z-\lambda=0\Rightarrow\theta=z+\lambda$, valid only if $z\lt-\lambda$.

Otherwise ($|z|\le\lambda$) the minimum sits at the kink: $\theta=0$.

$$\theta_{\text{lasso}}=\mathrm{sign}(z)\,\max\big(|z|-\lambda,\,0\big)$$

Lasso subtracts, Ridge divides. The L2 penalty's slope $2\theta$ vanishes near 0, so it never pushes all the way. The L1 penalty pushes with constant force $\lambda$, however small $\theta$ is: anything with $|z|\le\lambda$ is pushed exactly to 0.

Regularization – The geometric picture

Minimizing $J+\lambda R$ is the same as minimizing $J$ subject to $R(\bth)\le t$. The solution (green) is where the contours of $J$ first touch the region. The L1 diamond has corners on the axes, so it usually touches at a corner, where $\theta_2=0$.

Elastic Net: $\ \lambda\Big[\rho\norm{\bth}_1+\frac{1-\rho}{2}\norm{\bth}_2^2\Big],\ \rho\in[0,1]$.

Lasso is unstable on correlated features: it keeps one of near-duplicates arbitrarily, or splits the weight erratically. The L2 part makes them share the weight; the L1 part still gives sparsity.

Ridge shrinks everything gradually. Lasso switches coefficients off one by one: at a moderate λ only the useful features survive. Which one of a correlated pair Lasso keeps is somewhat arbitrary.

Regularization – A prior in disguise (MAP)

Bayes: $p(\bth\mid\mathcal D)\propto p(\mathcal D\mid\bth)\,p(\bth)$. Maximize the posterior:

$$\hat\bth_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\min_{\bth}\big[\underbrace{-\log p(\mathcal D\mid\bth)}_{\text{loss}}\ \underbrace{-\log p(\bth)}_{\text{penalty}}\big]$$
  • Gaussian prior $\theta_j\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)$: $\ -\log p(\bth)=\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_j\theta_j^2+c$ → L2.
  • Laplace prior $p(\theta_j)\propto e^{-|\theta_j|/b}$: $\ -\log p(\bth)=\frac1b\sum_j|\theta_j|+c$ → L1.

For linear regression with noise $\sigma^2$: MAP = Ridge with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$. A strong prior (small $\tau$) or noisy data (large $\sigma$) means more regularization.

Regularization – Choosing λ

  • Training error always prefers $\lambda=0$ (blue): it can't choose λ.
  • Validation error (orange) has a minimum: that is the λ we want.
  • Search λ on a log grid with $k$-fold cross-validation.
  • Put the scaling inside the CV loop (scikit-learn Pipeline), or the validation fold leaks into $\mu,\sigma$.

RidgeCV, LassoCV, LogisticRegressionCV do this for you.

Regularization – Logistic regression

$$J(\bth)=-\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\Big[y^{(i)}\log h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})+(1-y^{(i)})\log\big(1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)\Big]+\frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^n\theta_j^2$$
$$\nabla J=\frac1m\Big[\bX^\top\big(g(\bX\bth)-\by\big)+\lambda\mathbf L\bth\Big]$$
  • The Hessian gains $+\frac\lambda m\mathbf L$: strictly convex, and it always has a finite solution, even for separable data.
  • L1 works the same way and gives sparse logistic models.
  • scikit-learn's LogisticRegression is L2-regularized by default with $C=1/\lambda=1$. Large $C$ = weak regularization.

Regularization – Input variables

Scale numerics

Standardize before GD and before any penalty. Compute $\mu_j,\sigma_j$ on the training data only and reuse them everywhere. After standardizing, $\theta_j$ = effect of +1 standard deviation: comparable across features.

Encode categoricals

Ordinal codes for ordered levels, one-hot for unordered ones (chapter 02: "which encoder when?"). One-hot: keep $K-1$ dummies with an intercept (dummy trap). With L2, keeping all $K$ is also fine: the penalty makes the solution unique. High-cardinality categories: group rare levels, or target-encode inside the CV folds.

Add transforms

curved$x^2$, splines
skewed$\log(1+x)$

More features means more variance: pair them with regularization and CV. Watch for leakage features, known only after the outcome (e.g. "claim paid amount" when predicting fraud at claim opening).

Regularization – Collinearity

Under the model assumptions, with $\hat\bth=(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\by$ and $\by=\bX\bth+\boldsymbol\varepsilon$:

$$\hat\bth=\bth+(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\boldsymbol\varepsilon\quad\Rightarrow\quad\E[\hat\bth]=\bth\ \ \text{(unbiased)}$$
$$\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\bth)=\sigma^2(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}$$

Nearly dependent columns → $\bX^\top\bX$ has a tiny eigenvalue → its inverse has a huge one → the variance of the coefficients explodes. Predictions barely suffer; interpretations do.

$$\mathrm{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}$$

$R_j^2$ = the $R^2$ of regressing $x_j$ on all the other features. VIF above 5–10 signals a problem. Remedies: drop or combine features, or use Ridge.

Not assumed:

  • normally distributed features,
  • a linear relation between $\bx$ and $p$ itself (it is S-shaped by design),
  • equal variance: $p(1-p)$ is part of the model.

There is no error term, so no normality or homoscedasticity is needed.

Regularization – Summary and lab

  • Regularization = loss + $\lambda\times$ penalty; don't penalize $\theta_0$; standardize first; choose $\lambda$ by CV.
  • L2 (Ridge): closed form $(\bX^\top\bX+\lambda\mathbf L)^{-1}\bX^\top\by$, always unique; shrinks, never zeroes; Gaussian prior; weight decay in GD.
  • L1 (Lasso): soft-thresholding, exact zeros, feature selection; Laplace prior. Elastic Net for correlated features.
  • Logistic regression: L2 guarantees a finite solution; scikit-learn uses $C=1/\lambda$.
  • Inputs: scale, encode (ordinal or one-hot with $K-1$ dummies), transforms, watch collinearity (VIF) and leakage. Practical guide: "what to do when…".
