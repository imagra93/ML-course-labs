Approximate $y$ as a linear function of $\bx$:

$$h(\bx)=\sum_{j=0}^n\theta_j\cdot x_j=\bth^\top\bx$$

First perform linear regression and then establish a threshold, e.g. 0.5.

One extra data point leads to bad results. A clearly malignant tumour far to the right drags the line, and the 0.5 crossing moves into the malignant cases.

Also: $\bth^\top\bx$ is unbounded, so predictions like −0.3 or 1.4 are not probabilities.

Logistic Regression – Sigmoid function.

The sigmoid function:

  • $0\le g(z)\le1$ is bounded.
  • $g(z)$ can be interpreted as the probability that the class is 1.
    • $h_{\bth}(\bx)=P(Y=1\mid\bx;\bth)$
    • $P(Y=0\mid\bx;\bth)=1-h_{\bth}(\bx)$
  • Easy to establish a threshold (or at least it makes sense to establish one).

Approximate $y$ as a non-linear function of $\bx$:

$$h_{\bth}(\bx)=g(\bth^\top\bx)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\bth^\top\bx}},$$

where

$$g(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$
  • $g(z)$ is the sigmoid (logistic) function.

Logistic Regression – Sigmoid properties

Four facts we use later. Each one is a two-line proof.

1 · Range. $g(z)\in(0,1)$, $\ g(0)=\tfrac12$, $\ g(z)\to1$ as $z\to\infty$, $\ g(z)\to0$ as $z\to-\infty$.

2 · Symmetry. $1-g(z)=g(-z)$:

$$1-\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}=\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=\frac{1}{e^{z}+1}=g(-z)$$

3 · Derivative. $g'(z)=g(z)\big(1-g(z)\big)$:

$$g'(z)=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=g(z)\,(1-g(z))$$

4 · Inverse (the logit). If $p=g(z)$ then $z=\log\frac{p}{1-p}$:

$$p(1+e^{-z})=1\ \Rightarrow\ e^{-z}=\frac{1-p}{p}\ \Rightarrow\ z=\log\frac{p}{1-p}$$

Logistic Regression – Odds: another way to say "how likely"

Let $p$ be the probability that a claim is fraud. The odds of fraud are

$$\text{odds}=\frac{p}{1-p}=\frac{\text{probability it happens}}{\text{probability it does not}}$$
  • $p=0.75$: odds $=\frac{0.75}{0.25}=3$. "Three to one": fraud is 3 times more likely than not.
  • $p=0.5$: odds $=1$. "Even": as likely as not.
  • $p=0.1$: odds $=\frac{0.1}{0.9}=0.11$. Fraud is 9 times less likely than not.

Going back: $\ p=\dfrac{\text{odds}}{1+\text{odds}}$.  (odds 3 → $p=\frac34$.)

Odds live in $(0,\infty)$ and are lopsided: $p=0.9$ gives 9 but $p=0.1$ gives $0.11$. Hard to add or compare. The logarithm fixes that.

Logistic Regression – Log-odds (the logit) and its properties

$$\operatorname{logit}(p)=\log\frac{p}{1-p}$$

The logarithm of the odds: the log-odds. In logistic regression it is also called the score.

1 · Range. $p\in(0,1)$ → odds $\in(0,\infty)$ → log-odds $\in(-\infty,+\infty)$: any real number. Exactly what a linear function $\bth^\top\bx$ can produce.

2 · Zero in the middle. $\operatorname{logit}(0.5)=\log1=0$. Positive when $p\gt0.5$, negative when $p\lt0.5$.

3 · Symmetric. Swapping the classes only flips the sign: $\ \log\frac{1-p}{p}=-\log\frac{p}{1-p}$.

4 · Products become sums. Multiplying the odds by $c$ adds $\log c$: $\ \log(c\cdot\text{odds})=\log c+\log(\text{odds})$.

5 · Its inverse is the sigmoid. Solve $z=\log\frac{p}{1-p}$ for $p$:

$$e^{z}=\frac{p}{1-p}\ \Rightarrow\ e^{z}-pe^{z}=p\ \Rightarrow\ p=\frac{e^{z}}{1+e^{z}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}=g(z)$$

Logistic Regression – The linear score is the log-odds

Apply the logit to $p=h_{\bth}(\bx)=g(\bth^\top\bx)$ (property 5 undoes the sigmoid): the log-odds are linear in $\bx$:

$$\log\frac{p}{1-p}=\bth^\top\bx=\theta_0+\theta_1x_1+\dots+\theta_nx_n$$

So logistic regression is a linear regression of the log-odds. That is where the name comes from.

Reading $\theta_j$. Increasing $x_j$ by one unit (others fixed) adds $\theta_j$ to the log-odds, i.e. multiplies the odds $\frac{p}{1-p}$ by $e^{\theta_j}$ (the odds ratio).

Example: $\theta_j=0.7$ means the odds roughly double per unit of $x_j$ ($e^{0.7}\approx2$).

The effect on the probability itself is not constant: it is largest near $p=0.5$.

Worked example. Fraud model with one feature, claim amount in k€: $\theta_0=-3$, $\theta_1=0.7$.

Logistic Regression – Cost function.

If we want to use the square error cost, the function $J(\bth)$ would no longer be convex. We define the Binary Cross-Entropy loss:

$$L\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)}),y^{(i)}\big)=\begin{cases}-\log\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)&\text{if }y^{(i)}=1\\-\log\big(1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)&\text{if }y^{(i)}=0\end{cases}$$

or in 1 line,

$$L=-y^{(i)}\log\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)-(1-y^{(i)})\log\big(1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)$$
  • If $y^{(i)}=1$ we penalize very hard low probabilities.
  • If $y^{(i)}=0$ we penalize very hard high probabilities.

For a small change in $p$ near the wrong end there is a large change in the loss: confident mistakes cost a fortune.

Logistic Regression – Cost function.

$$L\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)}),y^{(i)}\big)=\begin{cases}-\log\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)&\text{if }y^{(i)}=1\\-\log\big(1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)&\text{if }y^{(i)}=0\end{cases}$$

or in 1 line, $\ L=-y^{(i)}\log(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)}))-(1-y^{(i)})\log(1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)}))$.

And the cost function ends up being

$$J(\bth)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^mL\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)}),y^{(i)}\big)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\Big(-y^{(i)}\log\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)-(1-y^{(i)})\log\big(1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)\Big)$$

Logistic Regression – Probabilistic interpretation

What we want to show. As in linear regression (there, Gaussian noise gave least squares), maximum likelihood explains our cost. Here $y$ is 0 or 1, so the noise is a coin flip with $P(y=1)=h_{\bth}(\bx)$, and the same recipe gives back the cross-entropy cost.

Another fit by maximum likelihood. Assume:

$$P(y^{(i)}=1\mid\bx^{(i)};\bth)=h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})$$ $$P(y^{(i)}=0\mid\bx^{(i)};\bth)=1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})$$

That can be compacted to (plug in $y=1$ or $y=0$ to check):

$$p(y^{(i)}\mid\bx^{(i)};\bth)=h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})^{y^{(i)}}\cdot\big(1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)^{1-y^{(i)}}$$

With $m$ i.i.d. training samples, the likelihood of the $\theta$'s is

$$L(\bth)=p(\by\mid\bX;\bth)=\prod_{i=1}^mh_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})^{y^{(i)}}\big(1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)^{1-y^{(i)}}$$

Once again, it is easier to maximize the log likelihood:

$$\ell(\bth)=\log L(\bth)=\sum_{i=1}^m\log p(y^{(i)}\mid\bx^{(i)};\bth)$$ $$=\sum_{i=1}^m\Big[y^{(i)}\log h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})+(1-y^{(i)})\log\big(1-h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})\big)\Big]$$

And maximizing $\ell$ is equivalent to minimizing its negative:

$$J(\bth)=-\frac1m\,\ell(\bth)$$

Same recipe as linear regression: minimising the cost is maximum likelihood. Only the noise model changed, Bernoulli instead of Gaussian.

Logistic Regression – Optimization.

We minimize $J$ with gradient descent. Shorthand for this slide: $h^{(i)}=h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})=g(z^{(i)})$ with $z^{(i)}=\bth^\top\bx^{(i)}$.

$$\frac{\partial J(\bth)}{\partial\theta_j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\,\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\Big(-y^{(i)}\log h^{(i)}-(1-y^{(i)})\log\big(1-h^{(i)}\big)\Big)$$
$$=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\Big(-\frac{y^{(i)}}{h^{(i)}}+\frac{1-y^{(i)}}{1-h^{(i)}}\Big)\cdot\hl{\frac{\partial h^{(i)}}{\partial\theta_j}}$$

$(\log u)'=u'/u$; the minus sign of $\log(1-h)$ flips the second sign

$$=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\frac{h^{(i)}-y^{(i)}}{h^{(i)}\big(1-h^{(i)}\big)}\cdot\hl{h^{(i)}\big(1-h^{(i)}\big)}\cdot\ho{\frac{\partial z^{(i)}}{\partial\theta_j}}$$

put the two fractions over a common denominator; chain rule $\partial h/\partial\theta_j=g'(z)\,\partial z/\partial\theta_j$ with $g'=g(1-g)$

$$=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\big(h^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\big)\cdot\ho{x_j^{(i)}}$$

the blue factors cancel; $z=\sum_k\theta_kx_k$ so $\partial z/\partial\theta_j=x_j$

HINT
$$g'(z)=\frac{d}{dz}\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}=g(z)\,(1-g(z))$$

The blue factor $h(1-h)$ cancels the denominator exactly. That cancellation is why cross-entropy and the sigmoid work so well together.

Same shape as linear regression: (prediction − target) × input.

Is $J$ convex, so that gradient descent finds the minimum? Yes. Proof in the next two slides.

Logistic Regression – Convexity, step by step (1/2)

What we must show. $J$ is convex if its Hessian $\mathbf H$ (the matrix of second derivatives, $H_{jk}=\partial^2J/\partial\theta_j\partial\theta_k$) satisfies $\bv^\top\mathbf H\bv\ge0$ for every vector $\bv$. Same test as for linear regression, where $\mathbf H=2\bX^\top\bX$.

Step 1 · the gradient (previous slide), one component:

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_j}=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\big(h^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\big)\,x_j^{(i)}$$

Step 2 · differentiate again, now with respect to $\theta_k$. Only $h^{(i)}$ depends on $\bth$; $y^{(i)}$ and $x_j^{(i)}$ are data:

$$H_{jk}=\frac{\partial^2J}{\partial\theta_j\,\partial\theta_k}=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\hl{\frac{\partial h^{(i)}}{\partial\theta_k}}\,x_j^{(i)}$$

Step 3 · chain rule with $h=g(z)$ and $g'=g(1-g)$:

$$\hl{\frac{\partial h^{(i)}}{\partial\theta_k}}=g'(z^{(i)})\,\frac{\partial z^{(i)}}{\partial\theta_k}=h^{(i)}\big(1-h^{(i)}\big)\,x_k^{(i)}$$

Step 4 · put it together. Write $s_i=h^{(i)}(1-h^{(i)})$:

$$H_{jk}=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^ms_i\,x_j^{(i)}x_k^{(i)}$$

All the entries at once, as a matrix (compare with $\frac1m\sum_i\bx^{(i)}\bx^{(i)\top}$ in linear regression):

$$\mathbf H=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^ms_i\,\bx^{(i)}\bx^{(i)\top}=\frac1m\bX^\top\mathbf S\bX$$

with $\mathbf S=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_m)$. Unlike linear regression, $\mathbf H$ depends on $\bth$ (through the $s_i$), so there is no closed form. But we only need its sign.

Logistic Regression – Convexity, step by step (2/2)

Step 5 · test with an arbitrary vector $\bv$. Multiply on both sides:

$$\bv^\top\mathbf H\bv=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^ms_i\,\bv^\top\bx^{(i)}\bx^{(i)\top}\bv=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^ms_i\,\big(\bv^\top\bx^{(i)}\big)^2$$

$\bv^\top\bx^{(i)}$ is a number, and $\bx^{(i)\top}\bv$ is the same number, so their product is a square.

Step 6 · every term is $\ge0$.

  • $s_i=h^{(i)}(1-h^{(i)})\gt0$, because the sigmoid gives $0\lt h^{(i)}\lt1$ (sigmoid property 1).
  • $(\bv^\top\bx^{(i)})^2\ge0$, because it is a square.
  • $\frac1m\gt0$. A sum of non-negative terms is non-negative.
$$\bv^\top\mathbf H\bv\ge0\ \text{ for every }\bv$$ $$\Rightarrow\ \mathbf H\text{ positive semi-definite}\ \Rightarrow\ J\text{ is convex}\quad\blacksquare$$

What we gain.

  • One bowl: every local minimum is the global minimum.
  • Gradient descent (small enough $\alpha$) and Newton's method both reach it, from any starting point.
  • Strictly convex ($\bv^\top\mathbf H\bv\gt0$ for $\bv\ne\bzero$) if the columns of $\bX$ are independent: then the minimum is unique.

Why MSE fails here. With $J_{\text{MSE}}=\frac1{2m}\sum(h^{(i)}-y^{(i)})^2$ the second derivative contains $(h^{(i)}-y^{(i)})\,g''(z^{(i)})$, which changes sign. Plateaus and local minima appear (the flat MSE curve on the cost-function slide).

One caveat: convex does not mean the minimum is reached. With separable data $J$ keeps decreasing forever (next slides).

Logistic Regression – Gradient descent.

Repeat until convergence (and simultaneously among all parameters, i.e. for $j\in\{0,\dots,n\}$):

$$\theta_j:=\theta_j-\alpha\,\frac{\partial J(\bth)}{\partial\theta_j}=\theta_j-\alpha\,\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\Big(\hl{h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})}-y^{(i)}\Big)\cdot x_j^{(i)}$$

Very similar to linear regression! (with its own non-linear hypothesis)

Vectorized:

$$\bth:=\bth-\alpha\,\frac1m\bX^\top\big(g(\bX\bth)-\by\big)$$

No closed form this time: the unknown is inside the sigmoid. We iterate (GD, or Newton / L-BFGS in scikit-learn).

Logistic Regression – Separable data

If a line separates the classes perfectly, scaling $\bth\to c\,\bth$ with $c\to\infty$ pushes every $h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})$ to the correct 0 or 1.

  • $J\to0$ but never reaches it: the maximum likelihood solution does not exist.
  • Gradient descent keeps increasing $\norm{\bth}$ forever: probabilities become over-confident.
  • Fix: L2 regularization, $J+\frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_{j\ge1}\theta_j^2$. scikit-learn does this by default with $C=1/\lambda=1$ (chapter 05).

$g(c\,z)$ for $c=1,3,10$: the same boundary, ever more confident.

Logistic Regression – A nice property of the optimum

At the minimum every partial derivative of $J$ is zero. Take the first one, for the intercept $\theta_0$, whose input is $x_0^{(i)}=1$ (slide on the gradient):

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_0}=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\big(h^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\big)\cdot1=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\sum_{i=1}^mh^{(i)}=\sum_{i=1}^my^{(i)}$$

The predicted probabilities add up to the number of positives. Divide by $m$: the average prediction equals the observed positive rate. If 30 % of the training cases are positive, the model predicts 30 % on average.

In terms of errors, $\sum_{y=0}h=\sum_{y=1}(1-h)$: the over-predictions on the negatives balance the under-predictions on the positives. For the plain fit the same holds for every feature: $\sum_i\big(h^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\big)x_j^{(i)}=0$.

True on the training data, for the plain fit or a penalty that skips $\theta_0$ (our $j\ge1$). Class weights or resampling (SMOTE) break it on purpose: see calibration later.

50 claims are fraudulent (5 %). The detector flags 60 claims, 40 of which are really fraud.

Accuracy $=\frac{40+930}{1000}=$ 0.97. Looks great.

Precision $=\frac{40}{60}=$ 0.67: a third of the investigated claims are false alarms.

Recall $=\frac{40}{50}=$ 0.80: one fraud in five gets through.

$F_1=\frac{2\cdot40}{80+20+10}=$ 0.73.

The trivial model "nothing is fraud" has accuracy 0.95, recall 0 and undefined precision. With imbalanced classes, accuracy mostly reflects the majority class.

Logistic Regression – Prec. vs Recall tradeoff.

By varying the threshold, you can either improve precision or sensitivity/recall – but not both.

So one of the two alone can be gamed: flag everything and recall is 1; flag only the surest case and precision is 1. Report both, or combine them:

$$F_1=\frac{2}{\frac{1}{\text{Recall}}+\frac{1}{\text{Precision}}}$$
  • Widely used, "good" for comparing.
  • Good balance even on imbalanced classification problems.
  • A low $F_1$ doesn't say which cases it failed (poor information if alone).
  • The PR curve baseline is the positive rate, not a diagonal. Summarised by average precision (AP).

Receiver operating characteristic curve: TPR against FPR, computed for many different thresholds on a single graph.

  • The diagonal is a model with random scores, whatever the class balance.
  • Good for comparing different classifiers: set separation to 0 and the curve collapses to the diagonal (AUC ≈ 0.5).
  • With very rare positives it can look too optimistic (FPR is divided by a huge number of negatives): look at the PR curve too.
  • Good to answer: what TPR can I obtain if I want to fix FPR to 0.02?

1 metric → AUC-ROC, the area under the curve, between 0.5 (random) and 1 (perfect).

Metrics – What AUC means, in one sentence

pairs drawn–
fraud scored higher–
fraction–
AUC (all 98 pairs)–

Take one fraud claim and one legit claim at random. AUC is the probability that the model gives the fraud the higher score.

  • AUC = 1: every fraud scores above every legit claim.
  • AUC = 0.5: a coin toss. The scores carry no information.
  • AUC = 0.8: "8 times out of 10 the fraud comes out on top".

No threshold is involved: AUC grades the ordering of the scores. That is why it is good for comparing models, and why it says nothing about what happens at the one threshold you will actually use.

Metrics – Choosing the threshold, simply

The model gives every claim a score. The threshold is where you cut. Three ways to decide, all on the validation set:

1 · I have a budget

"We can investigate 60 claims a week." Sort the claims by score and flag the top 60. The threshold is simply the 60th score.

The threshold follows the budget, not the other way round.

2 · I have a requirement

"We must catch 90 % of the fraud." Lower the threshold until recall reaches 0.9, then stop.

"No more than 2 % of legit claims may be bothered." Raise it until FPR is 0.02.

Read the point off the PR or ROC curve.

3 · I know the costs

A missed fraud costs 900 €, a useless investigation 100 €. Flag whenever the expected cost of flagging is lower. Derived on the next slide:

$$\tau^*=\frac{100}{100+900}=0.1$$

0.5 is only the right cut when both mistakes cost the same and the classes are balanced. With rare positives and expensive misses, the right cut is usually far below 0.5. Never tune it on the test set.

Metrics – The threshold from costs, slowly

One claim. The model says: probability of fraud $=p$. Two possible actions.

Flag it. With probability $1-p$ it was legit and the investigation was wasted:

$$\E[\text{cost of flagging}]=(1-p)\,c_{FP}$$

Let it through. With probability $p$ it was fraud and we pay for it:

$$\E[\text{cost of not flagging}]=p\,c_{FN}$$

Flag when flagging is the cheaper option:

$$(1-p)\,c_{FP}\lt p\,c_{FN}$$

Expand and collect the $p$ terms on one side:

$$c_{FP}-p\,c_{FP}\lt p\,c_{FN}\ \Rightarrow\ c_{FP}\lt p\,(c_{FP}+c_{FN})$$
$$p\gt\frac{c_{FP}}{c_{FP}+c_{FN}}=\tau^*$$

Class imbalances.

Appear in many domains, such as fraud detection, spam filtering, … Problem! High accuracy just by predicting the majority class.

Solutions:

  • Resampling techniques:
    • Undersampling: reduces the amount of data.
    • Oversampling: can cause overfitting (if exact copies).
  • Improvements: Tomek links and SMOTE.
  • Class weights in the loss (class_weight="balanced").
  • Use precision, recall, F1, PR curves instead of accuracy.
  • Resample only the training set. Validation and test keep the real class proportions.

Metrics – Probabilities and calibration

Often we use the probability itself (pricing, risk scores, expected cost).

  • Log-loss ($=J$ on the test set) and Brier score $\frac1m\sum_i(p^{(i)}-y^{(i)})^2$: lower is better.
  • Calibration: among claims with predicted $p\approx0.3$, about 30 % should be fraud. The reliability diagram should follow the diagonal.
  • Logistic regression tends to be well calibrated; SVMs and trees often are not. AUC does not see calibration.

Metrics – Scoring probabilities, by hand

Four claims, the model's probability of fraud $p$, and what really happened:

claim$y$$p$log-loss termBrier term
A10.90$-\ln0.90=0.11$$(0.90-1)^2=0.01$
B10.60$-\ln0.60=0.51$$(0.60-1)^2=0.16$
C00.20$-\ln0.80=0.22$$(0.20-0)^2=0.04$
D00.95$-\ln0.05=3.00$$(0.95-0)^2=0.90$

For $y=1$ the log-loss term is $-\ln p$; for $y=0$ it is $-\ln(1-p)$. The Brier term is always $(p-y)^2$.

$$\text{log-loss}=\tfrac{0.11+0.51+0.22+3.00}{4}=0.96\qquad\text{Brier}=\tfrac{0.01+0.16+0.04+0.90}{4}=0.28$$

One confident mistake (D: 95 % sure of fraud, but legit) costs 3.00, more than the other three claims together. Log-loss punishes confident mistakes very hard; a Brier term can never exceed 1.

Baseline "always say 0.5": log-loss $=\ln2=0.69$, Brier $=0.25$. Because of claim D, this model is worse than a coin flip on both.

Accuracy with a 0.5 threshold: 3 of 4 right (75 %). It cannot see how sure the model was. Use log-loss or Brier when the probability itself is used (prices, reserves, expected costs).

One-vs-all (one-vs-rest):

If we have K classes, repeat for each class:

  • The class label is set to 1 and the rest to 0.
  • Learn a binary classifier.

Run all classifiers and select the one with the highest $h_{\bth}(\bx)$.

Linear and logistic regression – Summary

  • Pros:
    • Simple model and interpretability.
    • Computationally efficient.
  • Cons:
    • Overly-simplistic (linearity assumptions).
    • Affected by outliers.
    • Correlated features (multicollinearity) make the coefficients unstable and hard to interpret; the predictions can still be fine.
      • Solution: drop or combine features, dimensionality reduction (like PCA), or regularization.
    • Linear regression only: its standard errors assume homoscedasticity (constant variance around the line).
