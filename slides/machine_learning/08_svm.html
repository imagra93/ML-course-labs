SVM: motivation

Many lines separate the data perfectly. Which one would you pick? A, B, C or D?

The SVM chooses the one that stays as far as possible from the closest points of both classes: the maximum margin.

SVM: hyperplanes

In $\R^n$ a hyperplane is an $(n-1)$-dimensional "flat" that cuts the space in two halves: a line in 2-D, a plane in 3-D, and so on.

SVM: the hyperplane and the decision rule

Input $\bx\in\R^n$. Parameters: a weight vector $\mathbf w\in\R^n$ and a bias $b\in\R$. Labels are $y\in\{-1,+1\}$ (not 0/1: it makes the formulas symmetric).

$$f(\bx)=\mathbf w\cdot\bx+b=w_1x_1+\dots+w_nx_n+b$$

The hyperplane is the set of points with $f(\bx)=0$. In 2-D, $w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b=0$: one equation, two unknowns, so one coordinate stays free and the solutions form a line.

$$\text{decision rule:}\quad\hat y=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if }f(\bx)\gt0\\-1&\text{if }f(\bx)\lt0\end{cases}$$

Example: $\mathbf w=(1,1)$, $b=-3$, the line $x_1+x_2-3=0$:

x⁽¹⁾ = (3, 3)+3+1
x⁽²⁾ = (0, 0)−3−1

SVM: the dot product, what it means

45°
w · x–
cos α–
what it says–

The dot product of two vectors: multiply the matching coordinates and add.

$$\mathbf w\cdot\bx=w_1x_1+w_2x_2+\dots+w_nx_n$$

Example: $\mathbf w=(2,1)$ and $\bx=(1,3)$ give $\ \mathbf w\cdot\bx=2\cdot1+1\cdot3=5$.

It is one number that says how much two vectors point the same way:

  • positive: roughly the same direction. $(2,1)\cdot(2,1)=5$.
  • zero: perpendicular. $(2,1)\cdot(-1,2)=-2+2=0$.
  • negative: roughly opposite directions. $(2,1)\cdot(-2,-1)=-5$.

In one formula, with $\alpha$ the angle between the vectors and $\norm{\mathbf w}=\sqrt{w_1^2+\dots+w_n^2}$ the length of $\mathbf w$: $\ \mathbf w\cdot\bx=\norm{\mathbf w}\,\norm{\bx}\cos\alpha$. In the figure both lengths are $\sqrt5$, so $\mathbf w\cdot\bx=5\cos\alpha$.

SVM: what b does

Keep $\mathbf w=(1,1)$ fixed and move only $b$. Three points, $f(\bx)=\mathbf w\cdot\bx+b$:

$\mathbf w\cdot\bx$ does not depend on $b$: it is how far each point lies along $\mathbf w$ (previous slide). $b$ adds the same number to all three, so on the number line under the plot they slide together.

A point changes class when its $f$ crosses 0, that is, when the line passes over it: A at $b=-1$, B at $b=-2.5$, C at $b=-4.5$.

The line $x_1+x_2+b=0$ cuts the axes at $(-b,0)$ and $(0,-b)$: changing $b$ moves it parallel to itself, it never turns.

$\mathbf w$ sets the direction of the boundary (perpendicular to $\mathbf w$); $b$ sets where it sits.

SVM: the dot product is a shadow

Shine a light perpendicular to $\mathbf w$: the vector $\bx$ casts a shadow on the line of $\mathbf w$ (orange).

Its length is $\norm{\bx}\cos\alpha$ (the side of the right triangle next to the angle). Compare with $\mathbf w\cdot\bx=\norm{\mathbf w}\,\norm{\bx}\cos\alpha$:

$$\mathbf w\cdot\bx=\norm{\mathbf w}\times\text{shadow}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{shadow}=\frac{\mathbf w\cdot\bx}{\norm{\mathbf w}}$$

Check: $\mathbf w=(2,1)$ and $\bx=(0.6,2.6)$. Then $\mathbf w\cdot\bx=2\cdot0.6+1\cdot2.6=3.8$ and $\norm{\mathbf w}=\sqrt5=2.24$, so the shadow is $\frac{3.8}{2.24}=1.70$, as in the figure.

If $\bx$ points away from $\mathbf w$ the dot product is negative: the shadow falls on the other side of the origin.

Dividing by $\norm{\mathbf w}$ turns the score $\mathbf w\cdot\bx$ into a length. Next: the distance to a hyperplane.

SVM: the hyperplane is a wall of equal shadows

The hyperplane is where $f(\bx)=\mathbf w\cdot\bx+b=0$, that is, $\mathbf w\cdot\bx=-b$. Dividing by $\norm{\mathbf w}$, every point of the hyperplane has the same shadow:

$$\text{shadow}(\bx)=\frac{\mathbf w\cdot\bx}{\norm{\mathbf w}}=\frac{-b}{\norm{\mathbf w}}$$

1 · Points A and B of the figure project to the same spot on the axis of $\mathbf w$. So the hyperplane is a straight wall perpendicular to $\mathbf w$.

2 · The distance from $\bx$ to the wall is the difference of two shadows, the one of $\bx$ and the one of the wall:

$$r(\bx)=\frac{\mathbf w\cdot\bx}{\norm{\mathbf w}}-\frac{-b}{\norm{\mathbf w}}=\frac{\mathbf w\cdot\bx+b}{\norm{\mathbf w}}=\frac{f(\bx)}{\norm{\mathbf w}}$$

Check: $\mathbf w=(1,1)$, $b=-3$, $\bx=(4,2)$. $f=4+2-3=3$, so $r=\frac{3}{\sqrt2}=2.12$. The closest point of the line is $(2.5,0.5)$, at distance $\sqrt{1.5^2+1.5^2}=2.12$ ✓. The sign of $r$ tells the side.

SVM: fixing the scale

The length of $\mathbf w$ is free. $\mathbf w=(1,1),b=-3$ and $\mathbf w=(2,2),b=-6$ give the same line $x_1+x_2=3$: only the values of $f$ double. The distances $r=f/\norm{\mathbf w}$ do not change.

We spend that freedom: choose the scale so that the closest points of each class have $f=+1$ and $f=-1$. Then every training point satisfies

$$\begin{cases}\mathbf w\cdot\bx^{(i)}+b\ge+1&\text{if }y^{(i)}=+1\\\mathbf w\cdot\bx^{(i)}+b\le-1&\text{if }y^{(i)}=-1\end{cases}\iff\ y^{(i)}\big(\mathbf w\cdot\bx^{(i)}+b\big)\ge1$$

Multiplying by $y^{(i)}=\pm1$ merges both cases into one line (for $y^{(i)}=-1$ it flips the $\le$ into $\ge$).

Equality holds exactly for the closest points, the support vectors (circled):

$$y^{(i)}\big(\mathbf w\cdot\bx^{(i)}+b\big)=1$$

No training point lies strictly between the dashed lines $f=\pm1$. That empty band is the margin.

SVM: measuring the margin

Take a support vector on each side: $\bx_+$ with $f=+1$ and $\bx_-$ with $f=-1$. The margin is the difference of their shadows on $\mathbf w$:

$$d=\frac{\mathbf w\cdot\bx_+}{\norm{\mathbf w}}-\frac{\mathbf w\cdot\bx_-}{\norm{\mathbf w}}=\frac{\mathbf w\cdot\bx_+-\mathbf w\cdot\bx_-}{\norm{\mathbf w}}$$

From the support-vector equations, $\mathbf w\cdot\bx_++b=1$ and $\mathbf w\cdot\bx_-+b=-1$:

$$\mathbf w\cdot\bx_+=1-b,\qquad\mathbf w\cdot\bx_-=-1-b$$
$$d=\frac{(1-b)-(-1-b)}{\norm{\mathbf w}}=\frac{1-b+1+b}{\norm{\mathbf w}}$$
$$d=\frac{2}{\norm{\mathbf w}}$$

Check on the figure: $\mathbf w=(1,1)$, $b=-3$, $\bx_+=(3,1)$, $\bx_-=(1,1)$: $d=\frac{4-2}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2=\frac{2}{\sqrt2}$ ✓

$b$ cancels. Once the scale is fixed, a short $\mathbf w$ means a wide margin.

SVM: the optimization problem

20°
margin in this direction–

The widest margin that keeps every point on its side, outside the band:

$$\max_{\mathbf w,b}\ \frac{2}{\norm{\mathbf w}}\quad\text{s.t. }\ y^{(i)}\big(\mathbf w\cdot\bx^{(i)}+b\big)\ge1\ \ \forall i$$

Maximizing $2/a$ (for $a\gt0$) is minimizing $a$, which is minimizing $\frac12a^2$: no square root, and the $\frac12$ cancels the 2 of the derivative.

$$\min_{\mathbf w,b}\ \frac12\norm{\mathbf w}^2\quad\text{s.t. }\ y^{(i)}\big(\mathbf w\cdot\bx^{(i)}+b\big)\ge1\ \ \forall i$$

A convex quadratic program: one global optimum, and standard QP solvers find it. Only the constraints of the closest points end up mattering.

Next: if we knew in advance which points are the closest, the problem would be easy.

But we almost never know them: which points touch the margin is part of the answer. A wrong guess, $(3.5,2)$ instead of $(3,1)$, breaks a constraint: $(3,1)$ ends up with $f=0.14\lt1$. We need a method that finds the support vectors by itself: Lagrange multipliers, reviewed next.

Review: Lagrange multipliers with an equality

0.80
f(w)–
angle between ∇f and ∇g–

What for. Minimize $f(\mathbf w)$ subject to $g(\mathbf w)=0$: the lowest point on a curve, not the lowest point overall.

The idea. At the best point, sliding along the curve cannot lower $f$. So $\nabla f$ has no part along the curve: it is perpendicular to it, like $\nabla g$. Two arrows perpendicular to the same curve are parallel:

$$\nabla f(\mathbf w^*)=\lambda\,\nabla g(\mathbf w^*)$$

$\lambda$, the multiplier, is just a number: how many times $\nabla g$ fits in $\nabla f$ (4 in the figure). It can have any sign.

The Lagrangian puts this condition and the constraint into one function:

$$\mathcal L(\mathbf w,\lambda)=f(\mathbf w)-\lambda\,g(\mathbf w)$$

$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mathbf w}=\nabla f-\lambda\nabla g=\bzero$ is the condition above, and $\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\lambda}=-g=0$ is the constraint.

The general solution:

  1. write $\mathcal L$;
  2. set $\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mathbf w}=\bzero$: $\mathbf w$ as a function of $\lambda$;
  3. put that $\mathbf w$ into $g(\mathbf w)=0$: it gives $\lambda$, then $\mathbf w^*$.

Several constraints $g_k(\mathbf w)=0$: one multiplier each, $\mathcal L=f-\sum_k\lambda_kg_k$. Next: the recipe on an example.

Lagrange multipliers: an equality, an example

The recipe on an example: minimize $f=\frac12(w_1^2+w_2^2)$ subject to $g=w_1+w_2-1=0$.

The picture. The blue rings join the points with the same $f$. The green line is where the constraint holds. The best allowed point is where a ring just touches the line, and there $\nabla f$ and $\nabla g$ point the same way: $\ \nabla f=\lambda\,\nabla g$.

1 · The Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal L(\mathbf w,\lambda)=\tfrac12(w_1^2+w_2^2)-\lambda\,(w_1+w_2-1)$$

2 · $\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_1}=w_1-\lambda=0\ $ and $\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_2}=w_2-\lambda=0$, so $w_1=w_2=\lambda$. 3 · The constraint $w_1+w_2=1$ then gives $2\lambda=1$:

$$\lambda=\tfrac12,\qquad\mathbf w^*=\big(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\big)$$

In the picture, $\nabla f=\mathbf w^*=(\frac12,\frac12)$ and $\nabla g=(1,1)$: $\ \nabla f=\frac12\nabla g$ ✓.

Review: Lagrange multipliers with an inequality

α–
g(w*)–
α · g(w*)–

What for. Minimize $f(\mathbf w)$ subject to $g(\mathbf w)\ge0$: the lowest point in a region, like the SVM's.

Two cases.

  • The free minimum is allowed: it is the answer, the constraint does nothing. Inactive, $\alpha=0$.
  • It is not allowed: the answer is on the edge, $g(\mathbf w^*)=0$, and we are back to the equality, $\nabla f=\alpha\,\nabla g$. Active, $\alpha\gt0$.

Why $\alpha\gt0$: $\nabla g$ points into the allowed region. If $\nabla f$ pointed the other way, a step inside would lower $f$.

One set of conditions for both, with $\mathcal L(\mathbf w,\alpha)=f(\mathbf w)-\alpha\,g(\mathbf w)$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mathbf w}=\bzero,\qquad g(\mathbf w^*)\ge0,\qquad\alpha\ge0,\qquad\alpha\,g(\mathbf w^*)=0$$

The last one, complementary slackness, says "one of the two cases": $\alpha=0$ or $g=0$.

The general solution, the route the SVM takes:

  1. write $\mathcal L$, with $\alpha\ge0$;
  2. minimize over $\mathbf w$: $\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mathbf w}=\bzero$ gives $\mathbf w$ as a function of $\alpha$;
  3. substitute: the dual, a function of $\alpha$ only;
  4. maximize it over $\alpha\ge0$.

Several constraints: one $\alpha_i\ge0$ each, each active or inactive on its own.

Lagrange multipliers: an inequality, in pictures

The two cases of the previous slide, in one dimension: minimize $\frac12w^2$, a bowl with its bottom at $w=0$.

Top: $w\ge1$. The orange zone is forbidden, and it holds the bottom of the bowl. The best allowed point is the nearest edge, $w^*=1$: the constraint is active, the solution is pressed against it.

Bottom: $w\ge-1$. The bottom of the bowl is allowed. $w^*=0$, as if the constraint did not exist: inactive.

Each constraint $g(w)\ge0$ gets a multiplier $\alpha\ge0$. Active: the constraint pushes, $\alpha\gt0$. Inactive: it does nothing, $\alpha=0$. In both cases $\ \alpha\cdot g(w^*)=0$. This is the rule that will pick the support vectors.

Lagrange multipliers: an inequality, the maths

Top problem: $\min_w\tfrac12w^2$ subject to $g(w)=w-1\ge0$.

1 · Lagrangian, with a multiplier $\alpha\ge0$:

$$\mathcal L(w,\alpha)=\tfrac12w^2-\alpha\,(w-1)$$

2 · Minimize over $w$ (derivative to zero):

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w}=w-\alpha=0\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ w=\alpha$$

3 · Substitute: the dual, a function of $\alpha$ only:

$$\tfrac12\alpha^2-\alpha(\alpha-1)=\alpha-\tfrac12\alpha^2$$

4 · Maximize over $\alpha\ge0$: the derivative $1-\alpha=0$ gives $\alpha=1$, so $w=\alpha=1$ ✓. Active constraint, $\alpha\gt0$.

Bottom problem ($w\ge-1$): the same steps give $w=\alpha$ and the dual $-\alpha-\frac12\alpha^2$. It only decreases for $\alpha\ge0$, so its maximum is at $\alpha=0$ and $w=0$ ✓. Inactive.

Both cases: $\ \alpha\cdot g(w^*)=0$, complementary slackness.

SVM: two known support vectors, with the Lagrangian

Two points, $\bx_1=(1,1)$ with $y_1=+1$ and $\bx_2=(-1,-1)$ with $y_2=-1$, both obviously on the margin: two equalities $y_i(\mathbf w\cdot\bx_i+b)=1$. Two equations cannot fix three unknowns ($w_1,w_2,b$), so we still minimize $\frac12\norm{\mathbf w}^2$: the Lagrangian of the review.

1 · Lagrangian, one multiplier per point:

$$\mathcal L=\tfrac12\norm{\mathbf w}^2-\alpha_1\big[y_1(\mathbf w\cdot\bx_1+b)-1\big]-\alpha_2\big[y_2(\mathbf w\cdot\bx_2+b)-1\big]$$

2 · $\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mathbf w}=\mathbf w-\alpha_1y_1\bx_1-\alpha_2y_2\bx_2=\bzero$. Since $y_1\bx_1=y_2\bx_2=(1,1)$, we get

$$\mathbf w=\alpha_1y_1\bx_1+\alpha_2y_2\bx_2=(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)\,(1,1)$$

3 · $\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial b}=-\alpha_1y_1-\alpha_2y_2=-\alpha_1+\alpha_2=0$, so $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha$ and $\mathbf w=(2\alpha,2\alpha)$.

4 · Use the two equalities: $\ \mathbf w\cdot\bx_1+b=4\alpha+b=1\ $ and $\ \mathbf w\cdot\bx_2+b=-4\alpha+b=-1$. Adding them: $b=0$, and then $\alpha=\frac14$.

$$\mathbf w=\big(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\big),\quad b=0,\quad\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\tfrac14$$

Margin $\frac{2}{\norm{\mathbf w}}=2\sqrt2=2.83$, the distance between the two points ✓. And $\mathbf w=\frac14\bx_1-\frac14\bx_2$ is built from the two support vectors.

SVM: in general we do not know the support vectors

1 · Lagrangian2 · minimize over $\mathbf w,b$3 · the dual4 · solve for $\boldsymbol\alpha$5 · $\mathbf w$, $b$, predict

With two points it was obvious. With 13 points, or 13,000, which ones end up on the margin? We cannot know before solving.

The fix: one multiplier $\alpha_i\ge0$ per point, and let the maths decide. For a problem with constraints $g_i(\mathbf w)\ge0$:

$$\mathcal L(\mathbf w,\boldsymbol\alpha)=f(\mathbf w)-\sum_i\alpha_i\,g_i(\mathbf w),\qquad\alpha_i\ge0$$

Why it works. Fix $\mathbf w$ and make $\mathcal L$ as large as possible over the $\alpha_i$:

  • a constraint is violated ($g_i\lt0$): then $-\alpha_ig_i$ grows without limit as $\alpha_i$ grows. An infinite penalty;
  • all constraints hold: the best is $\alpha_i=0$, and we get $f(\mathbf w)$ back.

So minimizing over $\mathbf w$ the maximum over $\boldsymbol\alpha$ is exactly the constrained problem. And at the optimum $\alpha_ig_i=0$: points off the margin get $\alpha_i=0$; the others are the support vectors.

SVM: the Lagrangian

1 · Lagrangian2 · minimize over $\mathbf w,b$3 · the dual4 · solve for $\boldsymbol\alpha$5 · $\mathbf w$, $b$, predict

For the SVM: $f=\frac12\norm{\mathbf w}^2$, and one constraint per training point, $g_i=y_i(\mathbf w\cdot\bx_i+b)-1\ge0$.

Shorthand from now on: $\bx_i,y_i$ is the $i$-th training example (so far $\bx^{(i)},y^{(i)}$), for $i=1,\dots,m$.

$$\mathcal L(\mathbf w,b,\boldsymbol\alpha)=\frac12\norm{\mathbf w}^2-\sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_i\big[y_i(\mathbf w\cdot\bx_i+b)-1\big]$$

The same expression as for the two points, with $m$ terms instead of 2. The multipliers must satisfy $\alpha_i\ge0$.

Expand it and group the terms:

$$\mathcal L=\frac12\,\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf w-\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\,\mathbf w\cdot\bx_i-b\sum_i\alpha_iy_i+\sum_i\alpha_i$$

The plan, as in 1-D:

  1. minimize over $\mathbf w$ and $b$: derivatives to zero;
  2. substitute the result back: a function of $\boldsymbol\alpha$ only, the dual;
  3. maximize it over $\alpha_i\ge0$.

For convex problems like this one, minimizing first and maximizing afterwards gives the same answer as the constrained problem.

SVM: minimizing over w and b

1 · Lagrangian2 · minimize over $\mathbf w,b$3 · the dual4 · solve for $\boldsymbol\alpha$5 · $\mathbf w$, $b$, predict

The expanded Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal L=\frac12\,\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf w-\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\,\mathbf w\cdot\bx_i-b\sum_i\alpha_iy_i+\sum_i\alpha_i$$
HINT
$\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf w}\big(\tfrac12\,\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf w\big)=\mathbf w$   and   $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf w}\big(\mathbf w\cdot\bx_i\big)=\bx_i$, the vector versions of $\frac{d}{dw}\frac12w^2=w$ and $\frac{d}{dw}wx=x$.

With respect to $\mathbf w$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mathbf w}=\mathbf w-\sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_iy_i\bx_i=\bzero$$
$$\mathbf w=\sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_iy_i\bx_i$$

The optimal $\mathbf w$ is a weighted sum of the training points: the formula we found for the two points.

With respect to $b$ (only the third term contains $b$):

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial b}=-\sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_iy_i=0$$
$$\sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_iy_i=0$$

The multipliers of the two classes balance: $\displaystyle\sum_{y_i=+1}\alpha_i=\sum_{y_i=-1}\alpha_i$.

SVM: the dual problem

1 · Lagrangian2 · minimize over $\mathbf w,b$3 · the dual4 · solve for $\boldsymbol\alpha$5 · $\mathbf w$, $b$, predict

Substitute both results into the Lagrangian. The $b$ term is $b\cdot0=0$, and the two $\mathbf w$ terms become the same double sum:

$$\tfrac12\,\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf w=\tfrac12\Big(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\bx_i\Big)\cdot\Big(\sum_j\alpha_jy_j\bx_j\Big)=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,\bx_i\cdot\bx_j$$
$$\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\,\mathbf w\cdot\bx_i=\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\Big(\sum_j\alpha_jy_j\bx_j\Big)\cdot\bx_i=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,\bx_i\cdot\bx_j$$

Half of the double sum, minus all of it, leaves minus half. The dual problem:

$$\max_{\boldsymbol\alpha}\ \sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^m\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,\hl{\bx_i\cdot\bx_j}\qquad\text{s.t. }\ \alpha_i\ge0,\ \ \sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_iy_i=0$$
  • A problem in $m$ unknowns, one $\alpha_i$ per training point, with simple constraints. Solvers (SMO) handle it well.
  • The data enter only through the dot products $\bx_i\cdot\bx_j$ (highlighted). More on this soon.

SVM: the same two points, through the dual

1 · Lagrangian2 · minimize over $\mathbf w,b$3 · the dual4 · solve for $\boldsymbol\alpha$5 · $\mathbf w$, $b$, predict

Same two points as before. The dual has one unknown per point, and we can solve it by hand.

1 · The constraint $\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=\alpha_1-\alpha_2=0$ gives $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha$.

2 · Dot products: $\bx_1\cdot\bx_1=2$, $\bx_2\cdot\bx_2=2$, $\bx_1\cdot\bx_2=-2$. The double sum has 4 terms:

$$\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,\bx_i\cdot\bx_j=\alpha^2\big(2+2+2\cdot(-1)(-2)\big)=8\alpha^2$$

3 · Dual objective: $2\alpha-\frac12\cdot8\alpha^2=2\alpha-4\alpha^2$. Its derivative $2-8\alpha=0$ gives $\alpha=\frac14$.

$$\mathbf w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\bx_i=\tfrac14(1,1)-\tfrac14(-1,-1)=\big(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\big)$$

4 · $b$ from a support vector: $b=y_1-\mathbf w\cdot\bx_1=1-\big(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\big)=0$.

Same answer as with the Lagrangian directly. Boundary $\frac12x_1+\frac12x_2=0$, margin $\frac{2}{\norm{\mathbf w}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1/2}}=2\sqrt2=2.83$, exactly the distance between the two points ✓. Both are support vectors ($\alpha\gt0$).

SVM: support vectors and prediction

1 · Lagrangian2 · minimize over $\mathbf w,b$3 · the dual4 · solve for $\boldsymbol\alpha$5 · $\mathbf w$, $b$, predict

Complementary slackness (KKT), for every training point:

$$\alpha_i\big[y_i(\mathbf w\cdot\bx_i+b)-1\big]=0$$
  • A point outside the margin has a bracket $\gt0$, so $\alpha_i=0$.
  • If $\alpha_i\gt0$ the bracket must be $0$: the point is on the margin. These are the support vectors (a point on the margin can still have $\alpha_i=0$).

So $\mathbf w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\bx_i$ is built from the support vectors alone. Delete any other point and nothing changes.

The bias, from any support vector $s$: $y_s(\mathbf w\cdot\bx_s+b)=1\Rightarrow b=y_s-\mathbf w\cdot\bx_s$ (because $1/y_s=y_s$ when $y_s=\pm1$).

Prediction for a new $\bx$: substitute $\mathbf w$ into $f(\bx)=\mathbf w\cdot\bx+b$:

$$f(\bx)=\sum_{i\in\text{SV}}\alpha_iy_i\,\hl{\bx_i\cdot\bx}+b,\qquad\hat y=\operatorname{sign}f(\bx)$$

Again only dot products, now between the new point and the support vectors.

In the two-point example: $f(\bx)=\frac14\,\bx_1\cdot\bx-\frac14\,\bx_2\cdot\bx+0=\frac12x_1+\frac12x_2$ ✓

SVM: the data only enter through inner products

Training: the dual problem.

$$\max_{\boldsymbol\alpha}\ \sum_i\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,\hl{\bx_i\cdot\bx_j}$$ $$\text{s.t. }\ \alpha_i\ge0,\ \ \sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0$$

Prediction for a new point $\bx$:

$$f(\bx)=\sum_{i\in\text{SV}}\alpha_iy_i\,\hl{\bx_i\cdot\bx}+b$$

Look at where the data appear: only inside the highlighted inner products (dot products) of pairs of points.

Not the coordinates themselves, and not even how many features there are. Only the table of inner products between every pair of training points, the Gram matrix. For the two points of before:

$\bx_1$$\bx_2$
$\bx_1$$\bx_1\cdot\bx_1=2$$\bx_1\cdot\bx_2=-2$
$\bx_2$$\bx_2\cdot\bx_1=-2$$\bx_2\cdot\bx_2=2$

Give the algorithm that table and it can train and predict. If we replace the table by a different one, we get a different model, with no change to the algorithm. That is the door to kernels: the next topic.

SVM: kernels, the idea

Some data can't be separated by any hyperplane. Fix: map every point to a richer feature space, $\bx\mapsto\phi(\bx)$, where a hyperplane does separate them.

Simplest example, in 1-D: the blue class sits in the middle, red on both sides. No single threshold works.

Add the feature $x^2$: $\phi(x)=(x,x^2)$. In that plane the points lie on a parabola, and a horizontal line separates them.

Back on the original axis, that line is two thresholds: a hyperplane in $\phi$-space is a curved boundary in $\bx$-space.

SVM: kernels, a 2-D example

$$\phi(\bx)=\big(x_1,\ x_2,\ e^{-(x_1^2+x_2^2)}\big)\in\R^3$$
$$\phi(\bx)\cdot\phi(\mathbf z)=x_1z_1+x_2z_2+e^{-(x_1^2+x_2^2)}e^{-(z_1^2+z_2^2)}$$
  • The flat plane $x_3=0.72$ in $\R^3$ is, back in $\R^2$, the circle $x_1^2+x_2^2=-\ln0.72=0.33$ (radius 0.57, green on the left).
  • The dot product in $\R^3$ can be computed directly from the 2-D vectors. We never need to build $\phi(\bx)$ itself: that is the kernel trick.

SVM: the kernel trick

The dual and the prediction only use dot products $\bx_i\cdot\bx_j$. Replace them by a kernel:

$$K(\bx_i,\bx_j)=\phi(\bx_i)\cdot\phi(\bx_j)$$

We compute $K$, never $\phi(\bx)$: a linear SVM in the big space, at the cost of the small one.

Common kernels:

  • Linear: $K(\bx,\mathbf z)=\bx\cdot\mathbf z$
  • Polynomial: $K(\bx,\mathbf z)=(\bx\cdot\mathbf z+c)^d$
  • RBF (Gaussian): $K(\bx,\mathbf z)=\exp\big(-\gamma\norm{\bx-\mathbf z}^2\big)$, with $\gamma=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}$. Its $\phi$ is infinite-dimensional.

Why the polynomial kernel works (2-D, $c=1$, $d=2$):

$$(\bx\cdot\mathbf z+1)^2=(x_1z_1+x_2z_2+1)^2$$ $$=x_1^2z_1^2+x_2^2z_2^2+2x_1x_2z_1z_2+2x_1z_1+2x_2z_2+1$$
$$=\phi(\bx)\cdot\phi(\mathbf z),\qquad\phi(\bx)=\big(1,\sqrt2x_1,\sqrt2x_2,x_1^2,x_2^2,\sqrt2x_1x_2\big)$$

Check with numbers: $\bx=(1,2)$, $\mathbf z=(3,1)$. Kernel: $(3+2+1)^2=36$. Explicitly: $\phi(\bx)=(1,\sqrt2,2\sqrt2,1,4,2\sqrt2)$, $\phi(\mathbf z)=(1,3\sqrt2,\sqrt2,9,1,3\sqrt2)$, and $\phi(\bx)\cdot\phi(\mathbf z)=1+6+4+9+4+12=36$ ✓

For $n=100$ features and degree 3, $\phi$ would have $\binom{103}{3}=176{,}851$ coordinates. The kernel still costs one 100-D dot product.

SVM: the RBF kernel

The dual, with kernels:

$$\max_{\boldsymbol\alpha}\sum_i\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_i\sum_j\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,\hl{K(\bx_i,\bx_j)}$$

with the same constraints $\alpha_i\ge0$, $\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0$. The decision function for a new point:

$$f(\bx)=\sum_{i\in\text{SV}}\alpha_iy_i\,\hl{K(\bx_i,\bx)}+b$$

RBF: $K(\bx_i,\bx)=e^{-\gamma\norm{\bx-\bx_i}^2}$ is a bump centred on $\bx_i$. So $f(\bx)$ is a sum of Gaussian bumps, one per support vector, up for $+1$, down for $-1$, with height $\alpha_i$. $\gamma$ sets their width.

1.00

Here all $\alpha_i=1$ and $b=0$, to show the shape. Tune $C$ and $\gamma$ together by cross-validation on a log grid.

SVM: which functions are kernels? (Mercer)

A function $K$ is a valid kernel only if it really is a dot product $\phi(\bx)\cdot\phi(\mathbf z)$ in some feature space. Mercer's theorem gives a test on the kernel (Gram) matrix of any set of points $\bx_1,\dots,\bx_m$:

$$K_{ij}=K(\bx_i,\bx_j)$$

$K$ must be positive semi-definite: $\bv^\top K\bv\ge0$ for every vector $\bv$. This keeps the dual a concave maximization (a convex problem), with one global optimum.

Check for a real feature map: $\ \bv^\top K\bv=\sum_{i,j}v_iv_j\,\phi(\bx_i)\cdot\phi(\bx_j)=\Big(\sum_iv_i\phi(\bx_i)\Big)\cdot\Big(\sum_jv_j\phi(\bx_j)\Big)=\norm{\sum_iv_i\phi(\bx_i)}^2\ge0$.

Key takeaway: a valid kernel always produces a positive semi-definite kernel matrix. Linear, polynomial and RBF kernels all pass the test.

Real data overlap: no $\mathbf w$ satisfies every constraint. Allow each point a slack $\xi_i\ge0$, and pay for it:

$$\min_{\mathbf w,b,\boldsymbol\xi}\ \frac12\norm{\mathbf w}^2+\hl{C\sum_{i=1}^m\xi_i}\quad\text{s.t. }\ y_i(\mathbf w\cdot\bx_i+b)\ge1-\hl{\xi_i},\ \ \xi_i\ge0$$
  • $\xi_i=0$: on the correct side, outside or on the margin. $0\lt\xi_i\le1$: correct side, but inside the margin. $\xi_i\gt1$: misclassified.
  • $C$ is the regularization parameter: margin width against violations.
    • Large $C$: violations are expensive → narrow margin, fits the training data tightly. $C\to\infty$ is the hard margin.
    • Small $C$: wide margin, many violations tolerated.

SVM: the soft margin in scikit-learn

The $C$ of the previous slide is exactly the C hyperparameter of SVC. It minimizes the margin term plus $C$ times the total violation:

$$\frac12\norm{\mathbf w}^2+C\sum_i\xi_i$$
from sklearn.svm import SVC
model = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1.0)   # C = 1 is the default
model.fit(X_train, y_train)

SVM: review

Pros

  • Kernels give flexible, non-linear boundaries while training stays a convex problem: one global optimum.
  • Maximum margin: good results even with smaller datasets.
  • Sparse: the model only keeps the support vectors.

Cons

  • Kernel training is slow on big data: roughly $O(m^2)$ to $O(m^3)$. Beyond ~$10^5$ examples, use a linear SVM.
  • Prediction with kernels costs one $K$ per support vector.
  • Binary only: one-vs-rest or one-vs-one for more classes.
  • No probabilities out of the box (Platt scaling fits a sigmoid on top).
  • Scale the features: the margin is a Euclidean distance.
  • Hyperparameters $C$ and $\gamma$ must be tuned by cross-validation.

SVM: more than two classes

One-vs-rest: train $K$ binary SVMs, one per class, with $y=+1$ for class $k$ and $-1$ for the rest.

Winner takes all: predict the class with the highest score $f_k(\bx)=\mathbf w_k\cdot\bx+b_k$.

One-vs-one: one SVM for every pair of classes, $\frac{K(K-1)}{2}$ of them, each on less data; predict by majority vote.

scikit-learn: LinearSVC uses one-vs-rest; SVC uses one-vs-one.

Linear regression for time series

Same model $\hat y=\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf x$, same least squares. New data: days in order. Today looks a lot like yesterday, so the examples are not independent.

The model does not change, the method does:

  1. Never shuffle. Train on the past, test on the future.
  2. Features only from the past: yesterday $y_{t-1}$, last week $y_{t-7}$. A 7-day mean that includes today gives $R^2 = 1.00$: arithmetic, not forecasting.
  3. Beat a naive forecast (“same as last week”), not the mean. On the test year the lab's model halves its error (MAE 4.3 vs 8.5).
  4. Cross-validate forward with TimeSeriesSplit.
