Decision Trees: an example

A tree predicts by asking a sequence of yes/no questions, each about one feature, until it reaches a leaf that holds the prediction.

Press → to follow one car down the tree.

CART (Classification And Regression Trees, Breiman et al. 1984) is the standard algorithm to learn the questions from data. It is what scikit-learn implements.

A small tree is just a set of if / else rules: easy to explain to anyone.

Decision Trees: a tree = boxes in feature space

  • Root: all the training data. Internal node: one question $x_j\le s$ (feature $j$, threshold $s$). Leaf: no more questions.
  • Each question cuts a box in two with a line parallel to an axis. The leaves are the final boxes $R_1,\dots,R_L$.
$$h(\bx)=\sum_{\ell=1}^Lc_\ell\,\mathbb 1[\bx\in R_\ell]$$

"Find the box that contains $\bx$ and return its constant $c_\ell$." Classification: the class proportions of the box. Regression: the mean of its $y$.

Decision Trees: what a leaf predicts

A leaf predicts one constant $c$ for all its examples. The best $c$ depends on the loss:

loss on the leaf's examplesbest constant $c$
squared error $\sum_i(y^{(i)}-c)^2$the mean $\bar y$
absolute error $\sum_i|y^{(i)}-c|$the median
0–1 loss (number of mistakes)the majority class
log-lossthe class proportions $\hat p_k=N_k/N$

Examples. A regression leaf with $y=\{3,5,10\}$: mean $6$, median $5$. A classification leaf with 6 sold and 2 not sold: predicts "sold", with $\hat p=6/8=0.75$. That $\hat p$ is what predict_proba returns.

Proof for the mean. Set the derivative with respect to $c$ to zero:

$$\frac{d}{dc}\sum_{i=1}^N\big(y^{(i)}-c\big)^2=\sum_{i=1}^N2\big(y^{(i)}-c\big)\cdot(-1)=0$$

chain rule: the derivative of $(y-c)^2$ with respect to $c$ is $2(y-c)\cdot(-1)$

$$\sum_{i=1}^Ny^{(i)}-Nc=0\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ c=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Ny^{(i)}=\bar y$$

divide by $-2$, and $\sum_{i=1}^Nc=Nc$

Majority class. Predicting class $k$ is wrong for a fraction $1-p_k$ of the leaf: the fewest mistakes when $p_k$ is the largest.

No gradient descent in trees: the questions are found by a greedy search, then each leaf simply takes its mean or its majority.

Decision Trees: which question is better?

Our running example: 16 used cars, 8 sold within a month and 8 not. Two candidate first questions.

Both send most sold cars to "yes". But B produces a pure child: 4 cars, none sold. Nothing more to ask there.

To compare questions we need a number, the impurity of a node: 0 if all its examples have the same class, largest when the classes are evenly mixed.

A question is good if its children are, on average, much purer than the parent. Next: entropy and Gini.

Decision Trees: entropy

With $p_k$ = the fraction of class $k$ in the node ($K$ classes):

$$H=-\sum_{k=1}^Kp_k\log_2p_k=\sum_{k=1}^Kp_k\cdot\big(-\log_2p_k\big)$$
  • Pure node: one $p_k=1$ and $\log_21=0$, so $H=0$ (by convention $0\log_20=0$).
  • 50/50 with two classes: $H=\frac12\cdot1+\frac12\cdot1=1$ bit, the maximum.
  • Meaning: the average number of bits (yes/no answers) needed to tell the class of an example from the node.

The values of $-\log_2p$ we need today:

$p$1/21/43/41/32/3
$-\log_2p$120.4151.5850.585

The nodes of our example, written as (sold, not sold):

$$(8,8):\quad H=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac12\cdot1=1$$

the root: $p=\frac{8}{16}=\frac12$ for each class

$$(6,2):\quad H=\tfrac34\cdot0.415+\tfrac14\cdot2=0.311+0.5=0.811$$

$p_{\text{sold}}=\frac68=\frac34$, $p_{\text{not}}=\frac28=\frac14$; the same for $(2,6)$

$$(8,4):\quad H=\tfrac23\cdot0.585+\tfrac13\cdot1.585=0.390+0.528=0.918$$

$p_{\text{sold}}=\frac{8}{12}=\frac23$, $p_{\text{not}}=\frac{4}{12}=\frac13$

$$(0,4):\quad H=0\qquad\text{pure}$$

Decision Trees: information gain

The information gain of a question is the entropy before, minus the size-weighted entropy after:

$$IG=H(\text{parent})-\sum_{\text{children }c}\frac{N_c}{N}\,H(c)$$
  • $N_c/N$ = the fraction of the parent's examples that go to child $c$.
  • Weighting by size: a tiny pure child is not worth much.
  • CART always asks yes/no questions: two children. (ID3, an older algorithm, splits a categorical feature into one child per value.)

The tree asks the question with the largest gain.

Question A (price): children $(6,2)$ and $(2,6)$, 8 cars each.

$$IG_A=1-\Big(\tfrac{8}{16}\cdot0.811+\tfrac{8}{16}\cdot0.811\Big)=1-0.811=0.189$$

Question B (mileage): children $(8,4)$ with 12 cars and $(0,4)$ with 4.

$$IG_B=1-\Big(\tfrac{12}{16}\cdot0.918+\tfrac{4}{16}\cdot0\Big)=1-0.689=0.311$$
$IG_B=0.311\gt IG_A=0.189$: ask about the mileage first.

B wins even though its big child $(8,4)$ is more mixed than A's children (0.918 vs 0.811). The pure child is what pays off.

Decision Trees: Gini impurity

Draw two examples from the node at random, with replacement. Gini = the probability that their classes differ:

$$P(\text{same})=\sum_{k=1}^Kp_k\cdot p_k\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{Gini}=1-\sum_{k=1}^Kp_k^2$$

both draws are of class $k$ with probability $p_k\cdot p_k$; add over the classes

$$\text{two classes: }\ 1-p^2-(1-p)^2=1-p^2-1+2p-p^2=2p(1-p)$$

Our example. Root: $2\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac12=0.5$.

$$A:\ \ 2\cdot\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac14=0.375\ \text{(both children)}\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta=0.5-0.375=0.125$$
$$B:\ \ (8,4)\to2\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac13=0.444,\qquad(0,4)\to0$$ $$\Delta=0.5-\Big(\tfrac{12}{16}\cdot0.444+\tfrac{4}{16}\cdot0\Big)=0.5-0.333=0.167$$

B wins again. Gini is the CART and scikit-learn default: no logarithm, and in practice almost the same trees as entropy.

entropy (max 1 bit)Gini $2p(1-p)$ (max 0.5)misclassification $\min(p,1-p)$

All three are 0 for a pure node and largest for a 50/50 node. The third one, the misclassification error $1-\max_kp_k$, looks like the natural choice. Next slide: why trees don't use it.

Misclassification of a node = the fraction in the minority class, $1-\max_kp_k$.

A: $\frac12\cdot\frac14+\frac12\cdot\frac14=0.25$.  B: $\frac{12}{16}\cdot\frac13+\frac{4}{16}\cdot0=0.25$. A tie: both questions leave exactly 4 cars in a minority (2 + 2, or 4 + 0).

The weighted impurity after a split is a point on the chord between the two children, because the parent's $p$ is the weighted average of theirs.

Gini and entropy are curved (strictly concave): the chord lies below the curve, and the more extreme the children, the lower it gets. Pure children are rewarded.

The misclassification error is made of straight pieces, so many useful splits don't change it at all. It is still fine for measuring the final tree.

Decision Trees: searching the threshold

For a numeric feature the question is $x_j\le s$. Which thresholds $s$ do we try?

$$\Delta I=I(\text{parent})-\Big(\frac{N_L}{N}I(L)+\frac{N_R}{N}I(R)\Big)$$

The same formula for any impurity $I$. With entropy, $\Delta I$ is the information gain.

Only midpoints between consecutive sorted values change the partition: 8 distinct values give only 7 candidate thresholds. Sort the feature once, and all of them are scored in one pass.

By hand, 8 points with root 4 A / 4 B (Gini 0.5), threshold 3.5: left (A,A,A) has Gini 0; right (B,A,B,B,B) has $1-0.2^2-0.8^2=0.32$. Weighted: $\frac38\cdot0+\frac58\cdot0.32=0.20$, so the gain is $0.5-0.20=0.30$.

Decision Trees: the CART algorithm

Start at the root with the whole training set, then at every node:

  1. Stop? If the node is pure, too small or at max_depth, make it a leaf: mean, majority class or class proportions.
  2. Search: for every feature $j$ and every candidate threshold $s$, compute the gain $\Delta I(j,s)$.
  3. Split: keep the best $(j^*,s^*)$. Examples with $x_{j^*}\le s^*$ go left, the rest go right.
  4. Recurse: repeat 1–3 on each child, independently.

Predict: drop $\bx$ down the tree, one question per level, until it reaches a leaf.

grow(S, depth):
  if stop(S, depth):      # pure, too small, max_depth
    return Leaf(value(S))  # mean / majority / proportions
  for each feature j, threshold s:
    score ΔI(j, s)
  (j*, s*) = argmax ΔI
  return Node(j*, s*,
      grow(S_left,  depth + 1),
      grow(S_right, depth + 1))

Greedy: each split is the best one-step choice and is never revisited. Finding the optimal tree is NP-hard; the greedy tree is fast and works well. A worked example.

Decision Trees: regression trees

Same algorithm. The leaf predicts the mean, and the impurity is the variance of $y$ in the node:

$$I_V(S)=\frac1N\sum_{i\in S}\big(y^{(i)}-\bar y_S\big)^2\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ N\,I_V(S)=\mathrm{SSE}(S)$$

$\mathrm{SSE}(S)$ = the squared error of predicting the mean on $S$. Multiply the gain by $N$:

$$N\,\Delta I_V=\mathrm{SSE}(\text{parent})-\big[\mathrm{SSE}(L)+\mathrm{SSE}(R)\big]$$

So the best split is the one that reduces the squared error the most.

By hand: $y=\{1,2,6,7\}$, mean 4: SSE $=9+4+4+9=26$. Split into $\{1,2\}$ and $\{6,7\}$: means 1.5 and 6.5, SSE $=0.5+0.5=1$. The split removes $25$ of the $26$.

The prediction is a step function. Each extra level can double the number of steps.

  • Grown until every leaf is pure, a tree reaches zero training error: with at most $m-1$ splits it can isolate each of the $m$ training points.
  • Low bias, very high variance.
  • Boundaries are staircases of axis-aligned segments.
  • Depth 2 predicts the same as depth 1 here: its splits change no majority, but they make depth 3 possible. A greedy tree can need splits that look useless on their own.

Decision Trees: regularization

1 · Stop early (pre-pruning). The scikit-learn hyperparameters:

  • max_depth: at most this many questions on any path (typically 3 to 10).
  • min_samples_leaf, min_samples_split: no tiny nodes.
  • min_impurity_decrease: skip splits with a small gain. max_leaf_nodes.

Simple, but short-sighted: a split that looks useless may enable very good ones below it (XOR is the classic case).

2 · Grow, then prune (post-pruning): collapse the branches that do not pay for themselves.

Cost-complexity pruning (CART's own method). For a penalty $\alpha\ge0$, keep the subtree $T$ of the big tree that minimizes

$$R_\alpha(T)=R(T)+\alpha\,|T|$$

$R(T)$ = training error, $|T|$ = number of leaves. Fit + $\alpha$ × complexity: regularization again.

Is a branch worth it? Branch $T_t$ below node $t$. Keeping it costs $R(T_t)+\alpha|T_t|$; collapsing it into one leaf costs $R(t)+\alpha\cdot1$. Collapse when

$$R(t)+\alpha\le R(T_t)+\alpha|T_t|\iff\alpha\ge\frac{R(t)-R(T_t)}{|T_t|-1}$$

Example: a branch with 3 leaves lowers the error from 0.20 to 0.14. It is collapsed as soon as $\alpha\ge\frac{0.20-0.14}{3-1}=0.03$.

Larger $\alpha$ → smaller tree. Choose $\alpha$ (ccp_alpha) by cross-validation.

Decision Trees: pros and cons

Advantages

  • Easy to read and to explain: a small tree is a set of if / else rules.
  • No feature scaling: only the order of the values matters, so any increasing transformation of a feature gives the same tree.
  • Numeric and (encoded) categorical features; non-linear effects and interactions are captured automatically.
  • Fast to train, and very fast to predict: one question per level.
  • Non-parametric: no assumption about the shape of the boundary.

Disadvantages

  • Unstable (high variance): a small change in the data can change the top split, and with it the whole tree.
  • Very prone to overfitting: with $m-1$ splits it can isolate every training point.
  • Axis-aligned and piecewise constant: a diagonal boundary needs a long staircase.
  • Can't extrapolate: outside the training range it predicts the constant of the last leaf.
  • Greedy: not the best possible tree.

The fix for the variance: average many trees (next topic).
