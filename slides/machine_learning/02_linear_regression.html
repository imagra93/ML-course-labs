Linear Regression – Start simple

The simplest possible model: a line through the origin, with a single parameter, the slope $\theta$:

$$h_\theta(x)=\theta\,x$$

Three training points: $(1,1)$, $(2,3)$, $(3,7)$. Which slope fits them best?

Measure the fit with a cost: half the average squared error,

$$J(\theta)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m\big(\theta x^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\big)^2$$

Each orange square has side = the error of one point, so $J$ is their average area, halved (the $\frac12$ only makes the derivative cleaner). The black segment on the right is the slope $J'(\theta)$.

Linear Regression – The best θ, step by step

At the bottom of the bowl the slope is zero. Differentiate $J$ with respect to $\theta$:

$$J'(\theta)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m2\big(\theta x^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\big)\cdot x^{(i)}=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\big(\theta x^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\big)\,x^{(i)}$$

chain rule: the derivative of $(\theta x-y)^2$ is $2(\theta x-y)\cdot x$; the 2 cancels the $\frac12$

$$=\frac1m\Big(\theta\sum_{i=1}^m\big(x^{(i)}\big)^2-\sum_{i=1}^mx^{(i)}y^{(i)}\Big)=0$$

split the sum in two; $\theta$ is the same in every term, so it comes out

$$\theta^*=\frac{\sum_ix^{(i)}y^{(i)}}{\sum_i\big(x^{(i)}\big)^2}$$

With our three points $(1,1)$, $(2,3)$, $(3,7)$:

$$\sum_ix^{(i)}y^{(i)}=1\cdot1+2\cdot3+3\cdot7=28$$ $$\sum_i\big(x^{(i)}\big)^2=1+4+9=14$$
$$\theta^*=\frac{28}{14}=2$$
$$J(2)=\frac16\Big[(2-1)^2+(4-3)^2+(6-7)^2\Big]=\frac36=0.5$$

$m=3$, so $\frac1{2m}=\frac16$; the three errors are $+1$, $+1$, $-1$

No iterations needed: a closed-form solution. On the previous slide, $\theta=2$ is exactly where the slope $J'$ is 0.

Linear Regression – Is it really the minimum? Convexity

A zero slope could also be a maximum. Expand the square inside $J$:

$$J(\theta)=\frac1{2m}\sum_i\Big(\theta^2\big(x^{(i)}\big)^2-2\theta\,x^{(i)}y^{(i)}+\big(y^{(i)}\big)^2\Big)$$

$(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2$ with $a=\theta x^{(i)}$, $b=y^{(i)}$

$$=\underbrace{\frac{\sum_i\big(x^{(i)}\big)^2}{2m}}_{a\,\ge\,0}\,\theta^2-\underbrace{\frac{\sum_ix^{(i)}y^{(i)}}{m}}_{b}\,\theta+\underbrace{\frac{\sum_i\big(y^{(i)}\big)^2}{2m}}_{c}$$

a parabola $a\theta^2-b\theta+c$ with $a\ge0$: it opens upwards, a bowl

$$J''(\theta)=2a=\frac1m\sum_i\big(x^{(i)}\big)^2\ \ge\ 0$$

the second derivative is an average of squares: it is never negative

$J''\ge0$ everywhere means $J$ is convex: one bowl, no other valleys. The point where $J'=0$ is the global minimum, and it is unique as soon as one $x^{(i)}\ne0$.

Our example: $J(\theta)=\frac16\big(14\theta^2-56\theta+59\big)$ and $J''=\frac{14}{3}\gt0$ ✓

Coming next, in general. Many features and an intercept: $h_{\bth}(\bx)=\bth^\top\bx$. The same three steps (cost → derivative $=0$ → convexity) come back in matrix form: the normal equation $\bth=(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\by$ and the Hessian $\bX^\top\bX$. With one feature and no intercept they give back $\theta^*=\frac{\sum xy}{\sum x^2}$.

The general model: $n$ features and an intercept $\theta_0$. Approximate $y$ as a linear function of $\bx$:

$$h_{\bth}(\bx)=\theta_0+\theta_1x_1+\dots+\theta_nx_n=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\theta_jx_j=\bth^\top\bx$$

$\theta$'s are called the parameters (also called weights). For $n=1$, $\theta_0$ is the intercept and $\theta_1$ the slope.

Notice that for convenience we made $x_0=1$.

How do we select the $\theta$'s?

Linear Regression – The cost function

Exactly as for $h=\theta x$: measure, for each $\bth$, how close $h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})$ is to $y^{(i)}$. The cost function:

$$J(\bth)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\Big)^2$$

The least-squares cost of the ordinary least squares (OLS) model. Two ways to minimize it:

  1. Gradient descent: walk downhill, step by step (next slides).
  2. Set the gradient to zero and solve: the normal equation, a closed form like $\theta^*=\frac{\sum xy}{\sum x^2}$.

With two parameters $J(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ is still a bowl: its contour lines are rings (ellipses) around the minimum. In red, the 80 gradient-descent steps of the previous slide. Convexity for any $n$ is proved later, with the Hessian.

Linear Regression – Gradient Descent

Repeat until convergence (and simultaneously among all parameters, i.e. for $j\in\{0,\dots,n\}$):

$$\theta_j:=\theta_j-\alpha\,\frac{\partial J(\bth)}{\partial\theta_j}$$
  • Initial values of $\theta_j$? Anything, e.g. random: $J$ is convex, every start leads to the same minimum.
  • $\alpha$ is the learning rate. What value?
  • $\dfrac{\partial J(\bth)}{\partial\theta_j}$ is the gradient (direction of maximum slope). We step the opposite way: downhill.

Try α = 0.3, 1.0, 1.7 and 2.1 on the right (here J″ = 1).

How do we select the correct $\alpha$? The same bowl in all three panels; only $\alpha$ changes.

  • Too low ($\alpha=0.12$): safe but tiny steps, so it needs many updates.
  • Just right ($\alpha=0.7$): a few big steps land next to the minimum.
  • Too high ($\alpha=2.08$): every step jumps over the minimum and lands higher than before: it diverges.

Where do these numbers come from? On a bowl one step can be computed exactly. The next two slides do it and end with a rule for choosing $\alpha$.

Linear Regression – Gradient Descent: one step on a bowl

Near its minimum $\theta^*$ a cost curve looks like a bowl (with one feature it is exactly a bowl, as for our three points). Let $a=J''\gt0$ be its curvature. One step of gradient descent can then be computed exactly:

$$J(\theta)=J^*+\frac a2(\theta-\theta^*)^2$$

the bowl: lowest point $J^*$ at $\theta^*$; a larger $a$ means a narrower bowl

$$J'(\theta)=a\,(\theta-\theta^*)$$

the 2 from differentiating the square cancels the $\frac12$: the slope is proportional to the distance from the minimum

$$\theta_{\text{new}}=\theta-\alpha J'(\theta)=\theta-\alpha a\,(\theta-\theta^*)$$

the gradient-descent update

$$\theta_{\text{new}}-\theta^*=(\theta-\theta^*)-\alpha a(\theta-\theta^*)=(1-\alpha a)(\theta-\theta^*)$$

subtract $\theta^*$ from both sides, then take $(\theta-\theta^*)$ as a common factor

$$e_{\text{new}}=\rho\,e,\qquad e=\theta-\theta^*,\quad\rho=1-\alpha a$$

One step multiplies the distance to the minimum by $\rho$. Move $\alpha$ on the right (here $a=1$) and watch it.

Linear Regression – Gradient Descent: which α converges?

Every step multiplies the distance by the same $\rho$, so after $k$ steps

$$e_k=\rho^{\,k}\,e_0,\qquad\rho=1-\alpha a$$

$e_k\to0$ exactly when $|\rho|\lt1$, i.e. $-1\lt1-\alpha a\lt1$, i.e. $0\lt\alpha\lt2/a$. The sign of $\rho$ tells how it arrives (bowl of the previous slide, $a=1$):

With many parameters: $0\lt\alpha\lt2/\lambda_{\max}\big(\tfrac1m\bX^\top\bX\big)$, the largest curvature in any direction.

Linear Regression – Gradient Descent

The update $\theta_j:=\theta_j-\alpha\,\dfrac{\partial J(\bth)}{\partial\theta_j}$ needs the partial derivative. Let's compute $\dfrac{\partial J(\bth)}{\partial\theta_j}$ step by step:

$$\frac{\partial J(\bth)}{\partial\theta_j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\Big(\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)^2\Big)$$
$$=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)^2$$

the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives

$$=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)\cdot\frac{\partial\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)}{\partial\theta_j}$$

chain rule: $(u^2)'=2u\,u'$, and the 2 cancels the $\tfrac12$

$$=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)\cdot x_j^{(i)}$$

$h_{\bth}=\sum_k\theta_kx_k$, so $\partial h_{\bth}/\partial\theta_j=x_j$ (and $y^{(i)}$ does not depend on $\theta$)

$$\theta_j:=\theta_j-\alpha\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)\cdot x_j^{(i)}$$

"batch" gradient descent.
(the $m$ training samples in each step).

Each example pushes $\theta_j$ by (its error) × (its input). Examples already well predicted barely push. With one feature and no intercept it is the $J'(\theta)$ of the simple model.

Linear Regression – Gradient Descent

How do we make sure G.D. is working?

  • $J(\bth)$ decreases for every step. If not, $\alpha$ is too large or there is a bug.
  • If it decreases too slowly, $\alpha$ is too small.

Try and select a consistent $\alpha$. Examples: 0.001, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03 (right: a learning-rate sweep. Pick a value where the loss is still falling fast, about 10× smaller than where it blows up).

Linear Regression – Normal Equation.

Matrix notation:

$$\by=\begin{pmatrix}y^{(1)}\\y^{(2)}\\\vdots\\y^{(m)}\end{pmatrix}_{m\times1},\quad \bth=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_0\\\theta_1\\\vdots\\\theta_n\end{pmatrix}_{(n+1)\times1},\quad \bX=\begin{pmatrix}-(\bx^{(1)})^\top-\\-(\bx^{(2)})^\top-\\\vdots\\-(\bx^{(m)})^\top-\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&x_1^{(1)}&\cdots&x_n^{(1)}\\1&x_1^{(2)}&\cdots&x_n^{(2)}\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\1&x_1^{(m)}&\cdots&x_n^{(m)}\end{pmatrix}_{m\times(n+1)}$$

Taking this into account:

$$\hat\by=\bX\bth=\begin{pmatrix}(\bx^{(1)})^\top\bth\\\vdots\\(\bx^{(m)})^\top\bth\end{pmatrix}_{m\times1},\qquad \mathbf{E}_{m\times1}=\by-\hat\by=\by-\bX\bth$$

And finally:

$$J(\bth)=(\by-\bX\bth)^\top(\by-\bX\bth)=\by^\top\by-\by^\top\bX\bth-\bth^\top\bX^\top\by+\bth^\top\bX^\top\bX\bth$$

We drop the $\frac1{2m}$ here: multiplying $J$ by a positive constant does not move its minimum.

We can optimize by equating $\nabla_{\bth}J=\bzero$  $\big((n+1)\times1\big)$.

It turns out that there is a known closed-form solution!

Linear Regression – Normal Equation.

$$\nabla_{\bth}J(\bth)=\nabla_{\bth}\big(\by^\top\by-\by^\top\bX\bth-\bth^\top\bX^\top\by+\bth^\top\bX^\top\bX\bth\big)$$
$$=0-\bX^\top\by-\bX^\top\by+2\bX^\top\bX\bth=-2\bX^\top\by+2\bX^\top\bX\bth$$

So we want to solve $\ \bX^\top\bX\bth=\bX^\top\by$  (the "normal equations").

$$\bth=(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\by$$
  • Unique only if $\bX^\top\bX$ is invertible (can be replaced with the pseudo-inverse).
  • Slow if $n\gt10\,000$ (use gradient descent).
  • Check with the simple model (one feature, no intercept): $\bX$ is the column of $x$'s, so $\bX^\top\bX=\sum x^2$ and $\bX^\top\by=\sum xy$: $\ \theta=\frac{\sum xy}{\sum x^2}$ ✓

Geometrically: $\bX^\top(\by-\bX\bth)=\bzero$ says the error is perpendicular to every column of $\bX$. $\hat\by$ is the projection of $\by$ onto the columns.

HINT 1
$$\nabla_{\bth}(\ba^\top\bth)=\nabla_{\bth}(a_0\theta_0+\dots+a_n\theta_n)=\ba$$ $$\nabla_{\bth}(\bth^\top\ba)=\ba\quad\text{(same number)}$$

here $\ba=\bX^\top\by$, because $\by^\top\bX\bth=(\bX^\top\by)^\top\bth$

HINT 2
$$\nabla_{\bth}(\bth^\top\bX^\top\bX\bth)=2\bX^\top\bX\bth$$

To see why, write the product as a double sum:

$$\bth^\top\bX^\top\bX\bth=\sum_{i=0}^n\sum_{j=0}^n[\bX^\top\bX]_{ij}\theta_i\theta_j$$

Every pair $(i,j)$ adds $[\bX^\top\bX]_{ij}\theta_i\theta_j$. With 2 parameters: $p\theta_1^2+2q\theta_1\theta_2+r\theta_2^2$ ($p,q,r$: entries of $\bX^\top\bX$).

Differentiate with respect to one $\theta_k$: it appears in the terms with $i=k$ and with $j=k$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_k}=\sum_{j}[\bX^\top\bX]_{kj}\theta_j+\sum_{i}[\bX^\top\bX]_{ik}\theta_i=2\big[\bX^\top\bX\bth\big]_k$$

using that $\bX^\top\bX$ is symmetric, the two sums are equal (hence the 2). True for every $k$: the gradient is $2\bX^\top\bX\bth$.

Linear Regression – Normal Equation.

A critical point (where $\nabla_{\bth}J=\bzero$) is not automatically a minimum: this requires a second-order check. The Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives, the multi-parameter $J''$) is

$$\nabla^2_{\bth}J(\bth)=2\bX^\top\bX.$$

Positive semi-definiteness holds for any $\bX$. For an arbitrary vector $\bv$:

$$\bv^\top(\bX^\top\bX)\bv=(\bX\bv)^\top(\bX\bv)=\norm{\bX\bv}^2\ge0$$

Since the Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere, $J(\bth)$ is a convex function of $\bth$: the matrix version of $J''=\frac1m\sum x^2\ge0$. For a convex function, every critical point is automatically a global minimum: there are no saddle points or local maxima to worry about.

If $\bX^\top\bX$ is invertible, it is positive semi-definite with no zero eigenvalues, hence positive definite: $\norm{\bX\bv}^2\gt0$ for all $\bv\ne\bzero$. This makes $J(\bth)$ strictly convex, so the minimum is not merely global but unique, consistent with the uniqueness argument above.

Linear Regression – Newton method.

Newton's method for optimization updates a guess $\bth_{\text{old}}$ via:

$$\bth_{\text{new}}=\bth_{\text{old}}-\big[\nabla^2_{\bth}J(\bth_{\text{old}})\big]^{-1}\nabla_{\bth}J(\bth_{\text{old}})$$

Plug in the results from the previous slides:

$$\nabla_{\bth}J(\bth_{\text{old}})=-2\bX^\top\by+2\bX^\top\bX\bth_{\text{old}}$$ $$\nabla^2_{\bth}J(\bth_{\text{old}})=2\bX^\top\bX\ \ (\text{constant, does not depend on }\bth)$$

Substituting, starting from any $\bth_{\text{old}}$:

$$\bth_{\text{new}}=\bth_{\text{old}}-(2\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\big(-2\bX^\top\by+2\bX^\top\bX\bth_{\text{old}}\big)$$ $$=\bth_{\text{old}}-\underbrace{(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\bX}_{=\,\bI}\,\bth_{\text{old}}+(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\by$$ $$=\bth_{\text{old}}-\bth_{\text{old}}+(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\by=(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\by$$

The $\bth_{\text{old}}$ terms cancel exactly, regardless of the starting point.

Interpretation. Because $J(\bth)$ is exactly quadratic, its Hessian $2\bX^\top\bX$ is constant. Newton's method, which normally needs many iterations, lands exactly on the global minimum in a single step, from any starting point.

The normal equation is precisely this one Newton step. Gradient descent takes many small steps to the same point; Newton reaches it at once, at the cost of computing and inverting $\bX^\top\bX$. That is why it is impractical for large $n$ (slow if $n\gt10\,000$).

Linear regression is linear in the parameters $\bth$, not in $x$. The model never changes, only the column we feed it:

$$h(x)=\theta_0\cdot1+\theta_1\cdot x_1,\qquad x_1=\text{any function of }x$$

With $x_1=x^2$ a curve in $x$ is a straight line in $x_1$. Same cost $J$, same gradient descent, same normal equation $\bth=(\bX^\top\bX)^{-1}\bX^\top\by$.

Several features at once, $x_1=x$ and $x_2=x^2$: $h=\theta_0+\theta_1x_1+\theta_2x_2$ is a plane in the space of features; seen from $x$ it is a parabola.

Linear Regression – Feature encoding

Data is not always numerical. You must encode the categorical data to numbers before fitting your model.

  • Ordinal Variables (the categories have an order).
    • Ordinal Encoding: replace the names by numbers $0,1,\dots,C-1$ (or $1,\dots,C$) in that order.
      • Bad → 0, Good → 1, Excellent → 2
      • In scikit-learn, give the order explicitly: the categories argument, a list with the rank order of all expected labels.
  • Nominal Variables (no ordered relation).
    • One-Hot Encoding – 1 bit per category (new "features")
      • Cow → [1,0,0], Dog → [0,1,0], Cat → [0,0,1]
    • Dummy Encoding – C categories with C−1 binary variables.
      • Cow → [1,0], Dog → [0,1], Cat → [0,0]
      • Same data is represented.
      • For algorithms with intercept term (such as linear regression).
      • One-Hot Encoding makes $\bX^\top\bX$ non-invertible (linear dependence).

Linear Regression – Feature scaling

  • Why? Gradient descent converges faster, and penalties (regularization) treat all the features alike.
  • Normalization (min–max): $\ X'=\dfrac{X-X_{\min}}{X_{\max}-X_{\min}}\in[0,1]$
  • Standardization: $\ X'=\dfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}$, mean 0 and standard deviation 1, no fixed range.

When to use which?

  • Standardization: the default for linear and logistic regression, SVMs, neural networks and any model with a penalty.
  • Min–max: when a fixed range is needed (e.g. pixel intensities to [0, 1]). One outlier squeezes all the other values into a tiny range.
  • Outliers: standardization suffers less; RobustScaler (median and interquartile range) the least. Trees need no scaling at all.
  • Compute min, max, $\mu$, $\sigma$ on the training data only.

Linear Regression – Regularization

Add a penalization in the cost function to the weights!

  • L2 (Ridge regression): $\ J(\bth)=\dfrac{1}{2m}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^m\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)^2+\dfrac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^n\theta_j^2\ $ (not $j=0$)
    • Shrinks the coefficients.
  • L1 (Lasso regression): $\ J(\bth)=\dfrac{1}{2m}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^m\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)^2+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^n|\theta_j|$
    • Forces some of the coefficients to be zero and results in a simpler model (feature selection).
    • Not differentiable at 0.
  • Elastic Net: the combination of the L1 and L2 regularization.

Chapter 05 goes through regularization slowly: where the formulas come from, why L1 gives exact zeros, and how to pick λ.

Linear Regression – Metrics

Metrics measure the performance of a model on validation and test data. Unlike losses, they don't need to be differentiable.

  • Mean Square Error (MSE): $\ \dfrac1m\displaystyle\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2$
    • Squares the errors: big errors count much more, so it is sensitive to outliers.
    • In squared units (€²): hard to read.
  • Mean Absolute Error (MAE): $\ \dfrac1m\displaystyle\sum_i|y_i-\hat y_i|$
    • Every unit of error counts the same: more robust to outliers.
    • Same units as $y$: "on average we are 12 k€ off".
    • Not differentiable at 0, but it can also be used as a loss.
  • Root Mean Square Error (RMSE): $\ \sqrt{\dfrac1m\displaystyle\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}$
    • Ranks models exactly like MSE, but in the units of $y$.
    • Always $\ge$ MAE; much larger when a few errors are big.

Linear Regression – Metrics

"How much (what %) of the total variation in Y (target) is explained by the variation in X (regression line)"

  • Coefficient of determination $\ R^2=1-\dfrac{\sum_i^m(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i^m(y_i-\bar y)^2}$
    • 1: perfect predictions.
    • 0: no better than always predicting the mean $\bar y$.
    • On new data it can be negative: worse than the mean.
    • On the training data it never goes down when a feature is added, even pure noise: it cannot choose the features.

Best predictor before I knew $X$ vs knowing it
(weight of a person vs weight after knowing their height).

  • Adjusted $\ R_a^2=1-\Big(\dfrac{m-1}{m-n-1}\Big)(1-R^2)$, where $m$ is the number of samples and $n$ the number of features.
    • Only increases if adding the feature really helps.

Report on validation or test data, in the units the business understands. In the worked example, MAPE went from 6.5 % to 24.5 %: the big error was on the cheapest house.

Linear Regression – Probabilistic interpretation

What we want to show. Why do we minimize the sum of squared errors and not, say, the absolute errors? Assume the targets are the line plus random noise. The $\bth$ that makes the observed data most probable (maximum likelihood) turns out to be the one that minimizes the squared error.

Assume target variables and inputs are related via:

$$y^{(i)}=\bth^\top\bx^{(i)}+e^{(i)},\qquad e^{(i)}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\ \text{i.i.d.}$$
$$p(e^{(i)})=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(e^{(i)})^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)$$

which implies that

$$p(y^{(i)}\mid\bx^{(i)};\bth)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y^{(i)}-\bth^\top\bx^{(i)})^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)$$

Given $\bX$ and $\bth$, the probability of the data is $p(\by\mid\bX;\bth)$. As a function of $\bth$: $L(\bth)=p(\by\mid\bX;\bth)$. Assuming i.i.d. samples:

$$L(\bth)=\prod_{i=1}^mp(y^{(i)}\mid\bx^{(i)};\bth)=\prod_{i=1}^m\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y^{(i)}-\bth^\top\bx^{(i)})^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)$$

To make the problem simpler we maximize the log of $L(\bth)$ (monotonically increasing function):

$$\ell(\bth)=\log L(\bth)=\sum_{i=1}^m\log\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y^{(i)}-\bth^\top\bx^{(i)})^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)\Big)$$ $$=m\log\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^m\big(y^{(i)}-\bth^\top\bx^{(i)}\big)^2$$

The first term does not depend on $\bth$. Equivalent to minimizing:

$$\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m\big(h_{\bth}(\bx^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)^2$$

Maximum likelihood: choose $\bth$ to make the data as highly probable as possible, i.e., maximize $L(\bth)$. Least squares = MLE under Gaussian noise.
