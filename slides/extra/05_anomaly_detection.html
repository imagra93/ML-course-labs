Anomaly detection

imanolgranada@gmail.com

Where we are

The whole course learned from labels. Anomalies rarely come with them: they are rare (1 case in 100, or in 10,000), varied (every fraudster invents a new trick) and nobody can list them in advance.

So learn what normal looks like, and give every case a score: how far it is from normal. The top of the ranking goes to a person: an investigator, an engineer, a doctor.

Anomaly: "an observation which deviates so much from other observations as to arouse suspicions that it was generated by a different mechanism" (Hawkins, 1980).

Five parts: A what an anomaly is and why base rates matter, B statistical baselines, C distances, densities and isolation (LOF, Isolation Forest), D boundaries and reconstructions (one-class SVM, PCA, autoencoders), E evaluation and practice. It ends with the Extra 5 lab.

Part A
What is an
anomaly?

  • point, contextual, collective

  • supervised, semi-supervised, unsupervised

Three kinds of anomaly

Point

One case is unusual on its own. A €12,000 claim on a €13,000 car that has been insured for a week.

Contextual

Unusual only in its context: time, place, type. 100 claims on a Wednesday in July; normal on a Monday in January.

Collective

Each case looks normal, the group does not. Ordinary claims from young policies, all at one small garage, in one month.

Most detectors score points. Contextual anomalies become point anomalies with the right features (the amount against its claim type, today against the same weekday last month); collective ones with aggregates (per garage, per day). Part E comes back to both.

Every method gives a score $s(\bx)$, higher = more anomalous. A decision needs a threshold $\tau$ (flag if $s(\bx)\gt\tau$), or, more often, an alert budget $k$: the $k$ highest scores go to the investigators.

Base rates: 99.9 % accuracy means nothing

A detector with recall (TPR) 90 % and false positive rate (FPR) 1 %. Fraud is 0.1 % to 1 % of the claims. Bayes' rule gives the precision of its alerts:

$$P(\text{fraud}\mid\text{alert})=\frac{\text{TPR}\,\pi}{\text{TPR}\,\pi+\text{FPR}\,(1-\pi)}$$

When $\pi$ is small the denominator is the false alarms among the huge honest majority. "Everything is normal" has 99.9 % accuracy at $\pi = 0.1\,\%$ and catches nothing.

So: never report accuracy; report what the investigators see, the precision of the alerts they can review (precision@$k$), and the average precision (Part E). The FPR must be tiny.

The investigator's day: a team that reviews 50 claims a day does not care about the 99,000 claims it never sees, only about how many of its 50 are real.

The z-score and masking

$z_i = (x_i-\bar x)/\sigma$, flag $|z|\gt3$. But $\bar x$ and $\sigma$ are computed with the outliers: they pull the mean and inflate $\sigma$. This is masking.

A hard limit: with $n$ values, $|z_i|\le\sqrt{n-1}$.

$$\textstyle\sum_{i\ge2}x_i^2\ge\frac{x_1^2}{n-1}$$
centre ($\bar x=0$): the others sum to $-x_1$
$$\textstyle n\sigma^2\ge x_1^2\frac{n}{n-1}$$
so $z_1^2\le n-1$. With $n=10$: $|z|\le3$, always.

$m$ equal outliers at $v\to\infty$: $z\to\sqrt{(n-m)/m}$. Five outliers among 55 values: 3.16, however absurd $v$ is.

The robust z (green) uses the median and the MAD, which ignore up to half of the data: it grows without limit. Next slide.

Robust z with numbers: median, MAD, IQR

Ten claims (€): 420, 480, 510, 530, 560, 590, 620, 650, 2,900, 3,100.

Two claims look absurd to any human. Does the 3-sigma rule agree?

$\bar x = 1{,}036$, $\sigma = 985$.

$z(3{,}100) = (3{,}100-1{,}036)/985 = 2.10$, $z(2{,}900) = 1.89$.

Nothing above 3, and with $n=10$ nothing ever could be.

$\med = 575$. Deviations $|x-575|$ sorted: 15, 15, 45, 45, 65, 75, 95, 155, 2,325, 2,525: $\MAD = 70$.

$z_r = 0.6745\,(x-\med)/\MAD$:

$z_r(3{,}100) = 0.6745\cdot2{,}525/70 = 24.3$.

$0.6745=\Phi^{-1}(0.75)$: for a Gaussian $\MAD = 0.6745\,\sigma$, so $z_r\approx z$ on clean data. Flag $|z_r|\gt3.5$.

$Q_1 = 515$, $Q_3 = 642.5$, $\text{IQR} = 127.5$.

Fences $Q_1-1.5\,\text{IQR} = 324$ and $Q_3+1.5\,\text{IQR} = 834$: 2,900 and 3,100 are outside.

For a Gaussian the fences sit at $\pm2.70\,\sigma$ and flag 0.70 % of the values. The median and the quartiles have a breakdown point of 50 % and 25 %; the mean, 0 %.

Mahalanobis vs Euclidean distance

Correlated features: an amount grows with the vehicle value. A point can be ordinary on each feature and still break the relation. Measure distances in units of the data's own spread:

$$D_M(\bx)=\sqrt{(\bx-\bmu)^\top\bSigma^{-1}(\bx-\bmu)}$$

Whitening. Factor $\bSigma=\bL\bL^\top$ and set $\bz=\bL^{-1}(\bx-\bmu)$. Then $\mathrm{Cov}(\bz)=\bL^{-1}\bSigma\bL^{-\top}=\bI$ and

$$D_M^2=(\bx-\bmu)^\top\bL^{-\top}\bL^{-1}(\bx-\bmu)=\norm{\bz}^2$$

The Euclidean distance after decorrelating and rescaling. Here (estimated $\bSigma$, correlation 0.8): A, along the cloud, 3.11 → 2.27; B, against it, 1.68 → 3.74.

The $\chi^2$ threshold

If $\bx\sim\mathcal N(\bmu,\bSigma)$ then $\bz\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\bI)$, so

$$D_M^2=z_1^2+\dots+z_d^2\ \sim\ \chi^2_d$$

"Flag the 2.5 % most extreme Gaussian points" means $D_M^2\gt\chi^2_{d,\,0.975}$:

$d$121036
$\chi^2_{d,0.975}$5.027.3820.554.4
as a distance $D$2.242.724.537.38

$\mathbb E[D_M^2]=d$: in high dimension every point is far from the centre, and the threshold must grow with $d$.

In the lab, 3.0 % of the 500 Gaussian points exceed 7.38 (2.5 % expected). With heavy tails the $\chi^2$ quantile is too low: use an empirical quantile.

Robust covariance: the Minimum Covariance Determinant

A group of outliers drags $\hat\bmu$ towards itself and stretches $\hat\bSigma$ in its direction, until its own distances look ordinary: masking in $d$ dimensions.

MCD (Rousseeuw, 1984): among all subsets of $h\approx(n+d+1)/2$ points, take the one whose covariance has the smallest determinant (the tightest ellipse). Up to $n-h$ outliers cannot move it.

C-step: from a subset $H$, fit $\bmu,\bSigma$, keep the $h$ points with the smallest $D_M$. Theorem: the determinant never increases. Repeat from many random starts (FAST-MCD).

Then rescale by $\med(D^2)/\chi^2_{d,0.5}$ (half of the data was used) and re-weight. In the lab: 13 % outliers, classical 2 of 45 flagged, MCD 45 of 45; our C-steps find scikit-learn's exact subset.

Distance and density scores

Distance: $s(\bx)=d_k(\bx)$, the distance to the $k$-th neighbour.

Density: $s(\bx)=-\log\hat p(\bx)$, with a kernel estimate $\hat p(\bx)=\frac1n\sum_iK_h(\bx-\bx_i)$ or a Gaussian mixture fitted by EM (Extra 2).

They are the same idea: the ball of radius $d_k(\bx)$ holds $k$ points, so $\hat p(\bx)\approx k/(n\,V_d\,d_k^d)$.

A dense cluster ($\sigma=0.3$), a sparse one ($\sigma=1.2$), 2 local and 8 global outliers. $k$-NN and KDE use one scale everywhere: just outside the dense cluster is as empty as the edge of the sparse one, and 21 normal points score above a local outlier.

The GMM gives each cluster its own covariance and gets AUC 1.000 here, because the data really are two Gaussians. Real clusters rarely are.

Local Outlier Factor, step by step

Compare each point's density with its neighbours' (Breunig et al., 2000). Six points, $k=2$.

A, B, C, D: a 1 × 2 rectangle. E near it, F far away. Which points are outliers, and how much?

$k$-distance $d_k(\bo)$: the distance to the $k$-th neighbour; $\Nk(\bo)$ = those $k$ neighbours.

Corners: neighbours at 1 and 2, so $d_2=2$. E: D at $\sqrt5=2.24$, B at $\sqrt{10}=3.16$. F: E at 5.83, D at 8.06.

$$\reach_k(\bx,\bo)=\max\{d(\bx,\bo),\,d_k(\bo)\}$$

Corner A to C: $\max(1,2)=2$. Inside a neighbour's own neighbourhood the distance is replaced by its $k$-distance: smoothing.

E: $\max(2.24,2)=2.24$, $\max(3.16,2)=3.16$. F: 5.83, 8.06.

$$\lrd_k(\bx)=1\Big/\tfrac1k\textstyle\sum_{\bo\in \Nk(\bx)}\reach_k(\bx,\bo)$$

Corners $1/2=0.50$. E: $1/2.70=0.370$. F: $1/6.95=0.144$

$$\LOF_k(\bx)=\tfrac1k\textstyle\sum_{\bo\in \Nk(\bx)}\lrd_k(\bo)\,/\,\lrd_k(\bx)$$

Corners: 1.00. E: $0.50/0.370=1.35$. F: $\tfrac12(0.370+0.50)/0.144=3.02$.

≈ 1 inside any cluster, whatever its density; ≫ 1 when sparser than its neighbours. scikit-learn: identical.

LOF finds the local outliers

Same data as two slides ago. LOF divides out the density of each region, so the dense and the sparse cluster both score about 1 (median 1.06 and 1.01 in the lab, $k=20$).

The two local outliers: ranked 9th and 7th by LOF, 31st and 29th by the $k$-NN distance. LOF's top 10 holds all 10 anomalies.

Choosing $k$: small $k$ is noisy; large $k$ becomes global. A group of $m$ outliers is its own neighbourhood when $k\lt m$: micro-clusters mask themselves. Take $k\ge 10$–20 and larger than the biggest group you want to find, or the max over several $k$.

Cost: all $k$-nearest neighbours, $O(n^2d)$ naively. Like every distance method, standardise the features first.

Isolation Forest: isolate, don't model

Liu, Ting and Zhou (2008): do not describe the normal data at all. Cut the space at random until the point is alone:

  1. pick a feature at random;
  2. pick a cut uniformly between its min and max in the current box;
  3. keep the side that holds the point; repeat.

Anomalies are few and different: they sit in empty space, and a random cut separates them early. A point inside a cluster needs many cuts.

The path length $h(\bx)$ = the number of cuts = the depth of $\bx$ in a random isolation tree. Average it over many trees: in the lab, 3.8 for a global outlier, 15.1 for the centre of the dense cluster.

Path length and the anomaly score

Path lengths need a common scale. The average depth of a point in a random binary tree on $n$ points (an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree):

$$c(n)=2H(n-1)-\frac{2(n-1)}{n},\qquad H(i)\approx\ln i+0.5772$$

$c(256)=2(\ln255+0.5772)-1.99=10.24$.

$$s(\bx,\psi)=2^{-\mathbb E[h(\bx)]/c(\psi)}$$

$\mathbb E[h]\to0$: $s\to1$. $\mathbb E[h]=c(\psi)$: $s=0.5$, nothing special. Long paths: $s\to0$. Trees stop at depth $\lceil\log_2\psi\rceil=8$; a leaf with $m$ points left adds $c(m)$.

scikit-learn's score_samples returns $-s$. Our from-scratch forest and scikit-learn's: Spearman correlation 0.98.

Isolation Forest in practice

Sub-sample: each tree sees only $\psi=256$ random points. Fewer normal points around an anomaly (less swamping) and fewer anomalies around each other (less masking).

Cost: $O(T\psi\log\psi)$ to build, $O(T\log\psi)$ per point, whatever $n$. Lab, 32,000 points: Isolation Forest 0.18 s, LOF 6.0 s, one-class SVM 2.9 s.

No distances: no scaling of the features needed, mixed units are fine. A good first try on tabular data.

It is global (the local outliers rank around 20th; lab: 22nd and 32nd) and its cuts are axis-parallel: stripes along the axes. Extended Isolation Forest (Hariri et al., 2019) cuts with random hyperplanes instead.

One-class SVM

The course's SVM with one class (Schölkopf et al., 2001): separate the data from the origin of the kernel's feature space with the largest margin.

$$\min\ \tfrac12\norm{\bw}^2+\tfrac{1}{\nu n}\textstyle\sum_i\xi_i-\rho\quad\text{s.t.}\ \bw^\top\phi(\bx_i)\ge\rho-\xi_i$$

$f(\bx)=\sum_i\alpha_ik(\bx_i,\bx)-\rho$; normal if $f\ge0$. RBF: a sum of bumps on the support vectors.

$\nu$. Dual: $0\le\alpha_i\le\frac1{\nu n}$, $\sum_i\alpha_i=1$. Outliers sit at the bound $\Rightarrow$ at most $\nu n$ of them. Support vectors have $\alpha_i\gt0$ $\Rightarrow$ at least $\nu n$. So: outliers $\le\nu\le$ support vectors.

$\gamma$ matters as much: no label-free way to tune it (default: $1/(d\,\mathrm{Var})$). Scale the features. Training cost grows like $n^2$.

SVDD (Tax and Duin, 2004): the smallest ball around the data in feature space; with the RBF kernel the same solution.

PCA reconstruction error

Normal data often live near a low-dimensional subspace. Keep the top $k$ principal directions $\mathbf V_k$ of the normal data:

$$\hx=\bmu+\mathbf V_k\mathbf V_k^\top(\bx-\bmu),\qquad s(\bx)=\norm{\bx-\hx}^2$$

The error is the squared distance from $\bx$ to the subspace: it catches points that break the correlations, even when no single feature is extreme (B).

It ignores how far a point is along the subspace (A). Mahalanobis = the two parts together, each in its own units.

MNIST eights, 784 pixels: 16 components keep 60 % of the variance; AUC against the other digits 0.854 with 1 component, 0.861 with 16, 0.792 with 256: too many components reconstruct the anomalies too.

Autoencoders: error and threshold

A non-linear PCA (Extra 3): encoder to a 16-number code, decoder back, trained on normal data only (5,266 eights) to minimise $\norm{\bx-\hx}^2$. Score: the error.

Novelty detection sets $\tau$ without anomalies: hold out clean normal data and take a high quantile of their errors. The 95 % quantile fixes the false-alarm rate at about 5 % (4.8 % on the test eights).

Test set with 10 % other digits: AUC 0.919 (PCA 0.891). Moving $\tau$ trades recall against false alarms; the base rate then decides the precision.

The blind spot: simple inputs look normal

Trained on eights, the autoencoder detects every digit with AUC ≥ 0.93, except the ones: 0.463, worse than random (PCA: 0.404). A one has half the ink of an eight (7.7 % of the pixels vs 15.3 %) and a smaller error (0.0213 vs 0.0229). The 50 anomalies with the smallest error are all ones.

Fix: divide the error by the amount of ink. Ones: 0.463 → 0.893; all digits 0.897 → 0.955 (input-complexity correction, Serrà et al., 2020).

The likelihood caveat. Flows, PixelCNNs and VAEs trained on CIFAR-10 give higher likelihood to SVHN house numbers than to CIFAR-10 test images; trained on Fashion-MNIST, they prefer MNIST (Nalisnick et al., 2019). Simple images are easy to model, whatever they show.

So a VAE's ELBO or a flow's $\log p(\bx)$ is not a safe score alone: compare with a reference (likelihood ratios, typicality, complexity) and test on a few known anomalies.

ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC under imbalance

One detector: normal scores $\mathcal N(0,1)$, anomalies $\mathcal N(2,1)$. Only the base rate $\pi$ changes.

ROC: TPR against FPR. Both are rates within a class, so the curve and its area do not see $\pi$: ROC-AUC $=\Phi(2/\sqrt2)=0.921$ always.

Precision–recall: precision mixes the classes, so it collapses with $\pi$. Average precision $\text{AP}=\sum_j(R_j-R_{j-1})P_j$; a random ranking has AP $=\pi$.

The black dot: threshold $\tau = 2$, recall 50 %, FPR 2.3 %. At $\pi=0.1\,\%$ its precision is 2.2 %.

With rare anomalies, a ROC-AUC of 0.92 can mean that 49 alerts in 50 are false. Report AP and precision@$k$, and compare AP with $\pi$.

Precision@$k$ and the alert budget

Satellite (Goldstein and Uchida, 2016): 5,100 Landsat patches, 36 features, 75 anomalies ($\pi=1.47\,\%$), used without labels; the labels only evaluate.

$$\text{P@}k=\frac{\#\{\text{anomalies in the top }k\}}{k}$$

It is what the investigators experience. With $k$ = number of anomalies it is the R-precision; recall@$k$ says how much of the problem the team sees.

Choose $k$ from the team's capacity, then compare detectors at that $k$: the ranking can change with $k$.

Satellite: ROC-AUC flatters, AP tells

ROC-AUC 0.84 to 0.97 for all but LOF; AP from 0.15 to 0.64 (random: 0.015).

The GMM: ROC-AUC 0.939, AP 0.146. To catch 60 of the 75 anomalies it raises 482 alerts, 422 false: an FPR of only 8.4 %, a precision of 12.4 %.

Ensembles. Scores live on unrelated scales (the one-class SVM's from −16.5 to 24.7, the forest's from 0.40 to 0.75): normalise (ranks, z-scores) before averaging. The mean z of three different principles (distance, isolation, boundary) gets AP 0.653, above each member.

Averaging all eight is worse (AP 0.488, 0.222 with ranks): weak members drag the top down. Choose members, normalise, average or take the max.

Evaluate with the labels you have, even a few

Often only a handful of confirmed anomalies exist (past frauds). Simulate it on Satellite: keep all normal points and $m$ random anomalies, 300 draws.

With $m=10$, the Isolation Forest's AUC lies anywhere in [0.877, 0.992], and it ranks above the $k$-NN distance in 18 % of the draws, though it is worse with all labels: a few labels cannot rank two good detectors.

But they reject a bad one: LOF comes out last in 99 % of the draws with $m=10$.

So: always evaluate on the labelled anomalies you have, report the spread, use them to reject detectors and to set thresholds; do not crown a winner by 0.01 of AUC.

Thresholds from costs

Lab, 20,000 synthetic claims, 200 frauds. An investigation costs €150; a caught fraud saves its amount (€5,661 on average).

$$V(k)=\sum_{i\le k}y_{(i)}\,\text{amount}_{(i)}-150\,k$$

Investigate while the marginal alert is worth it: $P(\text{fraud}\mid s)\cdot\overline{\text{amount}}\gt150$, a break-even precision of only 2.6 %. Here $k^*=835$.

contamination in scikit-learn does not change the scores: it only places predict's cut so that this fraction of the training data is flagged (lab: 10.74 % with "auto", 1.00 % with 0.01, identical scores). Set the threshold from costs and budgets.

Same Isolation Forest, raw columns vs 6 engineered features: AP 0.280 → 0.758, P@200 0.315 → 0.730. "€12,000 on a €13,000 car" is two ordinary numbers until you divide them; a shop_id means nothing as a number.

Still missed: inflated glass claims (2 of 35), extreme on one feature of six. Many-feature detectors dilute single-feature anomalies.

Explaining an alert

An investigator needs a reason, not a score. Occlusion: replace one feature at a time by its median and see how much the score drops. Model-agnostic, two lines of code; SHAP values are the principled version.

Show the raw facts behind the top features ("€8,020 theft on a €9,500 car, 5 claims this year, reported after 49 days"), with a per-feature robust z for context. Explanations also catch bugs: an alert driven by an ID column is a data problem.

Drift and seasonality

Daily claims: winter storms (×2 vs summer), quiet weekends, a trend, Poisson noise. Injected: a summer spike, a hailstorm (5 days), an outage, a December spike.

A contextual baseline: compare each day with its expected value, $z_t=(y_t-b_t)/\sqrt{b_t}$ for counts. Rolling: $b_t$ = median of the same weekday over the last 4 weeks. Calendar model: a Poisson regression on weekday, yearly sines and cosines, trend.

Only the past may enter $b_t$: a centred window does not exist in production, and lets an event raise its own baseline. Refit as the business drifts.

A run of moderate days (the hailstorm) is a collective anomaly: a cumulative sum of the recent $z$ (CUSUM) sees it better than any single day.

From scores to labels: the feedback loop

Every investigated alert is a label. Lab: the first 10,000 synthetic claims are the past, the last 10,000 the future. Average precision on the future:

Gradient boosting learns the inflated glass claims the forest missed (15 of 21 in its top 100, against 1). Keep the unsupervised detector for new patterns.

Label bias: a model trained only on investigated alerts learns what the old detector already found. Audit a random sample too.
