Where we are

The whole course so far was supervised: every example comes with the right answer, and the loss says how far we are from it.

Often nobody knows the right answer, only whether the outcome was good: a game won after 200 moves, a robot that did not fall, a click, a thumbs up. And the agent's own choices decide what it sees next.

Reinforcement learning (RL): learn a policy, a rule from situations to actions, that maximises the total reward collected over time, by trial and error.

Four parts: A the RL problem and bandits, B MDPs and planning (Bellman equations, value and policy iteration), C learning from experience (TD, SARSA, Q-learning, DQN), D policy gradients, PPO, RLHF and practice. It ends with the Extra 12 lab.

Part A
The RL problem

The agent–environment loop

At each step $t$ the agent observes a state $S_t$, picks an action $A_t$ with its policy $\pi(a\mid s)$, and the environment answers with a reward $R_{t+1}$ and the next state $S_{t+1}$.

An episode: $S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, A_1, R_2, \dots$ until a terminal state.

The goal: maximise the expected return, the discounted sum of future rewards,

$$G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\gamma^2R_{t+3}+\dots$$

The gridworld of the lab: start S, exits +1 and +10, a cliff (−10) along the bottom, a slippery floor, $\gamma=0.9$. This episode follows the optimal policy and happens not to slip.

When labels exist, use them. Supervised learning is the special case with one step and the reward $-\text{loss}$ for the action "predict $\hat y$". RL is the tool for when you can only score outcomes, and your decisions change what happens next.

Not unsupervised learning either: there is a signal, but it evaluates instead of instructing. The data are not given: the agent has to go and get them.

Where RL is used

Games

Atari from pixels (DQN, 2015), Go (AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol, 2016), StarCraft II, Dota 2. A simulator gives unlimited free episodes.

Robotics

Walking, grasping, in-hand manipulation. Trained in simulation with randomised physics, then transferred to the real robot (sim-to-real).

Recommendation and ads

Each recommendation is an action, the click is the reward. Contextual bandits for news (Yahoo!, 2010), long-term engagement.

Pricing and operations

Dynamic pricing, inventory, ride-hailing dispatch: today's decision changes tomorrow's demand and stock.

Language models

RLHF: people compare answers, a reward model learns their taste, PPO tunes the model (InstructGPT, 2022). RL on checkable rewards for maths and code.

Control

Data-centre cooling (DeepMind and Google, 2016), the shape of the plasma in a fusion tokamak (Nature, 2022).

Bandits: explore or exploit?

The simplest RL problem, one state: $K$ slot machines, arm $a$ pays 1 with unknown probability $\mu_a$. Lab: $K=10$, best arm $\mu^*=0.8$, second best 0.7.

Regret after $T$ pulls: $\sum_t(\mu^*-\mu_{A_t})$, the reward lost while learning.

greedy: always the best average $\hat\mu_a$. Can lock onto a bad arm forever.

ε-greedy: a random arm with probability ε. It pays for exploration forever: linear regret, slope $\varepsilon\,(\mu^*-\bar\mu)=0.033$ per pull.

$$\text{UCB:}\quad A_t=\arg\max_a\ \hat\mu_a+c\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{N_a}}$$

UCB: optimism in the face of uncertainty, rarely tried arms get a bonus. Regret $O(\log T)$, but the constant matters.

Thompson sampling: act on a sample of your beliefs

Keep a belief about each $\mu_a$. With a uniform prior and $S_a$ successes in $N_a$ pulls, Bayes gives a Beta posterior. Then:

$$\theta_a\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1+S_a,\,1+N_a-S_a),\quad A_t=\arg\max_a\theta_a$$

Each arm is pulled with the probability that it is the best, given the data. Wide posteriors give wild samples: exploration, which fades by itself as the posteriors shrink.

Lab: after 1,000 pulls the best arm has 769; the 0.6 and 0.7 arms got 116 and 37, the others 4 to 14. Regret after 5,000 pulls: 62, as good as tuned UCB, and growing like $\log t$. No strategy can beat $\log T$ (Lai and Robbins, 1985).

Bandits are RL with one state: the action changes the reward, not the future. Contextual bandits, where arm values depend on the user's features, run recommendation, adaptive A/B tests and dynamic pricing.

Part B
MDPs and planning

  • states, transitions, discount
  • values and the Bellman equations

Markov decision processes

An MDP $(\Sc,\Ac,P,r,\gamma)$: states, actions, transition probabilities $P(s'\mid s,a)$, rewards $r(s,a,s')$ and a discount $\gamma$.

Markov property: the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on the path that led there. If it does not, put more into the state (the last frames of a game, a velocity).

Return: $G_t=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^kR_{t+k+1}=R_{t+1}+\gamma\,G_{t+1}$. Finite for $\gamma\lt1$; a reward 10 steps away counts $0.9^{10}=0.35$.

Gridworld of the lab: the reward is the exit's value when entering A (+1), B (+10) or the cliff (−10), and 0 otherwise. The short way to both exits runs along the cliff.

Why discount? Bounded sums, uncertainty about the far future, a preference for sooner rewards. $\gamma$ also sets the horizon: about $1/(1-\gamma)=10$ steps.

Policies and value functions

A policy $\pi(a\mid s)$ gives the probability of each action in each state; a deterministic one is a map $s\mapsto a$.

$$V^\pi(s)=\E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s],\quad Q^\pi(s,a)=\E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a]$$

$V^\pi$: how good it is to be in $s$ and follow $\pi$. $Q^\pi$: how good to take $a$ first, then follow $\pi$. So $V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\,Q^\pi(s,a)$.

Why $Q$? With $Q$ you can act without a model: take $\arg\max_aQ(s,a)$. With $V$ you would need $P$ to look one step ahead.

An optimal policy $\pi^*$ is at least as good as every other policy in every state at once. In a finite MDP one exists, and it can be chosen deterministic.

The Bellman expectation equation

$V^\pi(s)=\E_\pi[R_{t+1}+\gamma\,G_{t+1}\mid S_t=s]$
$G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}$
$=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,\E_\pi[G_{t+1}\mid S_{t+1}=s']\big]$
condition on $a$, then $s'$
$=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,V^\pi(s')\big]$
Markov: only $s'$ matters

The value of a state = the expected immediate reward + the discounted value of where you land.

With $P_\pi(s,s')=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)P(s'\mid s,a)$ and $r_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)R(s,a)$ it is a linear system:

$$V^\pi=r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi V^\pi\ \Longrightarrow\ V^\pi=(\mathbf I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1}r_\pi$$

18 equations here: solve them, or iterate $V\leftarrow r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi V$; each sweep shrinks the error by $\gamma$ (lab: 116 sweeps to $10^{-10}$).

Random policy, lab: the exact solve gives $V(S)=-5.911$; the average return of 5,000 simulated episodes is $-5.85\pm0.06$. Random moves next to a cliff end badly.

The Bellman optimality equation

$$V^*(s)=\max_a\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,V^*(s')\big]$$

$Q^*(s,a)=R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')$ and $\pi^*(s)=\arg\max_aQ^*(s,a)$: knowing $Q^*$ is knowing how to act.

By hand, the yellow cell, action up: enter B with 0.8, slip left with 0.1, slip right into the border and stay with 0.1:

$$0.8\cdot10+0.1\cdot0.9\cdot6.354+0.1\cdot0.9\cdot9.420=9.420$$

At S: $Q^*(S,\cdot)$ = up 3.83, left 2.17, right 1.15, down −7.44. Up, the safe way round. The max makes the equation non-linear: we iterate instead of solving.

Policy iteration

Start from any policy and repeat two steps:

1. evaluate: solve the linear system for $V^{\pi_k}$.

2. improve: act greedily,

$$\pi_{k+1}(s)=\arg\max_a\Big[R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V^{\pi_k}(s')\Big]$$

Policy improvement theorem: $V^{\pi_{k+1}}\ge V^{\pi_k}$ in every state. One greedy step then $\pi_k$ is at least as good as $\pi_k$; repeat the argument at every step.

Finitely many deterministic policies and values that never decrease: it stops, at a policy greedy with respect to its own values, which satisfies the optimality equation. Lab: 4 improvements.

Value iteration

$$V_{k+1}(s)=\max_a\Big[R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V_k(s')\Big]$$

The optimality equation used as an update, from $V_0=0$. The policy is read off at the end as the greedy one.

Contraction in one line: $|\max_af(a)-\max_ag(a)|\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|$, so $\|\Tc V-\Tc U\|_\infty\le\gamma\|V-U\|_\infty$. Unique fixed point $V^*$, and $\|V_k-V^*\|_\infty\le\gamma^k\|V_0-V^*\|_\infty$.

Lab: 80 sweeps to $10^{-10}$. The greedy policy is optimal from sweep 8, when the values are still 1.41 off: policies converge long before values. That is why policy iteration needs so few steps.

Discount, noise and rewards change the policy

The optimal policy is not a property of the grid alone. Three knobs:

γ: near or far. Without slip, +1 is worth $\gamma^2$ (3 moves) and +10 is worth $10\gamma^4$ (5 moves). Far wins when $\gamma\gt1/\sqrt{10}=0.316$; the lab's sweep finds the switch at 0.32.

slip: risky or safe. With noise the cliff edge costs too much: 5 moves instead of 3, 7 instead of 5.

reward per move. +1 per move makes staying worth up to $1/(1-\gamma)=10$: the agent never leaves. −2 per move: it rushes to the nearest exit.

You get what you reward, not what you meant. Choosing the reward is part of the problem.

Monte Carlo and temporal difference

No model now, only episodes: estimate $V^\pi$ from experience.

Monte Carlo: wait for the end, move towards the actual return: $V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha\,[G_t-V(S_t)]$.

TD(0): after every step, move towards a one-step guess:

$$V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha\,\delta_t,\quad\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t)$$

$\delta_t$, the TD error: the surprise between two successive predictions. TD bootstraps: it learns a guess from a guess.

The random walk (Sutton and Barto): start at C, left or right with probability ½, reward 1 at the right end, $\gamma=1$. True values 1/6, …, 5/6. In episode 1 only $V(E)$ changes: nothing else was surprising yet.

Monte Carlo vs TD on the random walk

100 episodes, RMS error of the 5 values, mean of 100 runs (lab). TD wins at every step size: after 25 episodes, 0.057 for the best TD against 0.140 for the best MC.

Bias vs variance. MC's target $G_t$ is unbiased but carries all the randomness of the rest of the episode. TD's target $R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})$ is biased while $V$ is wrong, but has the noise of one step only.

TD learns online, before the episode ends, and in tasks that never end. MC needs neither the Markov property nor bootstrapping, which makes it safer with function approximation.

The curves flatten and even rise for large α: a constant step keeps chasing the last episodes. A decreasing α, e.g. $1/N(s)$, converges. In between MC and TD: $n$-step returns and TD(λ).

SARSA and Q-learning

To control without a model, learn $Q(s,a)$ and act ε-greedily: a random action with probability ε, otherwise $\arg\max_aQ(s,a)$. After every step:

$$Q(S_t,A_t)\leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)+\alpha\big[\text{target}-Q(S_t,A_t)\big]$$

SARSA: target $R_{t+1}+\gamma Q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})$, with the action it will really take. It learns the value of the policy it follows, exploration included: on-policy.

Q-learning (Watkins, 1989): target $R_{t+1}+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(S_{t+1},a')$. It learns the value of the greedy policy, whatever it does: off-policy, a sampled value iteration.

Both converge (Q-learning to $Q^*$) if every pair keeps being tried and α decreases suitably. Off-policy also means learning from old data or from other agents: the door to replay buffers and offline RL.

Cliff walking: safe or optimal?

4 × 12 grid, −1 per move; the cliff costs −100 and sends you back to S. ε = 0.1, α = 0.5, 500 episodes, 50 runs (lab).

Q-learning learns the optimal path along the edge, 13 moves, in all 50 runs. But it explores on it, and one random step means a fall: online return −49.7.

SARSA puts its own exploration into its targets: the edge is dangerous for an explorer. It walks along the top (17 moves in most runs): online return −29.2.

Run greedily after training, Q-learning's path is better (−13 against −17). Which is right? If exploration goes on when it matters (a robot that keeps trying random moves), SARSA; if the policy is deployed greedily, Q-learning. With ε → 0 both reach the optimal path.

Deep Q-networks

Too many states for a table (Atari: 84 × 84 pixels): learn $Q(s,a;\mathbf w)$ with a network, one output per action, by minimising the squared TD error. Two stabilisers:

experience replay: store transitions and train on random mini-batches: less correlated data, every transition reused.

target network $\mathbf w^-$: a frozen copy computes the targets, synced every few hundred steps, so the target stops moving with each update.

DQN (Mnih et al., 2015): one network and one set of hyper-parameters for 49 Atari games from pixels; at least 75 % of a professional tester's score on 29 of them.

Lab, gridworld with one-hot states: the greedy policy reaches $V(S)$ = 3.78, 3.83, 3.83 in 3 seeds (optimum 3.832). Without replay and target network: 0.56, 3.66, 3.75.

Deadly triad: function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy learning can make the values diverge. Replay and target networks are the patches; Double DQN and prioritised replay followed.

REINFORCE: the log-derivative trick

Parametrise the policy directly, $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ = a softmax over a network's outputs, and climb $J(\theta)=\E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[R(\tau)]$:

$\nabla_\theta J=\sum_\tau R(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)$
sum over episodes τ
$=\sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)R(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)$
$\nabla p=p\,\nabla\log p$
$=\E_\tau\big[R(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)\big]$
sample it
$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)$
$P(s'\mid s,a)$ has no θ
$$\nabla_\theta J\approx\frac1B\sum_{\text{episodes}}\sum_tG_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)$$

REINFORCE (Williams, 1992). $G_t$ = reward-to-go: an action cannot change earlier rewards. In code, minimise $-\sum_tG_t\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)$: a cross-entropy whose label is the action taken, weighted by what followed.

Baselines and the advantage

Subtract any $b(s)$ that does not depend on the action: the mean is unchanged,

$$\E_{a\sim\pi}\big[b(s)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big]=b(s)\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=b(s)\nabla_\theta1=0$$

but the variance drops. With $b(s)\approx V(s)$ the weight is the advantage $A_t=G_t-V(S_t)$: better or worse than usual?

Lab, 3-armed bandit: 200,000 samples of the estimator agree with autograd, $(-0.061, 0.270, -0.209)$, with or without baseline; the variance per sample drops from 1.63 to 0.92.

CartPole written from its physics equations, policy MLP 4 → 32 → 2, 800 episodes, 5 seeds: last-100-episode length 491 with a learned baseline $V_w(s)$, 433 without, and a much narrower spread.

About 200,000 simulated steps per run, for a task a one-line rule solves: policy gradients are sample-hungry.

Actor–critic and PPO

Actor–critic: an actor $\pi_\theta$ and a critic $V_w$. Replace $G_t$ by the TD estimate of the advantage, $\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V_w(S_{t+1})-V_w(S_t)$: updates every step, lower variance, some bias (A2C).

PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) reuses each batch for several epochs, without letting the policy move too far. With the ratio $r_t=\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)/\pi_{\text{old}}(A_t\mid S_t)$:

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}=\E_t\Big[\min\big(r_tA_t,\ \clip(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t\big)\Big]$$

Once the ratio leaves $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ in the direction the advantage pushes, the objective is flat: no gradient, no incentive to go further. Usual value: ε = 0.2.

Simple and robust, for discrete and continuous actions: the default deep-RL algorithm, and the RL step of RLHF. Its ancestor TRPO used a hard KL constraint instead of the clip.

RLHF: learning from preferences

Reinforcement learning from human feedback, the recipe of InstructGPT and ChatGPT:

1. SFT: fine-tune on demonstrations → $\piref$.

2. Reward model: people compare two answers; fit $\hat r$ with Bradley–Terry, $P(y_w\succ y_l)=\sigma\big(\hat r(y_w)-\hat r(y_l)\big)$.

3. RL: PPO on

$$\max_\pi\ \E_{y\sim\pi}[\hat r(x,y)]-\beta\,\KL(\pi\,\|\,\piref)$$

One episode = one answer; the actions are the tokens; the reward arrives at the end.

DPO (2023) skips the reward model and the RL loop: the optimum of this objective gives $\hat r=\beta\log(\pi/\piref)+\text{const}$, so the policy is fitted to the preferences directly with the Bradley–Terry loss.

Reward hacking and the KL leash

The optimum of the KL-regularised objective has a closed form:

$$\pi^*_\beta(y)=\frac{\piref(y)\,e^{\hat r(y)/\beta}}{Z}$$

Large β: stay near $\piref$. β → 0: all the mass on the argmax of $\hat r$.

Lab toy: 1,000 answers; people like some extra length, not too much ($u$ = quality $+\,0.6\ell-0.3\ell^2$). The linear reward model (71 % held-out accuracy) learned +0.45 per unit of length: true where people looked.

As β falls the proxy rises steadily (to 4.64), the true utility peaks at 2.69 (β = 0.2) and collapses to −1.27: answers of length 4.8, far beyond the data.

Reward hacking: the policy finds the reward model's mistakes. Remedies: the KL leash, fresh comparisons of the new policy's answers, reward-model ensembles, human evaluation.

RL in practice: what to do when…

situationwhat to do
designing the rewardreward the outcome you want, not a behaviour you think leads to it; watch for exploits (a boat-racing agent learned to circle forever hitting the same targets). Potential-based shaping keeps the optimal policy.
interactions are expensiveRL needs millions of steps: build a simulator, learn a model of the dynamics (model-based RL), or reuse logged data.
the simulator is not realityrandomise the simulator (masses, friction, lighting) so the real world looks like one more variation, then fine-tune: sim-to-real.
only logged data (offline RL)the learned policy picks actions the logs rarely contain, whose values are guesses: conservative methods (CQL, IQL), and off-policy evaluation by importance sampling before deploying.
comparing algorithmsmany seeds and confidence intervals: RL results vary a lot from seed to seed. Never conclude from one run.
the action does not change the futureit is a (contextual) bandit, or plain supervised learning if the right answer is known. Much simpler and more data-efficient.
the model of the world is knownplan: value iteration, tree search, optimisation. No trial and error needed.
toolsGymnasium (environments), Stable-Baselines3 and CleanRL (algorithms), TRL (RLHF, DPO for language models).
