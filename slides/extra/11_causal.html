Causal inference and uplift modelling

imanolgranada@gmail.com

Where we are

Every model of the course answers "what will happen?": $P(Y\mid X)$, learned from data and checked on a test set. Business decisions ask "what happens if we act?": call this customer, give this discount, send this claim to a fast-track handler.

The second question is about $P(Y\mid\do(T=t))$, a world we change. A model trained on observational data does not answer it: the customers we acted on were chosen for a reason. Extra 9 said it about explanations: a SHAP value describes the model, not the world.

Causal inference: estimating what would happen under an intervention, from experiments or from observational data plus assumptions. Uplift modelling: estimating it person by person, to decide whom to treat.

Four parts: A prediction vs intervention and potential outcomes, B causal graphs, C estimating effects from observational data, D heterogeneous effects and uplift. It ends with the Extra 11 lab.

Simpson's paradox: kidney stones

A real study (Charig et al., 1986): open surgery (A) against a less invasive procedure (B), 350 patients each.

Large stones are harder to treat, and doctors gave A to the hard cases: 75 % of A's patients had a large stone, 23 % of B's. Stone size is a confounder: it drives both the treatment and the outcome.

"What if everybody got A?" keeps the population's mix of stone sizes $P(z)$ and uses A's success rate within each size:

$$P(Y{=}1\mid\do(T{=}t))=\sum_z P(Y{=}1\mid t,z)\,P(z)$$

$0.931\cdot0.51+0.730\cdot0.49=0.833$ for A, $0.867\cdot0.51+0.688\cdot0.49=0.779$ for B. Same data, opposite conclusion. If the third variable were caused by the treatment, the pooled table would be the right one: only the causal story decides (Part B).

Potential outcomes

Running example: a fictional telematics programme (a box in the car, feedback, a discount). Outcome: next year's claim cost.

Each driver has two potential outcomes: $Y_i(1)$ if he joins, $Y_i(0)$ if not. We observe one (consistency):

$$Y_i=T_i\,Y_i(1)+(1-T_i)\,Y_i(0)$$

Fundamental problem: the effect $\tau_i=Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)$ is never observed. Causal inference is a missing-data problem.

  • $\ATE=\E[Y(1)-Y(0)]$, everybody
  • $\ATT=\E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid T=1]$, the treated
  • $\tau(x)=\E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x]$, the CATE

The lab simulates 5,000 drivers and keeps both outcomes: ATE $=-51.9$ €, ATT $=-37.7$ €. The members are careful drivers, for whom the programme does less.

The naive difference = ATT + selection bias

Members cost 121 € less than non-members. Add and subtract $\E[Y(0)\mid T=1]$, the members' cost without the programme:

$$\underbrace{\E[Y\mid T{=}1]-\E[Y\mid T{=}0]}_{\text{naive}}=\underbrace{\E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid T{=}1]}_{\ATT}$$
$$\qquad+\underbrace{\E[Y(0)\mid T{=}1]-\E[Y(0)\mid T{=}0]}_{\text{selection bias}}$$

Consistency gives $Y=Y(1)$ among the treated and $Y=Y(0)$ among the controls, so it is an identity. Lab: $-120.9=-37.7-83.1$.

The selection bias is the gap had nobody been treated. Careful drivers join and are cheaper anyway: two thirds of the raw difference is not caused by the programme.

More data does not help: the bias does not shrink with $n$. Only the design or assumptions can remove it.

Why the A/B test is the gold standard

If $T$ is a coin flip, it is independent of the potential outcomes, $(Y(1),Y(0))\ind T$. Then

$$\E[Y\mid T{=}1]-\E[Y\mid T{=}0]=\E[Y(1)\mid T{=}1]-\E[Y(0)\mid T{=}0]$$
$$=\E[Y(1)]-\E[Y(0)]=\ATE$$

The selection bias is zero and $\ATT=\ATE$. No model, no assumption about covariates. In the lab, 500 coin-flip replays on the same drivers average $-52.1$ € (true $-51.9$); 500 self-selected replays $-123.8$ €.

When we cannot randomise: the data are historical; an experiment is too slow (claims develop over years), too expensive, unfair (a discount for some customers only) or unethical. Then we replace the coin flip by assumptions.

Four assumptions replace the coin flip

SUTVA

One version of the treatment, and no interference: my outcome does not depend on your treatment. Fails for vaccines, or a discount that spreads by word of mouth.

Consistency

The observed outcome is the potential outcome of the treatment received, $Y=Y(T)$. "Telematics" must mean one well-defined thing.

Ignorability

$(Y(1),Y(0))\ind T\mid X$: among drivers with the same $X$, joining is as good as random. Untestable: it is a claim about what we did not measure.

Positivity (overlap)

$0\lt e(x)\lt 1$ for every $x$, with $e(x)=P(T{=}1\mid X{=}x)$: every kind of driver could be in either group. Testable, and must be checked.

Under them: $\E[Y(t)\mid X]=\E[Y(t)\mid T{=}t,X]=\E[Y\mid T{=}t,X]=\mu_t(X)$. Every estimator of Part C follows from this line.

Causal graphs (DAGs)

An arrow $A\to B$: $A$ is a direct cause of $B$. No cycles. The graph is drawn from domain knowledge, before looking at the outcome. Pearl (2000).

Confounder: a common cause of $T$ and $Y$ (age, past claims, km, urban). Must be adjusted for.

Mediator: on a causal path, $T\to M\to Y$ (the harsh-braking score). Adjusting for it removes part of the effect.

Collider: an effect of two variables, $T\to C\leftarrow Y$ (renewing next year). Adjusting for it, or selecting on it, creates a false association.

This is the lab's simulator, so here it is the truth. In real work it is an assumption, and writing it down is what makes it discussable.

Chain, fork, collider

junctionmarginallygiven $Z$
chain $X\to Z\to Y$dependentblocked
fork $X\leftarrow Z\to Y$dependentblocked
collider $X\to Z\leftarrow Y$independentopened

Every path is built from these three. A path is blocked by a set $S$ if it has a chain or fork whose middle is in $S$, or a collider with neither it nor a descendant in $S$. All paths blocked: $X$ and $Y$ are d-separated, hence independent given $S$.

Lab, all coefficients 1, 5,000 points: correlation 0.578 → 0.012 (chain), 0.500 → −0.001 (fork), −0.014 → −0.516 (collider). Theory: $1/\sqrt3$, $0.5$ and $0\to-0.5$.

Collider bias: selecting creates correlation

Talent and looks are independent. People become actors when talent + looks is large enough. Being an actor is a collider: talent → actor ← looks.

Among actors, a handsome one is probably less talented: the two traits become negatively correlated, $r=-0.64$ in the lab. Nothing links them; the selection does.

Berkson's paradox: two diseases look negatively associated among hospitalised patients, because either one gets you admitted.

In the lab: renewing depends on the programme and on the claim cost. Analysing only the customers who renewed gives $-38.8$ €, while their true effect is $-46.7$ €.

The backdoor criterion

A backdoor path starts with an arrow into $T$: $T\leftarrow\text{age}\to Y$. It carries association that is not causation.

A set $S$ satisfies the backdoor criterion if

  1. no variable in $S$ is a descendant of $T$, and
  2. $S$ blocks every backdoor path.

Then ignorability holds given $S$, and

$$P(Y\mid\do(t))=\sum_s P(Y\mid t,s)\,P(s)$$

The lab's full DAG has 29 paths from T to Y: 2 causal, 21 backdoor. A from-scratch blocking test and NetworkX's d-separation agree: {age, km, claims, urban} is valid; without age, 2 backdoor paths stay open.

The do-operator and bad controls

$\do(T{=}t)$: set $T$ by intervention. In the graph, cut the arrows into $T$; the rest of the mechanism is unchanged. $P(Y\mid\do(t))$ is $Y$'s distribution in that mutilated graph, $P(Y\mid t)$ in the original one.

The backdoor adjustment turns the first into observable quantities. When no valid set exists, other identification rules may (front-door, do-calculus).

Regression adjustment and matching

Regression adjustment (g-computation): from $\E[Y(t)\mid X]=\mu_t(X)$,

$$\widehat{\ATE}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(\hmu_1(x_i)-\hmu_0(x_i)\big)$$

The continuous adjustment formula: predict both outcomes for every unit. Weak point: $\hmu_1$ extrapolates to drivers unlike the members.

Matching: impute the missing outcome with the nearest unit of the other arm (on $X$, or on the propensity score). Easy to explain; noisy, and needs overlap.

Lab (true $-51.9$ €): spline regression per arm $-51.1$ €; 1-NN matching on the propensity $-47.8$ €, one control reused 18 times. A single OLS coefficient for $T$ gives $-33.8$ €: it assumes one effect for all.

The propensity score

$e(x)=P(T{=}1\mid X{=}x)$. Rosenbaum–Rubin (1983): it is a balancing score, $X\ind T\mid e(X)$. So ignorability given $X$ implies ignorability given the single number $e(X)$.

Proof: $P(T{=}1\mid X,e(X))=e(X)$ depends on $X$ only through $e(X)$.

Estimate it (logistic regression, or calibrated boosting), then check two things:

  • overlap: both groups cover the same range of $\he$. Lab: 197 drivers have $\he\lt0.05$ and only 6 of them joined.
  • balance: after matching or weighting, $|\text{SMD}|\lt0.1$ for every covariate. Lab: age $0.80\to-0.01$, claims $-0.45\to0.01$.

The propensity is a tool for balance, not a prediction contest: a model with a higher AUC that uses instruments or post-treatment variables is worse.

Inverse probability weighting

$$\widehat{\ATE}_{\text{IPW}}=\frac1n\sum_i\frac{T_iY_i}{\he(x_i)}-\frac1n\sum_i\frac{(1-T_i)Y_i}{1-\he(x_i)}$$

Unbiased: by consistency $TY=T\,Y(1)$, then ignorability:

$$\E\Big[\frac{TY}{e(X)}\Big]=\E\Big[\frac{\E[T\mid X]\,\E[Y(1)\mid X]}{e(X)}\Big]=\E[Y(1)]$$

A member with $e=0.1$ stands for ten drivers like him, nine of whom did not join. The weighted sample is a pseudo-population, as if randomised. Positivity lets us divide by $e$.

Kidney stones: weighting each patient by $1/P(\text{his treatment}\mid\text{stone size})$ gives exactly 83.3 % and 77.9 %. Telematics lab: $-49.1$ € (true $-51.9$).

Weights in practice: stabilise, check, beware

Hájek / stabilised weights: divide each weighted mean by its sum of weights, equivalently $sw_i=P(T{=}T_i)/P(T{=}T_i\mid x_i)$, mean ≈ 1. Errors in the total weight cancel.

Effective sample size $(\sum w)^2/\sum w^2$. Lab: the 1,847 members are worth 909; the largest weight is 68.7 (a member with $\he=0.015$).

Extreme weights: a unit with $e\approx0$ that was treated anyway dominates the estimate. The stress test repeats the lab 100 times with the selection strength multiplied by $s$.

Clipping or trimming $\he$ (say to $[0.05,0.95]$) lowers the variance but changes the population: lab $-55.1$ € instead of $-49.1$ €. First look at the overlap plot.

Doubly robust: AIPW

$$\widehat{\ATE}=\frac1n\sum_i\Big[\hmu_1-\hmu_0+\frac{T_i(Y_i-\hmu_1)}{\he}-\frac{(1-T_i)(Y_i-\hmu_0)}{1-\he}\Big]$$

Regression adjustment plus weighted residuals. Consistent if either the outcome models or the propensity is right:

  • $\hmu_t=\mu_t$: the residual terms have mean zero, whatever the weights;
  • $\he=e$: the weighted residuals correct the bias of $\hmu$ exactly.

With ML models fit $\hmu$ and $\he$ by cross-fitting: double machine learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018). The bracket terms give a standard error: 4.2 €.

Bootstrap: resample rows, refit everything (propensity included), 500 times, take the 2.5 and 97.5 % quantiles. Lab: the naive interval excludes the truth; the four adjusted ones contain it. AIPW: bootstrap SE 4.31, analytic 4.23.

Refutation and unobserved confounding

Refuters test consequences of the analysis (DoWhy automates them):

  • placebo treatment: permute $T$; lab: 200 estimates, mean $-0.19$ €, sd 3.4, none near $-51.0$;
  • random common cause: add a noise covariate; lab: change $+0.08$ €.

Unobserved confounding is invisible to them. Hide past claims and AIPW says $-80.0$ €, with clean overlap, balance and refuters.

Sensitivity analysis: how strong must a hidden confounder be to explain the effect away? Rosenbaum's $\Gamma$, the E-value, omitted-variable bounds benchmarked on observed covariates, as on the right.

Linear intuition: bias ≈ (effect of $U$ on $Y$) × (imbalance of $U$ between the arms).

Another design: difference-in-differences

No randomisation, no credible list of confounders, but a before and after: the programme is launched in region A in 2025, not in region B.

$$\widehat\tau=(\bar y_{A,\text{after}}-\bar y_{A,\text{before}})-(\bar y_{B,\text{after}}-\bar y_{B,\text{before}})$$

Toy numbers: $(440-470)-(540-520)=-30-20=-50$ €. B's change (+20 €, claims inflation) estimates what would have happened in A without the programme.

Key assumption: parallel trends. Without the programme, A and B would have moved by the same amount. Level differences are allowed; different trends are not.

Check: plot several years before the launch. In regression form, $\widehat\tau$ is the interaction coefficient of $Y\sim\text{group}+\text{period}+\text{group}\times\text{period}$.

Another design: instrumental variables

An unmeasured $U$ (driving style) confounds $T$ and $Y$. An instrument $Z$ shifts $T$ but touches $Y$ only through $T$: a randomised encouragement letter to join.

$$\widehat\tau_{\text{Wald}}=\frac{\E[Y\mid Z{=}1]-\E[Y\mid Z{=}0]}{\E[T\mid Z{=}1]-\E[T\mid Z{=}0]}$$

Toy numbers: the letter raises joining from 25 % to 45 % and lowers the mean cost from 490 to 480 €: $\ -10/0.20=-50$ € per joiner.

Key assumptions: relevance ($Z$ moves $T$), independence ($Z$ as good as random), exclusion ($Z$ affects $Y$ only through $T$: untestable). With monotonicity it estimates the effect on the compliers (LATE).

Weak instruments (a small denominator) give huge, unstable estimates. Two-stage least squares generalises the Wald ratio to covariates.

Uplift: four kinds of customer

An insurer calls a random half of 40,000 renewing customers with a retention offer (an A/B test). Retention: 82.7 % without a call, 88.0 % with one.

Whom to call next year? We need the uplift, the CATE:

$$\tau(x)=P(Y{=}1\mid\do(T{=}1),x)-P(Y{=}1\mid\do(T{=}0),x)$$

The ATE = share of persuadables − share of sleeping dogs: each persuadable adds a stay, each sleeping dog removes one.

A churn model ranks by $P(\text{leave}\mid x)$. In the lab, churn risk and uplift correlate only 0.29: risk-based targeting spends the budget on lost causes.

Meta-learners: CATE from ordinary models

S-learner: one model $\hmu(x,t)$, $\hat\tau(x)=\hmu(x,1)-\hmu(x,0)$. Simple; regularisation may ignore $t$ and shrink the effect to zero.

T-learner: $\hmu_1$ on the treated, $\hmu_0$ on the controls, $\hat\tau=\hmu_1-\hmu_0$. Each sees half the data; the errors add up.

X-learner (Künzel et al., 2019): impute $\tilde D^1_i=Y_i-\hmu_0(x_i)$, $\tilde D^0_i=\hmu_1(x_i)-Y_i$, regress them, combine with $g=\he(x)$. Good with unequal arms.

R- and DR-learners regress an orthogonal pseudo-outcome (residual on residual, or the AIPW terms) with cross-fitting: the workhorses of EconML. Uplift trees split on the difference in response between arms instead of on purity (CausalML, causal forests).

The Qini curve, step by step

Sort a randomised test set by predicted uplift. Among the top $k$: $n_t,n_c$ treated and controls, $r_t,r_c$ of them stayed.

$$Q(k)=r_t(k)-r_c(k)\,\frac{n_t(k)}{n_c(k)}$$

Incremental stays among the treated, controls rescaled to the same size. The uplift curve $U(k)=\big(\tfrac{r_t}{n_t}-\tfrac{r_c}{n_c}\big)k$ counts extra stays if all top $k$ were called.

A random ranking gives the straight line to $Q(n)$. Qini coefficient: the area between the curve and that line ($x$ = fraction called). Here 1.55.

A good curve rises (persuadables), flattens (sure things, lost causes) and falls at the end (sleeping dogs).
