Dimensionality reduction

imanolgranada@gmail.com

Where we are

Data come with many columns: 64 pixels per digit, 4,096 per face photo, thousands of word counts per document. They rarely fill all those dimensions: pixels are correlated, words come in topics, columns repeat each other.

Why reduce: to see the data (a 2-D map), to compress (21 numbers keep 90 % of a digit's variance), to denoise, to make other models faster, to remove collinear features, and because distances misbehave in high dimension.

Dimensionality reduction: a map $\bx_i\in\R^d\mapsto\bz_i\in\R^k$, $k\ll d$, that keeps what matters: variance, distances, classes or neighbourhoods. Which one we keep defines the method.

Four parts: A why reduce, and PCA in depth; B other linear methods (random projections, LDA, LSA, NMF); C manifold learning (kernel PCA, MDS, Isomap); D t-SNE, UMAP and practice. It ends with the Extra 6 lab.

The curse of dimensionality

$n$ random points in the cube $[0,1]^d$. How much farther is the farthest point than the nearest one? In the lab (500 points): 86.5× in 2-D, 3.36× in 10-D, 1.11× in 1000-D.

Why. For uniform $x,y$: $\E[(x-y)^2]=\frac16$, $\mathrm{Var}[(x-y)^2]=\frac{7}{180}$. A squared distance sums $d$ such terms:

$$\frac{\mathrm{std}\,\lVert\bx-\by\rVert^2}{\mathrm{mean}\,\lVert\bx-\by\rVert^2}=\frac{\sqrt{7d/180}}{d/6}=\frac{1.183}{\sqrt d}\to0$$

All distances look alike, so kNN, k-means and RBF kernels lose their contrast. Volume misbehaves too: the unit ball fills 79 % of $[-1,1]^2$ but 0.25 % of $[-1,1]^{10}$.

Real data escape the curse because their coordinates are not independent: they live near a low-dimensional subset. Every method of this deck looks for it.

PCA: two views of the same problem

Centre the data. Project each point on the line of a unit vector $\bw$: coordinate $z_i=\bw^\top\bx_i$, reconstruction $z_i\bw$.

Max variance: keep the spread $\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_iz_i^2=\bw^\top\bS\bw$.
Min error: lose little, $\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i\lVert\bx_i-z_i\bw\rVert^2$.

The residual is orthogonal to $\bw$, so by Pythagoras $\lVert\bx_i\rVert^2=z_i^2+\lVert\bx_i-z_i\bw\rVert^2$. Summing:

$$\underbrace{\mathrm{tr}\,\bS}_{\text{fixed}}=\underbrace{\bw^\top\bS\bw}_{\text{captured}}+\underbrace{\text{error}(\bw)}_{\text{lost}}$$

Whatever raises one term lowers the other: the same $\bw$ solves both. The lab checks it over 720 directions: the sum equals $\mathrm{tr}\,\bS=4.69$ to $10^{-15}$.

Reconstruction is also what a linear autoencoder minimises: it learns the PCA subspace (Extra 3 · Autoencoders).

Maximum variance gives an eigenvector

Maximise $\bw^\top\bS\bw$ subject to $\bw^\top\bw=1$. Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal L=\bw^\top\bS\bw-\lambda(\bw^\top\bw-1)$$
one multiplier for one constraint
$$\nabla_\bw\mathcal L=2\bS\bw-2\lambda\bw=\bzero$$
$\bS$ is symmetric
$$\bS\bw=\lambda\bw$$
stationary points are eigenvectors
$$\bw^\top\bS\bw=\lambda\,\bw^\top\bw=\lambda$$
the variance along $\bw$ is its eigenvalue

So take the largest eigenvalue: $\bv_1$, variance $\lambda_1$. The next direction maximises again among vectors orthogonal to $\bv_1$: that is $\bv_2$, and so on.

With $k$ directions: captured variance $\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k$, reconstruction error $\lambda_{k+1}+\dots+\lambda_d$. In the lab's 2-D cloud, $\lambda_1=4.312$, $\lambda_2=0.380$: the best line keeps 92 %.

Two algorithms: eigenvectors of $\bS$ or the SVD of $\bX_c$

Eigen route: form $\bS$ ($d\times d$), diagonalise $\bS=\bV\bLambda\bV^\top$, sort, keep $\bV_k$, scores $\bZ=\bX_c\bV_k$. Cost $O(nd^2+d^3)$.

SVD route: $\bX_c^\top\bX_c=\bV\bSigma\bU^\top\bU\bSigma\bV^\top=\bV\bSigma^2\bV^\top$, so the right singular vectors are the directions and

$$\lambda_j=\frac{\sigma_j^2}{n-1},\qquad\bZ=\bX_c\bV_k=\bU_k\bSigma_k$$

Prefer the SVD. Forming $\bX^\top\bX$ squares the condition number. Läuchli matrix with $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$: the SVD finds $\sigma_2=10^{-8}$, the eigenvalues of $\bX^\top\bX$ give 0. scikit-learn's PCA uses the SVD (randomized for big data).

Signs are arbitrary: $-\bv$ is as good as $\bv$. On the digits 6 of 10 columns came out flipped between the two routes; after aligning signs they agree with scikit-learn to $10^{-12}$.

Eckart–Young: $\bU_k\bSigma_k\bV_k^\top$ is the best rank-$k$ approximation of $\bX_c$ in squared error, the "reconstruction" view again.

How many components?

Explained variance ratio of component $j$: $\lambda_j/\sum_l\lambda_l$. Plotted against $j$: the scree plot. Ways to choose $k$:

  • a threshold on the cumulative ratio, 90 % or 95 %
  • the elbow, where the curve flattens
  • Kaiser: $\lambda_j\gt1$ on standardised data
  • best: $k$ as a hyper-parameter, by cross-validation

Digits ($d=64$): 90 % needs 21 components, 95 % needs 29. The rest is mostly noise and border pixels that are always 0.

Reconstruction: $\hat\bx=\xbar+z_1\bv_1+\dots+z_k\bv_k$. Its squared error, summed and divided by $n-1$, is exactly $\sum_{j\gt k}\lambda_j$ (lab: 314.7 for $k=10$).

Scale first: standardise or not?

PCA maximises variance, and variance depends on units: metres to millimetres multiplies it by $10^6$.

Wine: 178 wines, 3 cultivars, 13 measurements. Proline has std 314, hue 0.23. Raw PCA: PC1 explains 99.8 % and is just the proline axis. Standardised: 36.2 %, 19.2 %, 11.1 %…; 5-NN on 2 PCs goes from 0.691 to 0.966.

Rule: different units → standardise (PCA on the correlation matrix). Same unit and meaningful scale (pixels, counts) → keep it.

Whitening
$\bZ_w=\bZ\bLambda_k^{-1/2}$ has covariance $\bI$: uncorrelated, unit variance. Rotate back, $\bZ_w\bV_k^\top$: ZCA whitening. Used before ICA and some clustering; it blows small, noisy directions up to the size of the signal.

PCA on images: eigenfaces

400 photos, $64\times64=4{,}096$ pixels, 40 people (Olivetti). A direction $\bv_j$ is itself an image: an eigenface (Turk and Pentland, 1991). Each face = mean + weighted sum of eigenfaces. With $n\lt d$ at most $n-1=399$ directions carry variance; 90 % needs 66.

Denoising. Noise spreads equally over all $d$ directions; the signal lives in the top ones. Projecting on $k$ directions keeps a fraction $k/d$ of the noise. Lab: noisy digits (σ = 4) have MSE 16.0; projected on 18 components, 6.91.

Probabilistic PCA in one slide

A generative version (Tipping and Bishop, 1999): a low-dimensional cause plus noise,

$$\bz\sim\mathcal N(\bzero,\bI_k),\quad\bx=\bW\bz+\bmu+\boldsymbol\varepsilon,\quad\boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(\bzero,\sigma^2\bI_d)$$

so $\bx\sim\mathcal N(\bmu,\ \bW\bW^\top+\sigma^2\bI)$. Maximum likelihood has a closed form:

$$\sigma^2_{\text{ML}}=\frac{1}{d-k}\sum_{j\gt k}\lambda_j,\qquad\bW_{\text{ML}}=\bV_k(\bLambda_k-\sigma^2\bI)^{1/2}\bR$$

The noise is the average discarded variance; $\bW$ spans the PCA subspace ($\bR$ any rotation). As $\sigma^2\to0$ we recover PCA.

What it adds: a likelihood (choose $k$, score outliers), EM with missing values, mixtures of PPCA. With a diagonal noise $\boldsymbol\Psi$ instead of $\sigma^2\bI$ it is factor analysis; with a neural network instead of $\bW$, a VAE (Extra 3).

The limits of PCA

Linear only. PCA finds flat subspaces. Data on a curve or a rolled sheet need $k$ as large as the space the curve spans: an arc in 2-D needs both components. Parts C and D handle curved data.

Outliers. Squared error lets one far point pull the directions. Fixes: robust PCA (low rank + sparse), PCA on ranks, or remove the outliers first.

Scale. Not unit-invariant (wine). Decide on standardisation consciously.

Variance ≠ importance. The direction that separates two classes can have little variance; PCA may throw it away. If labels exist, see LDA.

Random projections and the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma

Multiply by a random $\bR\in\R^{d\times k}$, entries $\mathcal N(0,1/k)$. No fitting, one matrix product.

Why distances survive: for a fixed $\bx$, each coordinate of $\bR^\top\bx$ is $\mathcal N(0,\lVert\bx\rVert^2/k)$, so $\lVert\bR^\top\bx\rVert^2/\lVert\bx\rVert^2\sim\chi^2_k/k$: mean 1, std $\sqrt{2/k}$, whatever $d$.

JL lemma (1984). For any $n$ points and $0\lt\varepsilon\lt1$, if $\ k\ge\dfrac{4\ln n}{\varepsilon^2/2-\varepsilon^3/3}$, every squared distance is kept within a factor $1\pm\varepsilon$.

Lab, 400 faces ($d=4096$): $\varepsilon=0.2$ needs $k\ge1{,}383$; the worst of 79,800 pairs is off by 0.168. At $k=100$ the std is 0.146 (theory 0.141).

Used for fast nearest neighbours (LSH), randomized SVD, sketching. PCA keeps distances better on average but only shrinks them, and needs the data.

LDA: reduce with the labels

Fisher (1936): separate the class means, relative to the spread inside each class.

$$\bS_W=\sum_c\sum_{i\in c}(\bx_i-\bmu_c)(\bx_i-\bmu_c)^\top,\quad\bS_B=\sum_cn_c(\bmu_c-\bmu)(\bmu_c-\bmu)^\top$$
$$J(\bw)=\frac{\bw^\top\bS_B\bw}{\bw^\top\bS_W\bw}\ \Rightarrow\ \bS_B\bw=\lambda\bS_W\bw$$

Same Lagrange story as PCA, with a generalised eigenproblem. $\bS_B$ has rank $\le C-1$: at most $C-1$ directions.

Lab, wine: $J$ = 9.08 along LDA, 4.00 along PC1. Digits in 2-D: 5-NN accuracy 0.624 (LDA) vs 0.512 (PCA).

Supervised: fit it inside the cross-validation folds, never on the test labels.

LSA and NMF

LSA: truncated SVD of word counts

Documents × terms, counts: huge and sparse. Do not centre (it would fill every zero); take $\bX\approx\bU_k\bSigma_k\bV_k^\top$ directly (TruncatedSVD). Terms get vectors $\bV_k\bSigma_k$.

Lab, 8 tiny documents: "car" and "automobile" never share one, cosine 0.000 in counts; both co-occur with "engine" and "road", cosine 0.985 in 2-D LSA. The ancestor of word embeddings.

NMF: non-negative parts

$\bX\approx\bW\bH$ with $\bW,\bH\ge0$ (Lee and Seung, 1999). No subtraction allowed, so the basis images become parts. Lab, 16 components: 15.5 % near-zero pixels against 2.9 % for eigenfaces; error 93.76 vs PCA's optimal 92.15.

The manifold hypothesis

High-dimensional data often lie near a low-dimensional curved surface, a manifold. Photos of one face under different poses: thousands of pixels, a handful of degrees of freedom (angles, light).

On a curved surface the straight-line distance lies. The Swiss roll of the lab: 1,500 points on a rolled-up rectangle. Its two ends are 19.0 apart through the air but 92.6 along the sheet (the geodesic).

PCA can only project the roll flat: the layers land on top of each other, and its best coordinate correlates only 0.21 with the position along the roll.

Two ways out: bend the space so that the data become linear (kernel PCA), or measure distances along the manifold (Isomap) and keep only neighbourhoods (LLE, t-SNE, UMAP).

Kernel PCA

PCA needs only inner products: the scores $\bU\bSigma$ come from the Gram matrix $\bX_c\bX_c^\top=\bU\bSigma^2\bU^\top$. Replace it by a kernel $K_{ij}=k(\bx_i,\bx_j)=\boldsymbol\phi(\bx_i)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\bx_j)$: PCA in a feature space we never compute, the trick of the SVM chapter.

  1. centre in feature space: $\tilde\bK=\bJ\bK\bJ$
  2. eigen: $\tilde\bK=\mathbf A\,\mathrm{diag}(\mu_j)\,\mathbf A^\top$
  3. scores: $z_{ij}=\sqrt{\mu_j}\,a_{ij}$

Lab, two rings, RBF $e^{-\gamma\lVert\bx-\bx'\rVert^2}$: a logistic regression on component 1 scores 0.450 with linear PCA, 1.000 with $\gamma=2$, 0.705 with $\gamma=50$.

An $n\times n$ eigenproblem: $O(n^3)$, a few thousand points (Nyström for more). Linear kernel = PCA, checked in the lab.

Classical MDS: a map from a table of distances

Only distances $D_{ij}$ are known. Find points whose Euclidean distances reproduce them (Torgerson, 1952). Key identity:

$$\bB=-\tfrac12\,\bJ\,\bD^{(2)}\bJ=\bX_c\bX_c^\top,\quad D^{(2)}_{ij}=D_{ij}^2$$

Proof. $D_{ij}^2=\lVert\bx_i\rVert^2+\lVert\bx_j\rVert^2-2\bx_i^\top\bx_j$. $\bJ$ subtracts row and column means: terms in $i$ alone or $j$ alone vanish, the cross term becomes the centred Gram matrix.

Then $\bB=\bU\bLambda\bU^\top$, $\bZ=\bU_k\bLambda_k^{1/2}$. On Euclidean distances classical MDS is PCA (lab: equal to $10^{-12}$).

From 66 great-circle distances (Madrid–Barcelona 505 km) it redraws Spain, up to rotation and mirror: Procrustes disparity 0.0008. Small negative eigenvalues (down to −236) flag non-Euclidean input: a sphere.

Isomap: distances along the manifold

Isomap (Tenenbaum et al., 2000):

  1. $k$-NN graph, edges weighted by Euclidean length (short distances are trustworthy)
  2. geodesic distances = shortest paths (Dijkstra)
  3. classical MDS on them

Lab: the three steps match scikit-learn's Isomap to rounding. Correlation of the best coordinate with the position along the roll: 1.000 for $k$ = 5, 10, 20, then 0.746 at 30 and 0.273 at 40: short circuits.

LLE (Roweis and Saul, 2000) keeps local geometry instead: write $\bx_i\approx\sum_jw_{ij}\bx_j$ from its neighbours, then find low-D points rebuilt by the same weights (bottom eigenvectors of $(\bI-\mathbf W)^\top(\bI-\mathbf W)$). It orders the roll too (0.9999) but bends the rectangle into a wedge.

t-SNE: similarities as probabilities

t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) keeps neighbourhoods, not distances. High-D: a Gaussian around each point,

$$p_{j\mid i}=\frac{e^{-\lVert\bx_i-\bx_j\rVert^2/2\sigma_i^2}}{\sum_{l\ne i}e^{-\lVert\bx_i-\bx_l\rVert^2/2\sigma_i^2}},\qquad p_{ij}=\frac{p_{j\mid i}+p_{i\mid j}}{2n}$$

$\sigma_i$ by binary search so that $\mathrm{Perp}=2^{H(P_i)}$ hits a target (5–50): a smooth "number of neighbours". Dense regions get small $\sigma_i$. Lab, 600 digits at 30: $\sigma_i$ from 7.41 to 13.94.

Low-D: a Student-t (Cauchy) kernel, heavy-tailed:

$$q_{ij}=\frac{(1+\lVert\bz_i-\bz_j\rVert^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{l\ne m}(1+\lVert\bz_l-\bz_m\rVert^2)^{-1}}$$

Then move the $\bz_i$ until $Q$ matches $P$.

The objective, its gradient and the heavy tail

$$\min_{\bz}\ \mathrm{KL}(P\Vert Q)=\sum_{i\ne j}p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}$$
$$\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{KL}}{\partial\bz_i}=4\sum_j(p_{ij}-q_{ij})\,\frac{\bz_i-\bz_j}{1+\lVert\bz_i-\bz_j\rVert^2}$$

Springs: $p_{ij}\gt q_{ij}$ (neighbours too far apart) attracts; $p_{ij}\lt q_{ij}$ repels.

Asymmetry: a large $p$ with a small $q$ (neighbours torn apart) costs a lot; a small $p$ with a large $q$ costs little. Local structure is kept, large distances are free.

Crowding: in 10-D a point has room for 11 equidistant neighbours, in 2-D for 3. The Cauchy tail ($1/r^2$, not $e^{-r^2}$) lets moderately far points move much farther for the same $q$: room is made and clusters separate.

Tricks: early exaggeration ($P\times12$ for 250 steps), momentum, gains. The lab's 50-line version reaches KL 0.473; scikit-learn's exact one 0.468.

t-SNE on the digits: the perplexity

All 1,797 digits, scikit-learn TSNE (Barnes–Hut, $O(n\log n)$ per step, PCA initialisation), a few seconds each.

Share of each point's 10 nearest neighbours (in 64-D) kept in the map: 0.543 at perplexity 5, 0.584 at 30, 0.541 at 100. PCA's map keeps 0.118.

Low perplexity: every point cares about ~5 neighbours, the map shatters into clumps. High: rounder islands, more global arrangement, less local detail.

The KL values (0.944, 0.754, 0.594) are not comparable across perplexities: $P$ itself changes. Try several; no value is "right".

What a t-SNE plot does not show

Sizes lie. Spread ratio 10.5 in the data, 0.89 in the map. Each $\sigma_i$ adapts to its density, so density is normalised away.

Gaps lie. C is 5× farther from B than B from A (4.9 in the data); in the map the ratio is 1.02. Once clusters are apart, nothing pulls on large distances.

Positions are random. Two seeds (random init) on the digits: Procrustes disparity 0.754; the island next to the 0s is the 2s in one map, the 7s in the other.

Read a t-SNE map for who is with whom. Not for sizes, densities, distances between clusters or axes. Check with several perplexities and seeds.

UMAP

McInnes, Healy and Melville (2018). Same spirit as t-SNE, different machinery:

1 · fuzzy kNN graph. $w_{j\mid i}=\exp\!\big(-(d_{ij}-\rho_i)/\sigma_i\big)$ over the $k$ neighbours, $\rho_i$ the distance to the nearest one; symmetrise $w_{ij}=w_{j\mid i}+w_{i\mid j}-w_{j\mid i}w_{i\mid j}$.

2 · layout. Low-D similarity $v_{ij}=(1+a\lVert\bz_i-\bz_j\rVert^{2b})^{-1}$; minimise the cross-entropy

$$\sum_{ij}\Big[w_{ij}\log\frac{w_{ij}}{v_{ij}}+(1-w_{ij})\log\frac{1-w_{ij}}{1-v_{ij}}\Big]$$

The second term repels non-neighbours explicitly, with negative sampling: SGD over edges, no normalisation over all pairs.

vs t-SNE: much faster, scales to millions, keeps more global structure (debated: mostly the initialisation), has transform for new points, any $k$. Library umap-learn; not used in the lab.

Measuring an embedding, and three pitfalls

kNN preservation: the share of each point's $k$ nearest neighbours that stay its neighbours in the map,

$$Q_k=\frac{1}{nk}\sum_i\big|\mathcal N_k^{\text{high}}(i)\cap\mathcal N_k^{\text{low}}(i)\big|$$

1 = perfect; a random map scores $\approx k/n$. Related: trustworthiness. For global shape: correlation of all pairwise distances.

No single winner: on the digits t-SNE keeps neighbours (0.584) but PCA the global layout; on the roll Isomap keeps both.

1 · Fit on train only. Pure noise, 2,000 features, 60 examples: selecting 20 features on all data gives a CV accuracy of 0.95; inside a pipeline, 0.50. PCA leaks far less (0.895 vs 0.896), but the rule is the same.

2 · Scale first when units differ. 3 · PCA to ~50 dims before t-SNE on very wide data: less noise, faster.

The topic in one slide

A · Why reduce, and PCA

  • distances concentrate: relative spread $1.183/\sqrt d$
  • max variance = min error; $\bS\bw=\lambda\bw$; error $=\sum_{j\gt k}\lambda_j$
  • SVD: $\lambda_j=\sigma_j^2/(n-1)$, $\bZ=\bU_k\bSigma_k$; standardise if units differ

B · Other linear methods

  • random projection: ratio $\sim\chi^2_k/k$; JL $k\ge4\ln n/(\varepsilon^2/2-\varepsilon^3/3)$
  • LDA: $\bS_B\bw=\lambda\bS_W\bw$, at most $C-1$ dims, supervised
  • LSA: uncentred truncated SVD; NMF: $\bX\approx\bW\bH\ge0$, parts

C · Manifold learning

  • kernel PCA: eigenvectors of $\bJ\bK\bJ$, scores $\sqrt{\mu_j}\mathbf a_j$
  • classical MDS: $-\frac12\bJ\bD^{(2)}\bJ=\bX_c\bX_c^\top$, = PCA
  • Isomap: kNN graph + shortest paths + MDS; beware short circuits

D · t-SNE, UMAP, practice

  • Gaussian $p_{j\mid i}$ (perplexity), Student-t $q_{ij}$, $\min\mathrm{KL}(P\Vert Q)$
  • sizes, gaps, positions in the map mean nothing; check $Q_k$
  • fit every reduction inside the CV; scale first; PCA before t-SNE
