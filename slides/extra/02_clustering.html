Clustering

imanolgranada@gmail.com

Where we are

Every model of the course learned from pairs $(\bx_i, y_i)$: a price, a class, the next word. The loss compared the prediction with $y$.

Most data come without $y$: millions of policyholders, thousands of claim descriptions, the pixels of a photo. Unsupervised learning looks for structure in $\bX$ alone: groups, directions, densities, outliers.

Clustering: split the points into $k$ groups so that points in the same group are similar and points in different groups are not. No label says what "right" is.

Five parts: A what a cluster is and why the distance matters, B k-means, C Gaussian mixtures and EM, D hierarchical, density and spectral clustering, E evaluation and practice. It ends with the Extra 2 lab.

What clustering is for

Segmentation

Groups of customers or policyholders with similar age, premium, claims and region: one price, offer or retention plan per group.

Document topics

Cluster the word vectors (or embeddings) of claim descriptions or complaints: glass, theft, water damage, without reading them all.

Colour quantisation

Each pixel is a point in RGB space. With $k = 16$ clusters a photo needs 4 bits per pixel instead of 24 (Part B).

Faster labelling

Cluster first, then label a few points per cluster (or the clusters themselves) instead of every point.

Anomalies

Points far from every cluster, or flagged as noise by a density method, deserve a look: an odd claim, a broken sensor.

Exploring embeddings

Images, texts or cells mapped to vectors by a network; clusters reveal the kinds of objects in a collection.

What is a cluster?

Compact

Points close to a centre. k-means, Gaussian mixtures, Ward linkage.

Connected

Points linked by a chain of close neighbours, whatever the shape. Single linkage, spectral clustering.

Dense

Regions of high density separated by low density; isolated points are noise. DBSCAN, HDBSCAN.

An algorithm = a definition of "cluster" + a search procedure. No label tells us which definition is right: it depends on what the groups are for. Each definition fails where another one works (Part E puts them side by side).

The distance decides, and so do the units

Most methods compare points with the Euclidean distance, which adds the squared differences of the features in their own units:

$$\norm{\bx - \bx'} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_f (x_f - x'_f)^2}$$

300 policyholders: young (about 27) and seniors (about 60), with premiums from 300 to 1,500 € in both groups. k-means with $k = 2$ (Part B):

Policyholder a (25 y, 800 €): in euros b (62 y, 820 €) is at 42.1 and c (26 y, 1,100 €) at 300.0, so the senior is the "neighbour". Standardised: 2.19 and 0.88.

Standardise by default (each column minus its mean, over its standard deviation), or weight features by what matters. It is a choice: standardising also gives a pure-noise column full weight.

The k-means objective

Find $k$ centroids $\bmu_j$ and a cluster $c_i$ for every point that minimise the within-cluster sum of squares:

$$J=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\bx_i-\bmu_{c_i}}^2$$

Finding the global minimum is NP-hard. Two sub-problems are easy:

1 · Centroids fixed: each point goes to its nearest centroid. $J$ is a sum of one term per point, and the term of point $i$ depends only on $c_i$.

2 · Clusters fixed: the best centroid is the mean $\bar\bx_j$ of the cluster. Add and subtract $\bar\bx_j$; the cross term vanishes because deviations from a mean sum to zero:

$$\sum_{i\in C_j}\norm{\bx_i-\bmu}^2=\sum_{i\in C_j}\norm{\bx_i-\bar\bx_j}^2+n_j\norm{\bar\bx_j-\bmu}^2$$

The first term does not depend on $\bmu$; the second is zero only at $\bmu=\bar\bx_j$. Hence the name: k means.

Lloyd's algorithm with numbers

Lloyd: start from $k$ centroids; repeat assign (fact 1) and update (fact 2) until the assignment stops changing.

Six points, $k=2$, a poor start: $\bmu_1=(1,1)$, $\bmu_2=(2,1)$, both on the left.

p1, p3 → $\bmu_1$; p2, p4, p5, p6 → $\bmu_2$.

$J = 0 + 0 + 1 + 18 + 32 + 34 = 85$

$\bmu_1=(1, 1.5)$, $\bmu_2=(4.5, 4)$, the means of their points.

$J = 85 \to 23.5$

p2 $= (2, 1)$: squared distance $1.25$ to $\bmu_1$, $15.25$ to $\bmu_2$. It changes cluster.

$J = 23.5 \to 9.5$

$\bmu_1=(1.333, 1.333)$, $\bmu_2=(5.333, 5)$.

$J = 9.5 \to 4$

No point changes cluster: stop. $\ 85 \to 23.5 \to 9.5 \to 4$.

Why it stops. Each step minimises $J$ over one block with the other fixed, so $J$ never increases. There are at most $k^n$ assignments and none can repeat while $J$ strictly falls: it stops after finitely many steps.

It stops at a fixed point: a local minimum, not necessarily the global one. Cost $O(nkd)$ per iteration.

Voronoi cells, and starts that go wrong

The Voronoi cell of a centroid is the set of points of the plane closer to it than to any other. Between two centroids the border is the perpendicular bisector; the cells are convex polygons.

k-means can only return convex cells. That is its strength (simple, fast, easy to read) and its main limit.

Local minima. If two centroids start in the same blob, no single step can move one of them across: assign and update both keep $J$ from rising, and $J$ would have to rise first.

In the lab, on 9 blobs, Lloyd from 9 random points reaches the best $J$ in only 31.5 % of 200 runs.

Fix: several runs, keep the lowest $J$ (n_init), and better starts: next slide.

k-means++: spread out the starts

Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007):

  1. first centre: a data point at random;
  2. $D(\bx)$ = distance to the nearest centre chosen so far;
  3. next centre: point $\bx$ with probability
$$P(\bx)=\frac{D(\bx)^2}{\sum_{\bx'}D(\bx')^2}$$

until $k$ centres, then Lloyd. Expected start $J \le 8(\ln k+2)\,J_{\text{opt}}$.

Lab, 9 blobs, 200 runs each: best $J$ in 57.0 % of the runs (random starts: 31.5 %); the start alone is 3.1 times the best $J$ (random: 6.6). With n_init=10: 99.98 %.

Choosing $k$: the elbow and the silhouette

$J$ cannot choose $k$: the best $J$ always decreases with $k$, down to $J=0$ when every point is its own cluster.

The elbow: plot the best $J$ against $k$ and take the $k$ after which it flattens.

The silhouette (Rousseeuw, 1987) scores each point between $-1$ and $1$; the mean over all points scores the clustering (next slide). Take the $k$ with the highest mean.

On the lab's four blobs both agree: $J$ = 979, 434, 184, 164 for $k$ = 2…5, silhouette 0.560, 0.610, 0.680, 0.572.

On real data the elbow is often vague, and both criteria favour round clusters. Use them with stability (Part E) and with what the clusters are for.

The silhouette with numbers

For point $i$ in cluster $C$:

$a(i)$ = mean distance to the other points of $C$;

$b(i)$ = the smallest, over the other clusters $C'$, of the mean distance to the points of $C'$;

$$s(i)=\frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max\big(a(i),\,b(i)\big)}$$

$s\approx1$: deep inside its cluster. $s\approx0$: on a border. $s\lt0$: probably in the wrong cluster. $s=0$ by convention for a point alone in its cluster.

It needs all pairwise distances, $O(n^2d)$: on large data, compute it on a sample. The lab's version matches silhouette_samples exactly.

What k-means assumes

Every cluster is a convex cell, and the border between two clusters lies halfway between their centroids, whatever the spreads. k-means assumes clusters that are convex, round (the same spread in every direction) and of similar spread.

When that fails it still returns $k$ clusters, just the wrong ones (ARI in each panel, 1 = perfect). Fixes: full covariances (Part C), density or graph methods (Part D), or a better representation of the data first.

k-means at scale: mini-batches and colour quantisation

Colour quantisation. Each pixel is a point in RGB space. k-means on the pixels, then every pixel is replaced by its centroid: $\log_2 k$ bits per pixel instead of 24, plus a palette of $k$ colours.

Mini-batch k-means (Sculley, 2010). One Lloyd pass reads all $n$ points. Instead, take a random batch, assign it, and move each centroid towards its batch points with its own learning rate $1/N_j$:

$$N_j\leftarrow N_j+1,\qquad \bmu_j\leftarrow\bmu_j+\tfrac{1}{N_j}(\bx-\bmu_j)$$

So $\bmu_j$ is the running mean of every point it ever received. Lab, 200,000 points in 10-D: 100 batches of 1,024 points (half a pass) end 0.02 % above the $J$ of full Lloyd.

Soft clusters: a generative story

The story: pick a component $j$ with probability $\pi_j$, then draw $\bx\sim\Nc(\bmu_j,\bSigma_j)$. The density of a point:

$$p(\bx)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi_j\,\Nc(\bx\mid\bmu_j,\bSigma_j)$$

The cluster of a point is the hidden component that produced it. Bayes' rule gives its responsibility:

$$\gamma_{ij}=\frac{\pi_j\,\Nc(\bx_i\mid\bmu_j,\bSigma_j)}{\sum_l\pi_l\,\Nc(\bx_i\mid\bmu_l,\bSigma_l)}$$

A soft clustering: $\gamma_{i1}=0.693$ says "probably 1, maybe 2". Here $\pi=(0.6,0.4)$, $\mu=(0,3)$, $\sigma=(1,0.8)$.

The log-likelihood $\ell=\sum_i\log\sum_j\pi_j\Nc(\bx_i\mid\bmu_j,\bSigma_j)$ is a log of a sum: no closed-form maximum.

EM: expectation, then maximisation

E-step: the responsibilities $\gamma_{ij}$ with the current parameters (previous slide).

M-step: with $N_j=\sum_i\gamma_{ij}$, weighted versions of the usual estimates:

$$\pi_j=\frac{N_j}{n},\qquad \bmu_j=\frac{1}{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}\,\bx_i$$
$$\bSigma_j=\frac{1}{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}\,(\bx_i-\bmu_j)(\bx_i-\bmu_j)^\top$$

They maximise $\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}[\log\pi_j+\log\Nc(\bx_i\mid\bmu_j,\bSigma_j)]$: set the gradient to zero (a Lagrange multiplier for $\sum_j\pi_j=1$).

Lab: 300 points from three Gaussians (weights 0.5, 0.33, 0.17). Point colour = mix of the component colours by $\gamma$. scikit-learn's GaussianMixture from the same start gives the same log-likelihood, $-3.2818$ per point.

Why the likelihood never decreases

For any probabilities $q_{ij}$ over the components of point $i$, Jensen's inequality ($\log$ is concave):

$$\log p(\bx_i)=\log\sum_jq_{ij}\frac{\pi_j\Nc_{ij}}{q_{ij}}\ \ge\ \sum_jq_{ij}\log\frac{\pi_j\Nc_{ij}}{q_{ij}}$$

Summed over $i$: $\ell(\theta)\ge B(q,\theta)$, a lower bound, with equality when $q_{ij}=\gamma_{ij}$.

E-step: $q=\gamma(\theta_{\text{old}})$, so the bound touches: $B(q,\theta_{\text{old}})=\ell(\theta_{\text{old}})$.

M-step: maximise the bound, easy because it has no log of a sum.

$$\ell(\theta_{\text{new}})\ge B(q,\theta_{\text{new}})\ge B(q,\theta_{\text{old}})=\ell(\theta_{\text{old}})\ \blacksquare$$

So EM climbs, to a local maximum. Lab: 6 of 8 random starts reach $-984.54$, two stop at $-1032.66$ and $-1087.32$. Use n_init or a k-means start.

k-means is the hard limit of EM

Fix $\pi_j=1/k$ and $\bSigma_j=\sigma^2\mathbf I$ for every component. The responsibilities become a softmax of minus the squared distances:

$$\gamma_{ij}=\frac{e^{-\norm{\bx_i-\bmu_j}^2/2\sigma^2}}{\sum_le^{-\norm{\bx_i-\bmu_l}^2/2\sigma^2}}$$

As $\sigma\to0$ the softmax becomes an arg max: $\gamma_{ij}\to1$ for the nearest centre. The E-step is the k-means assignment, the M-step for the means is the k-means update.

k-means = EM for equal, round, infinitely narrow Gaussians. That is why it wants round clusters of similar spread, and why a GMM with free $\bSigma_j$ can do better.

Lab: mean max responsibility 0.250, 0.855, 0.982, 1.000, 1.000, 1.000 for $\sigma$ = 3, 1.5, 1, 0.5, 0.2, 0.05; at 0.5 the centres agree with k-means to 0.002.

Covariance types, and choosing $k$ with BIC

A full $\bSigma_j$ has $d(d+1)/2$ numbers: 2,080 in 64 dimensions. Cheaper options, and the free parameters for $k$ components:

typecovariance$p$ ($k=3$, $d=2$)
spherical$\sigma_j^2\mathbf I$11
diagdiagonal $\bSigma_j$14
tiedone shared $\bSigma$11
fullany $\bSigma_j$17

The likelihood always grows with $k$. Penalise the parameters, lower is better:

$$\text{BIC}=-2\ell+p\ln n,\qquad \text{AIC}=-2\ell+2p$$

Lab: full covariances with $k=3$ win (BIC 2066). The restricted types need 5 or 6 components to fake the tilted ellipses.

Agglomerative clustering: four linkages

Start with $n$ singletons; merge the two closest clusters; repeat until one is left. The linkage is the distance between two clusters $A$, $B$:

linkagedistance
single$\min_{a,b}d(a,b)$, the closest pair
complete$\max_{a,b}d(a,b)$, the farthest pair
averagethe mean of all $d(a,b)$
Ward$\Delta J=\frac{n_An_B}{n_A+n_B}\norm{\bmu_A-\bmu_B}^2$

Ward merges the pair that increases $J$ the least: a greedy k-means.

The same six numbers, three different two-cluster answers. The tree is built once; every $k$ comes from it. Cost: all pairwise distances, $O(n^2)$ memory.

The dendrogram, and where to cut it

Each merge is drawn at its height. Cutting the tree at height $h$ keeps the clusters that exist below $h$: one run gives every $k$.

A long vertical gap means clusters that stay apart over a wide range of scales: a natural place to cut. Here the last merges are at 5.37, then 13.72 and 14.65.

Lab: cutting at $h=6$ gives three clusters, the same answer as AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=3) (ARI 1), and ARI 0.898 with the three generating blobs: two or three points in the overlap change side.

scipy's Ward heights are $\sqrt{2\Delta J}$; the lab checks it on the six points of the previous slide.

Single linkage chains; Ward does not

Single linkage merges two clusters as soon as one pair of their points is close. A chain of close points glues everything together: the chaining effect.

The same property follows each crescent: single linkage is good at shapes and bad at noise. Ward is the safe default for compact clusters.

Agglomerative clustering accepts any distance matrix (Ward needs Euclidean): useful for mixed data (Part E).

DBSCAN: core, border and noise

Ester et al. (1996). Two parameters, a radius $\varepsilon$ and min_samples (4 here). With $N_\varepsilon(\bx)$ the points within $\varepsilon$ of $\bx$, itself included:

  • core: $|N_\varepsilon(\bx)|\ge$ min_samples
  • border: not core, within $\varepsilon$ of a core point
  • noise: neither (label $-1$)

Clusters = core points chained within $\varepsilon$, plus their border points: a breadth-first search. Any shape, any number of clusters, no $k$.

Choosing $\varepsilon$: sort every point's distance to its $(\text{min\_samples}-1)$-th neighbour and take the knee of the curve.

Lab: on 330 points (moons + 30 uniform), $\varepsilon=0.15$, min_samples 5: 299 core, 9 border, 22 noise points, identical to scikit-learn's DBSCAN.

HDBSCAN: one density for each cluster

One $\varepsilon$ is one density threshold for the whole dataset. Small: the wide cluster dissolves into noise. Large: the tight clusters merge.

HDBSCAN (Campello et al., 2013) runs DBSCAN for every $\varepsilon$. Core distance $\text{core}(\bx)$ = distance to the min_samples-th neighbour; mutual reachability $d_{\text{mr}}(a,b)=\max(\text{core}(a),\text{core}(b),d(a,b))$.

Single linkage on $d_{\text{mr}}$ gives a tree whose cut at $\varepsilon$ is DBSCAN. A split counts only if both sides have min_cluster_size points; the clusters that persist longest are kept, each at its own density.

Lab: the best single $\varepsilon$ (chosen with the labels!) reaches ARI 0.762; HDBSCAN, with only min_cluster_size=15, 0.943. In scikit-learn since 1.3.

Spectral clustering: cluster the graph

  1. Similarity graph: $m$ nearest neighbours (symmetrised), or weights $A_{ij}=e^{-\norm{\bx_i-\bx_j}^2/2\sigma^2}$.
  2. Laplacian $\bL=\bD-\bA$; its $k$ eigenvectors with the smallest eigenvalues, as columns of $\bU$ ($n\times k$).
  3. k-means on the rows of $\bU$.

From Extra 1: $\bff^\top\bL\bff=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}A_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2$ is small for signals that are smooth over the graph. The Fiedler vector split the karate club; here it splits the moons.

A crescent is not compact, but it is well connected through its neighbours, and that is what the graph sees.

Lab: $\lambda_1=0.0080$, then a gap to $\lambda_2=0.0407$.

Why the eigenvectors find the clusters

$k$ components ⇒ eigenvalue 0, $k$ times. $\bff^\top\bL\bff=0$ forces $f_i=f_j$ on every edge, so $\bff$ is constant on each connected component. The eigenvectors of 0 are combinations of the $k$ indicator vectors of the components.

In the embedding (rows of $\bU$), all points of a component land on one spot: k-means cannot fail.

Almost disconnected (a few weak edges): $k$ eigenvalues near 0, then a gap; the eigenvectors are almost piecewise constant. The eigengap hints at $k$.

Two clusters: the Fiedler vector is the relaxed solution of the minimum cut (few edges across, no tiny pieces cut off).

Ng, Jordan and Weiss (2002) use $\bL_{\text{sym}}=\bI-\bD^{-1/2}\bA\bD^{-1/2}$ and rows of length 1. Scales to tens of thousands of points with a sparse graph.

Internal indices, and stability

Davies–Bouldin (low is good): each cluster against its most similar one, $\frac1k\sum_j\max_{l\ne j}\frac{s_j+s_l}{\norm{\bmu_j-\bmu_l}}$. Calinski–Harabasz (high): between- over within-cluster dispersion, an $F$ statistic.

All three prefer k-means' wrong cut of the moons to the true moons: they measure compactness. They compare clusterings of the same kind, not definitions of "cluster".

Stability: a real structure comes back when the data change a little. Cluster two random 80 % subsamples, compare them on the shared points with ARI, repeat.

Lab, four blobs, 20 pairs per $k$: $k=4$ is the most stable (0.981); $k=5$ splits a blob in a different place each time (0.851). Stable does not mean right: k-means on the moons is stable and wrong.

External indices: when labels exist

Cluster numbers are arbitrary names: plain accuracy is meaningless.

Matched accuracy: from the contingency table $n_{ab}$, find the best one-to-one matching of clusters to classes (Hungarian algorithm, linear_sum_assignment), then count.

ARI: over all $\binom n2$ pairs, the fraction on which both labellings agree (together / apart), corrected for chance: 1 = identical, about 0 = random (lab: 0.0001 over 200 random labellings of 1,000 points).

NMI: mutual information over the mean entropy; 1 = identical, 0 = independent. Not chance-corrected.

ARI is the honest default. The lab computes all three from scratch and matches scikit-learn.

No free lunch: every algorithm on every dataset

No column wins every row. Shapes: single linkage, density, spectral. Ellipses: GMM. Blobs: k-means, Ward. On uniform noise, a fixed $k$ always returns $k$ convincing clusters; DBSCAN and HDBSCAN say "one".

Mixed data: the Gower distance

Numbers and categories in one table: average one dissimilarity per feature, each in $[0,1]$:

$$d_{\text{Gower}}=\frac1p\sum_f\delta_f,\quad \delta_f=\begin{cases}|x_f-x'_f|/R_f&\text{numeric, range }R_f\\ \mathbb 1[x_f\ne x'_f]&\text{categorical}\end{cases}$$

k-medoids: k-means whose centres must be data points, so it works with any distance. Assign to the nearest medoid; make the medoid the member with the smallest total distance to its cluster.

Lab, 300 synthetic policyholders, 3 hidden segments: Gower + k-medoids ARI 0.735; one-hot + standardised + k-means 0.839; numbers only 0.605. Gower gives a category mismatch the weight of a whole numeric range: the weights are a choice.
