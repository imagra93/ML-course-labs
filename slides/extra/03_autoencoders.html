Autoencoders and variational autoencoders

imanolgranada@gmail.com

Where we are

The whole course trained networks on pairs $(\bx, y)$. Most data has no labels. An autoencoder uses the input as its own target: squeeze $\bx$ into a short code $\bz$, rebuild it, and learn from the reconstruction error.

To rebuild 784 pixels from a few numbers, the code must keep what matters. That code compresses, denoises, feeds clustering and search, and flags inputs that cannot be rebuilt (anomalies).

Variational autoencoder (VAE): an autoencoder whose code is a random variable with a known prior $\Nc(\mathbf 0,\bI)$, trained on a lower bound of the log-likelihood. It can generate new data.

Four parts: A autoencoders and PCA, B variants and uses, C the VAE (KL, ELBO, reparameterisation), D β, failures and practice. It ends with the Extra 3 lab.

The autoencoder: squeeze, then rebuild

Encoder $\bz=f_\phi(\bx)\in\R^k$, decoder $\hx=g_\theta(\bz)\in\R^D$, trained together so that $\hx\approx\bx$. No labels: the input is the target.

$$\min_{\phi,\theta}\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell\big(\bx_i,\ g_\theta(f_\phi(\bx_i))\big)$$

Undercomplete, $k\lt D$: the code is a bottleneck. To rebuild 784 pixels from 8 numbers it must keep which digit, the slant, the stroke width, and drop the rest.

Overcomplete, $k\ge D$: nothing forces compression; the network can learn to copy. Two slides ahead.

Lab: MNIST (60,000 training digits), an MLP 784 → 256 → 64 → $k$ and back, sigmoid output, squared error. 435,858 weights for $k=2$.

The reconstruction loss is a likelihood

As everywhere in the course, a loss is a negative log-likelihood. Read $\hx$ as the parameters of $p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)$:

Gaussian pixels, $\Nc(\bx;\hx,\sigma^2\bI)$:

$$-\log p=\frac{\lVert\bx-\hx\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}+\frac D2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\ \Rightarrow\ \text{squared error}$$

Bernoulli pixels, black with probability $\hat x_j$:

$$-\log p=-\sum_j\big[x_j\log\hat x_j+(1-x_j)\log(1-\hat x_j)\big]\ \Rightarrow\ \text{BCE}$$

MSE assumes Gaussian noise of one size on every pixel; BCE assumes black-or-white pixels and punishes a confident wrong pixel far harder.

Lab, one digit: $-\log\Nc=-719.18$ = the formula, $-\log\mathrm{Bern}=102.12$ = the BCE. With grey targets the BCE is minimal at $\hx=\bx$ but not 0 (40.97 nats for a test digit).

Overcomplete codes: the identity trap

With $k=2D$ and a ReLU, $\bz=(\mathrm{ReLU}(\bx),\mathrm{ReLU}(-\bx))$ and $\hx=\mathrm{ReLU}(\bx)-\mathrm{ReLU}(-\bx)=\bx$: zero error on every input, digits or noise. A perfect loss, nothing learned. The lab builds it: max error 0.

A trained 784 → 1024 → 784 network: test MSE 0.0153, but 912 of its 1024 units fire for every digit. A dense copy, not a summary.

Sparse autoencoder: add $\lambda\lVert\bz\rVert_1$, or $\sum_j\KL(\rho\,\|\,\hat\rho_j)$ with a small target firing rate $\rho$. With $\lambda=3\cdot10^{-4}$: 49 active units (4.8 %), MSE 0.0231. Units become reusable parts: strokes, loops.

Other ways to rule out the identity: a bottleneck ($k\lt D$), noise on the input (denoising, Part B), a penalty on the encoder's derivatives (contractive autoencoder).

PCA: the linear autoencoder, with numbers

Encode with a unit vector $\bw$: $z=(\bx-\bmu)^\top\bw$; decode $\hx=\bmu+z\,\bw$. By Pythagoras, averaged over the data:

$$\underbrace{\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\lVert\bx_i-\hx_i\rVert^2}_{\text{error}}=\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}\bC}_{\text{total}}-\underbrace{\bw^\top\bC\bw}_{\text{variance kept}},\quad \bC=\tfrac1n\bX_c^\top\bX_c$$

Maximise $\bw^\top\bC\bw$ with $\lVert\bw\rVert=1$: the Lagrangian gives $\bC\bw=\lambda\bw$. An eigenvector, and it keeps $\lambda$ of the variance.

Here $\bmu=(3,4)$, $\bC=\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda=5$ and $1$, $\bv_1=(1,1)/\sqrt2$. One number per point keeps $5/6=83\,\%$; the error is $\lambda_2=1$.

With $k$ components: the top-$k$ eigenvectors $\bV_k$, codes $\bz=\bV_k^\top(\bx-\bmu)$, error $=\sum_{j\gt k}\lambda_j$.

PCA on MNIST, with the SVD

SVD of the centred data, $\bX_c=\bU\,\mathrm{diag}(s_j)\,\bV^\top$:

$$\bC=\tfrac1n\bX_c^\top\bX_c=\bV\,\mathrm{diag}\big(s_j^2/n\big)\,\bV^\top$$

The right singular vectors are the principal components, $\lambda_j=s_j^2/n$. Codes $\bZ=\bX_c\bV_k$, reconstructions $\mathbf 1\bmu^\top+\bZ\bV_k^\top$.

MNIST: 2 components keep 16.8 % of the variance, 16 keep 59.4 %; 90 % needs 87 of the 784.

Lab: with $k=16$ the training error is 21.3976 per image, exactly the sum of the discarded eigenvalues; scikit-learn finds the same components.

The components are global patterns of + and − pixels: a digit is the mean image plus a weighted sum of them.

The linear autoencoder finds the PCA subspace

No activations, no biases, centred data: $\hx=\bx\We\Wd$ with $\We\in\R^{D\times k}$, $\Wd\in\R^{k\times D}$.

Result (Bourlard & Kamp 1988; Baldi & Hornik 1989): at the minimum, $\We\Wd$ is the orthogonal projection on the top-$k$ principal components. Every other critical point is a saddle.

Why. For a fixed decoder, the best encoder projects on its row space (least squares). The loss is then the distance to a $k$-dimensional subspace, minimised by the PCs.

Not unique: $(\We\mathbf A,\ \mathbf A^{-1}\Wd)$ gives the same product. The AE finds the subspace, not the PCs: its directions are not orthogonal, not unit, not sorted.

Lab, $k=16$, Adam, 10 epochs, 7 s: largest principal angle 0.56°, loss 0.0004 above the optimum 21.3976. Yet each decoder direction matches its closest PC with |cos| 0.39 to 0.64 only.

Non-linear beats linear at the same code size

PCA fits a flat subspace. Digits lie near a curved, low-dimensional surface (the manifold hypothesis): a thicker or more slanted 1 is not a straight line in pixel space.

A 2-D code: PCA vs autoencoder

Colour = the true digit, which training never saw. PCA's two directions mix all digits; the autoencoder separates most of them. 15-nearest-neighbour accuracy from the code: PCA 45.0 %, AE 73.8 %.

Codes like these feed clustering (Extra 2) and similarity search. But look at the AE's scale and shape: clusters spread over tens of units, with empty space between. Part C shows why that matters.

Going back up: the transposed convolution

A conv encoder halves the resolution with strided convolutions, $28\to14\to7$. The decoder must go back up.

Transposed convolution: every input pixel paints a copy of the kernel, scaled by its value, moving $s$ pixels at a time; overlaps add.

$$\text{size}=(i-1)s-2p+k+\text{out\_pad}:\ (7-1)\cdot2-2+3+1=14$$

Why "transposed": a convolution is $\by=\mathbf K\bx$; this computes $\mathbf K^\top\by$, which is also how backprop computes a convolution's gradient. Lab: $\langle\mathrm{conv}(u),x\rangle=\langle u,\mathrm{conv}^\top(x)\rangle=5.0067$.

Uneven overlap (×1, ×2, ×4) leaves checkerboard artefacts in generated images. Fix: upsample (nearest or bilinear), then an ordinary convolution.

Denoising autoencoder

$$\tilde\bx=\mathrm{clip}(\bx+\sigma\boldsymbol\varepsilon,0,1),\qquad\min\ \big\lVert g_\theta(f_\phi(\tilde\bx))-\bx\big\rVert^2$$

Noisy input, clean target (Vincent et al., 2008). Copying is now useless, so even a large code must learn what digits look like. With MSE the best output is $\E[\bx\mid\tilde\bx]$.

Lab: two identical conv autoencoders (code 32, 4 epochs). On noisy digits, $\sigma=0.5$: input 0.116, plain AE 0.111, denoising AE 0.015.

The plain AE never saw noise: at $\sigma=0.2$ its output (0.056) is worse than the noisy input itself (0.021). Hiding pixels instead of adding noise is the idea behind BERT and masked image models.

What autoencoders are used for

Denoising

train on corrupted inputs: images, audio, sensors. Masked pre-training is its descendant.

Compression

the code is a short description. Learned image codecs = autoencoder + entropy coding of $\bz$.

Pre-training

learn the encoder without labels, then a small classifier on $\bz$. Masked autoencoders (MAE) pre-train vision transformers.

Anomaly detection

train on normal data only; a large reconstruction error flags the rest: defects, fraud. Extra 5.

Search and clustering

nearest neighbours and clusters in code space: similar images, near-duplicates. Extra 2.

Generation

decode new codes. Needs a density over $\bz$: the VAE (Part C) and, sharper, GANs (Extra 4).

An autoencoder cannot generate

To generate, sample a code and decode it. With a plain autoencoder:

  • no density: training never says which codes are likely;
  • holes: 50 % of the box around the 2-D codes holds no training code;
  • no scale: the 16-D codes have means from −12.2 to 4.5.

2-D: the decoder fills the box with digit-like images (the referee is confident on 83 %), in the wrong proportions: 21 % zeros, 1 % fives (test set: 10 % and 9 %).

16-D: $\bz\sim\Nc(\mathbf 0,\bI)$ gives smudges, 2 % confident. A Gaussian fitted to the codes after training: 37 %. The VAE puts the density into training.

Referee: an MNIST classifier, 97.3 % accurate, confident (> 0.9) on 90.8 % of real test digits.

A latent-variable model

Assume each image is made in two steps:

$$\bz\sim p(\bz)=\Nc(\mathbf 0,\bI),\qquad \bx\sim p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)=\textstyle\prod_j\mathrm{Bern}\big(x_j;\hat x_j(\bz)\big)$$

Sampling is trivial. Learning needs the likelihood:

$$p_\theta(\bx)=\int p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)\,p(\bz)\,d\bz$$

No closed form with a network decoder. Random codes from the prior almost never explain a given digit; the useful ones come from the posterior $p_\theta(\bz\mid\bx)=p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)p(\bz)/p_\theta(\bx)$, which needs $p_\theta(\bx)$ again.

VAE (Kingma & Welling 2014; Rezende et al. 2014): an encoder outputs an approximate posterior $q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)=\Nc\big(\bmu_\phi(\bx),\mathrm{diag}\,\bsig^2_\phi(\bx)\big)$, and we maximise a lower bound on $\log p_\theta(\bx)$.

The KL divergence

$$\KL(q\,\|\,p)=\E_{\bz\sim q}\Big[\log\frac{q(\bz)}{p(\bz)}\Big]=\int q(\bz)\log\frac{q(\bz)}{p(\bz)}\,d\bz$$

Always ≥ 0, and 0 only when $q=p$ (Gibbs). Jensen: log is concave, so $\E[\log Y]\le\log\E[Y]$ (figure). With $Y=p/q$:

$$-\KL(q\,\|\,p)=\E_q\Big[\log\frac pq\Big]\le\log\E_q\Big[\frac pq\Big]=\log\int p=0$$

Not symmetric: $\KL(\Nc(1,0.5^2)\,\|\,\Nc(0,1))=0.818$ but $\KL(\Nc(0,1)\,\|\,\Nc(1,0.5^2))=2.807$ nats. Not a distance.

You have used it: cross-entropy = entropy + KL, so training a classifier with cross-entropy minimises $\KL(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})$.

The ELBO, two ways

1 · Jensen. Multiply and divide by $q$ inside the integral, then use that log is concave:

$$\log p_\theta(\bx)=\log\E_{q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)}\Big[\frac{p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)\,p(\bz)}{q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)}\Big]\ \ge\ \E_{q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)}\Big[\log\frac{p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)\,p(\bz)}{q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)}\Big]=:\Lc(\bx)$$

2 · The exact gap. For every $\bz$, $p_\theta(\bx)=p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)p(\bz)/p_\theta(\bz\mid\bx)$. Take logs, multiply and divide by $q$, average over $q$:

$$\log p_\theta(\bx)=\Lc(\bx)+\KL\big(q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)\,\|\,p_\theta(\bz\mid\bx)\big)\qquad\text{the gap is the KL to the true posterior}$$

3 · Two terms. Split the log of the ratio into $\log p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)-\log\big(q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)/p(\bz)\big)$:

$$\Lc(\bx)=\underbrace{\E_{q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)}\big[\log p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)\big]}_{-\text{ reconstruction error}}-\underbrace{\KL\big(q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)\,\|\,p(\bz)\big)}_{\text{stay near the prior}}$$

Maximising $\Lc$ over $\phi$ brings $q$ closer to the true posterior (smaller gap); over $\theta$ it raises the likelihood. The VAE loss is $-\Lc$: the BCE of a decoded random code plus a KL penalty.

The ELBO with numbers

The lab's 2-D VAE (15 epochs). With two dimensions $\log p_\theta(\bx)$ can be computed exactly: decode a 181 × 181 grid of codes and add up $p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)\,p(\bz)\,\Delta z^2$.

Test "0": $\log p(\bx)=-136.23$; ELBO $=-130.11-6.61=-136.71$; gap $=\log p(\bx)-\text{ELBO}=0.48$ nats ($\KL(q\,\|\,p(\bz\mid\bx))$ computed directly on the grid: 0.47; the ELBO is a Monte-Carlo estimate).

Test "5": gap 8.64 nats. Its true posterior lies elsewhere (×): only 0.5 % of its mass is within 3 standard deviations of $q$. The encoder bet on the wrong reading of an ambiguous digit, and importance sampling with $q$ misses it too: −283.84 instead of −279.07.

Whole test set: ELBO −146.0 nats per digit = 0.269 bits per pixel with 2 dimensions, −108.5 = 0.200 bits per pixel with 16.

KL between Gaussians, in closed form

One dimension, $q=\Nc(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $p=\Nc(0,1)$:

$$\log q(z)-\log p(z)=-\log\sigma-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{z^2}{2}$$

Average over $q$, with $\E_q[(z-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2$ and $\E_q[z^2]=\mu^2+\sigma^2$:

$$\KL=\tfrac12\big(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\log\sigma^2\big)$$

A diagonal $q$ in $k$ dimensions: the log-densities add, so do the KLs, $\ \KL=\frac12\sum_j\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\big)$.

$\mu^2$ pulls the code to the origin; $\sigma^2-1-\log\sigma^2$ punishes posteriors that are too narrow or too wide. Lab: equal to numerical integration to $10^{-6}$, and to a 20,000-sample Monte Carlo estimate within 0.35 %.

The reparameterisation trick

The reconstruction term averages over $\bz\sim q_\phi$, and we need its gradient with respect to $\phi$. Backpropagation cannot go through "draw a sample".

$$\bz=\bmu_\phi(\bx)+\bsig_\phi(\bx)\odot\beps,\qquad\beps\sim\Nc(\mathbf 0,\bI)$$

Now $\E_{q_\phi}[h(\bz)]=\E_{\beps}\big[h(\bmu_\phi+\bsig_\phi\odot\beps)\big]$: the gradient moves inside the average and flows through $\bmu$ and $\bsig$; $\beps$ is just an input.

One sample per image per step is enough. The encoder outputs $\log\bsig^2$: any real number is valid and $\bsig=e^{\frac12\log\bsig^2}\gt0$.

The alternative: the score-function estimator

A general estimator that needs only $\log q$: since $\nabla q=q\,\nabla\log q$,

$$\nabla_\mu\E_{q_\mu}[h(z)]=\E_{q_\mu}\big[h(z)\,\nabla_\mu\log q_\mu(z)\big]$$

the score function (REINFORCE). It works for discrete $z$ too, but it uses only the value of $h$, not its slope.

Test: $h(z)=z^2$, $q=\Nc(\mu,1)$, $\mu=1$, true gradient $2\mu=2$. Per sample, the reparameterised estimate $2(\mu+\epsilon)$ has variance 4; the score-function one, $z^2(z-\mu)$, has variance 30.

In a VAE, $h$ is a 784-pixel log-likelihood and the gap is far larger. Score functions need baselines and many samples: reinforcement learning, discrete latent variables.

The VAE in PyTorch

class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, k):
        super().__init__()
        self.body = mlp([784, 256, 64])
        self.mu = nn.Linear(64, k)
        self.logvar = nn.Linear(64, k)     # log σ²: any real
        self.dec = mlp([k, 64, 256, 784])  # logits

    def forward(self, x):
        h = F.relu(self.body(x))
        mu, logvar = self.mu(h), self.logvar(h)
        eps = torch.randn_like(mu)
        z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * eps
        return self.dec(z), mu, logvar

def vae_loss(model, x, beta=1.0):
    x = torch.bernoulli(x)                 # binarise
    logits, mu, logvar = model(x)
    rec = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        logits, x, reduction="none").sum(1)    # pixels
    kl = 0.5 * (mu**2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(1)
    return (rec + beta * kl).mean()        # batch

Sum, then average: sum over the 784 pixels and the $k$ dimensions, average over the batch. Other bookkeeping changes β (Part D).

Bernoulli needs 0/1 pixels: dynamic binarisation, a fresh random black-and-white version of each digit every time. Then $-\Lc$ is an honest bound, in nats.

Logits and ..._with_logits: stable for pixels near 0 and 1.

After training: $\bmu(\bx)$ is the code of $\bx$; to generate, decode $\bz\sim\Nc(\mathbf 0,\bI)$.

Lab: the 2-D VAE trains in 22 s, the 16-D one in 14 s, on a CPU.

What the KL does to the code space

Summed over the data, the KL pulls every $q(\bz\mid\bx)$ towards $\Nc(\mathbf 0,\bI)$ and stops posteriors from shrinking to points. The reconstruction term keeps different digits apart.

The compromise: overlapping posteriors that fill the prior's region, without holes. Prior samples land where the decoder was trained.

Lab, 2-D codes: VAE mean (−0.11, 0.04), std (1.16, 1.06); AE mean (−0.77, −2.80), std (3.76, 3.52). Sample classes closer to the test set (total variation 0.16 vs 0.25). Same 15-NN accuracy: 74.2 % vs 73.8 %.

Two dimensions are too few for sharp digits: the referee is confident on 60 % of 2-D VAE samples. In 16-D: VAE 45 %, AE with $\Nc(\mathbf 0,\bI)$ 2 %.

Walking in the VAE code space

Decode a regular grid of codes $\bz=\Phi^{-1}(u)$ for evenly spaced $u$: every cell has the same prior mass.

During training every code is decoded with noise around $\bmu(\bx)$, so nearby codes must give similar images: the decoder is smooth, and digits morph into each other.

Nobody told the model what a digit is: it found its own coordinates. Labels only colour the plot.

With $\beta\gt1$ the axes tend to become disentangled: one factor of variation per coordinate (Part D).

Interpolation: pixels, AE, VAE

Walk along the straight line between two codes and decode. If the codes filled a convex region, every point would be a plausible digit.

A cross-fade in pixels leaves the data at once: half a 0 on top of half a 7. In this example the AE's middle images are ambiguous (lowest referee confidence 0.52), the VAE's morph (0.89).

Over 790 pairs of test digits the lowest confidence along the path averages 0.54 (pixels), 0.59 (AE), 0.58 (VAE): the VAE's real gain is sampling, not always interpolation.

β-VAE: rate versus distortion

$$\text{loss}=\underbrace{\E_q\big[-\log p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)\big]}_{\text{distortion }D}+\beta\,\underbrace{\KL\big(q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)\,\|\,p(\bz)\big)}_{\text{rate }R}$$

Rate: the nats the code carries about $\bx$. $\beta=1$ is the ELBO, the only β that maximises the bound on the likelihood (Higgins et al., 2017).

β = 0.1: $D=73.0$, $R=48.6$, all 16 dimensions active, poor samples (17 % confident): codes spread out like an AE's.

β = 4: $D=119.3$, $R=7.4$, 6 active dimensions: blurrier reconstructions, tidier samples (56 %).

Active dimension: $\mathrm{Var}_{\bx}[\mu_j(\bx)]\gt0.01$. A dimension with KL 0 carries nothing: $q(z_j\mid\bx)=p(z_j)$ for every $\bx$.

Posterior collapse

If the KL is too expensive, the cheapest solution is $q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)=p(\bz)$ for every input: KL = 0, the code says nothing, the decoder outputs one average image.

It also happens with strong decoders (an autoregressive decoder can model the pixels without $\bz$). Remedies: KL warm-up (β from 0 to 1 over the first epochs), free bits (a minimum KL per dimension), a weaker decoder.

The bug. F.binary_cross_entropy(x_hat, x) averages over the 784 pixels. With the KL summed over the dimensions, the loss is $\frac1{784}(D+784\,R)$: β = 784. Lab: KL 0.000 nats, 0 active dimensions, the same output for every input.

Why VAE samples are blurry

The decoder outputs a mean: the Bernoulli probabilities, or the Gaussian mean. Drawing the pixels gives salt and pepper, because they are independent given $\bz$.

When a code fits several images, the likelihood is maximised by their average: under MSE the best single guess for "a 0 or a 7, one half each" is the cross-fade.

The KL adds noise to the codes, so neighbouring codes share their images: more averaging. A larger β blurs more (lab: reconstruction 119.3 nats at β = 4, 90.3 at β = 1).

Fixes: richer likelihoods (autoregressive decoders), perceptual and adversarial losses (GANs, Extra 4), discrete codes (VQ-VAE), diffusion in the latent space.

A conditional VAE

$$\Lc(\bx,y)=\E_{q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx,y)}\big[\log p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz,y)\big]-\KL\big(q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx,y)\,\|\,p(\bz)\big)$$

In code: append the one-hot label to the encoder input and to the decoder input (Sohn et al., 2015).

The label says which digit, so the code is free to describe the style: slant, width, thickness. To draw a 7, sample $\bz\sim\Nc(\mathbf 0,\bI)$ and decode it with $y=7$.

Lab, $k=2$, 10 epochs: ELBO −132.6 nats against −146.0 without labels; the referee recognises the requested digit in 99.8 % of 1000 samples.

Where the field went: VQ-VAE and latent diffusion

VQ-VAE: replace the Gaussian code by the nearest of $K$ learned codebook vectors (the gradient is copied straight through the rounding). The prior is learned afterwards, by an autoregressive model or a transformer over the grid of indices: sharp samples, and the image tokenisers of DALL·E and VQGAN.

Latent diffusion: a VAE with a tiny KL weight compresses images 8× per side; a diffusion model generates in that latent space; the decoder paints the pixels. The autoencoder survived as the compressor, and generation moved to a stronger prior over its codes.

The topic in one slide

A · Autoencoders and PCA

  • $\bz=f_\phi(\bx)$, $\hx=g_\theta(\bz)$; loss $=-\log p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)$: MSE ↔ Gaussian, BCE ↔ Bernoulli
  • PCA: top-$k$ eigenvectors of $\bC$ (SVD); error $=\sum_{j\gt k}\lambda_j$
  • linear AE → the PCA subspace; non-linear AE: lower error, better codes

B · Variants and uses

  • overcomplete codes can copy: add sparsity or noise
  • denoising: noisy input, clean target; transposed convolutions to upsample
  • denoising, compression, pre-training, anomalies (Extra 5), embeddings (Extra 2)

C · The VAE

  • $p(\bz)=\Nc(\mathbf 0,\bI)$, $p_\theta(\bx\mid\bz)$, $q_\phi(\bz\mid\bx)=\Nc(\bmu,\mathrm{diag}\,\bsig^2)$
  • $\log p(\bx)=\Lc+\KL(q\,\|\,p(\bz\mid\bx))$; $\Lc=\E_q\log p(\bx\mid\bz)-\KL(q\,\|\,p(\bz))$
  • KL $=\frac12\sum(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\log\sigma^2)$; $\bz=\bmu+\bsig\odot\beps$

D · β, failures and practice

  • β trades rate for distortion; only β = 1 is the ELBO
  • collapse: KL → 0 (the β = 784 bug); blurry = the mean of a likelihood
  • CVAE: condition on $y$; today: VQ-VAE, latent diffusion
