Where we are

Extra 3 learned a density. The autoencoder compresses and reconstructs; the VAE maximises the ELBO and samples by decoding $\bz\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$. Its samples are blurry: a Gaussian decoder averages every image compatible with $\bz$.

A GAN drops the density altogether. A generator maps noise to samples; a discriminator, trained to tell real from fake, serves as the generator's loss function. The two are trained against each other.

Generative adversarial network (Goodfellow et al., 2014): a generator $G$ and a discriminator $D$ playing a two-player game whose equilibrium is $\pg=\pd$.

Four parts: A generative modelling and the game, with numbers; B the theory: optimal $D$, Jensen–Shannon, saturation; C failure modes and the Wasserstein GAN; D architectures (DCGAN, conditional, pix2pix, CycleGAN, StyleGAN) and evaluation (FID). It ends with the Extra 4 lab.

Two ways to learn $\pd$

We only have samples $\bx_1,\dots,\bx_n\sim\pd$. A generative model must evaluate a density, sample new $\bx$, or both.

Explicit density: write $p_\theta(\bx)$, maximise the likelihood.

  • autoregressive: $p(\bx)=\prod_ip(x_i\mid x_{\lt i})$, exact (PixelCNN, GPT)
  • flows: an invertible map, exact by change of variables
  • VAE: a lower bound, the ELBO (Extra 3)

Implicit: only a sampler $\bx=G_\theta(\bz)$. No formula for $\pg(\bx)$. GANs.

Why sampling can be easier: no normalising constant, no special architecture. And images live near a thin low-dimensional set of pixel space, where a density is huge on the set and zero off it; a generator from a low-dimensional $\bz$ simply lands on it. The price: no likelihood to train with, or to evaluate.

A generator is a push-forward

If $\bz\sim p_z$, then $\bx=G(\bz)$ has a distribution $\pg$: $G$ pushes the noise forward. In 1-D the exact sampler is known:

$$G^*(z)=F^{-1}\big(\Phi(z)\big)$$

$\Phi$ is the normal CDF, $F$ the data CDF. Proof: $\Phi(z)$ is uniform, so $P(G^*(z)\le t)=P(\Phi(z)\le F(t))=F(t)$.

The map is flat inside each mode and very steep across the gap (slope ≈ 420 at $z=-0.25$). A neural $G$ is continuous: it must learn this step, and it always leaves a thin bridge of samples between the modes.

For images there is no formula. A GAN learns $G$ from samples, guided by a discriminator.

What GANs are used for

Image synthesis

Faces, objects, scenes from noise. StyleGAN's faces (2019) are hard to tell from photos.

Super-resolution

An adversarial loss restores sharp texture where an L2 loss gives a blur. Old photos, satellite images, video.

Data augmentation

Extra examples of a rare class: an unusual damage type, a rare tumour. Validate on real data only.

Synthetic data

Share data without the records. Caveat: a GAN can memorise, and samples can leak; privacy needs a formal method (DP).

Image-to-image

Sketch → photo, day → night, map → aerial, horse → zebra (pix2pix, CycleGAN; Part D).

An adversarial loss

A discriminator as an extra loss inside other models: the autoencoders of latent diffusion, fast samplers.

The GAN game

$$\min_G\max_D\;V(D,G)=\E_{\bx\sim\pd}\big[\log D(\bx)\big]+\E_{\bz\sim p_z}\big[\log\big(1-D(G(\bz))\big)\big]$$

$D$ maximises $V$: $-V$ is the binary cross-entropy with label 1 for real and 0 for fake, so $D$ is a logistic-regression classifier (any network with a sigmoid output).

$G$ minimises $V$: it wants $D(G(\bz))\to1$. It never sees a real example; it learns only through the gradient of $D$.

A zero-sum game: what $D$ gains, $G$ loses. Its equilibrium (Part B): $\pg=\pd$ and $D=\tfrac12$ everywhere.

Training: alternating gradient steps

bce = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()           # D outputs a logit
ones, zeros = torch.ones(m, 1), torch.zeros(m, 1)
for x in loader:                       # a batch of m real examples
    # 1. discriminator step: real -> 1, fake -> 0
    fake = G(torch.randn(m, d_z))
    loss_D = bce(D(x), ones) + bce(D(fake.detach()), zeros)
    opt_D.zero_grad(); loss_D.backward(); opt_D.step()

    # 2. generator step: make D say "real" on fakes
    loss_G = bce(D(fake), ones)        # non-saturating loss
    opt_G.zero_grad(); loss_G.backward(); opt_G.step()

opt_G = torch.optim.Adam(G.parameters(), 2e-4, betas=(0.5, 0.999))
opt_D = torch.optim.Adam(D.parameters(), 2e-4, betas=(0.5, 0.999))

Algorithm (Goodfellow et al., 2014). Repeat: $k$ steps on $\phi$ to decrease $L_D=-\frac1m\sum_i\log D(\bx_i)-\frac1m\sum_i\log(1-D(G(\bz_i)))$, then one step on $\theta$.

$k=1$ in practice (DCGAN); WGAN uses $k=5$.

detach(): the $D$ step must not update $G$. The $G$ step backpropagates through $D$ into $G$; only opt_G steps.

Labels flipped for $G$: minimise $-\log D(G(\bz))$ instead of $\log(1-D(G(\bz)))$. Same goal, better gradients (Part B).

$\beta_1=0.5$ in Adam: less momentum, because each player's target moves at every step.

One round of the game, with numbers

$D(x)=\sigma(wx+b)$, $G(z)=\theta+z$. Real $x=1.5,\,2.5$; noise $z=-0.5,\,0.5$; $w=1$, $b=-1$, $\theta=0$; SGD, $\eta=1$.

The two groups and $D$ before training. Its boundary $wx+b=0$ is at $x=1$, exactly midway.

$D$ on real: $\sigma(0.5)=0.622$, $\sigma(1.5)=0.818$.
$D$ on fake: $\sigma(-1.5)=0.182$, $\sigma(-0.5)=0.378$.

$L_D=0.338+0.338=0.675$

$\frac{\partial L_D}{\partial w}=-\frac12\sum(1-D)x_r+\frac12\sum D\,\tilde x$

$\quad=-0.511+0.049=-0.462$

$\frac{\partial L_D}{\partial b}=-0.280+0.280=0$: the boundary stays, the slope grows, $w=1.462$. Fakes now get $0.150$ and $0.433$.

$\frac{\partial L_G}{\partial\theta}=-\frac12\sum(1-D(\tilde x))\,w$

$\quad=-\frac12(0.850+0.567)\cdot1.462=-1.036$

$\theta=0+1.036$: the fakes move to $0.536,\,1.536$, where $D$ says $0.446$ and $0.777$.

The minimax loss would give $-0.427$: 2.4 times smaller.

A 1-D GAN learns a mixture

$\pd=0.4\,\mathcal N(-2,0.5^2)+0.6\,\mathcal N(2,0.5^2)$. $G$: $\bz\in\R^4\to64\to64\to1$; $D$: $1\to64\to64\to1$; Adam $5\cdot10^{-4}$, 4000 iterations, 6 s on a CPU.

$D$ is a map of the error: high where $\pd\gt\pg$, low where $\pg\gt\pd$. $G$ follows its gradient to where mass is missing.

It overshoots, back and forth: all of $G$'s mass on the right mode (iterations 10–60), a broad blanket (100–200), a sharp peak on the right mode again (around 500). Episodes of mode collapse and recovery.

At the end: JS = 0.027 (it started at 0.692), mean 0.440 (data 0.400), std 1.953 (2.022). $D\approx0.5$ on the data, with a dip in the gap: the bridge a continuous $G$ cannot remove.

The losses hover at $L_D\approx$ 1.384 and $L_G\approx$ 0.700, near the equilibrium values $2\log2=1.386$ and $\log2=0.693$. A GAN loss curve says little about quality.

The optimal discriminator

An expectation over $\bz$ of a function of $G(\bz)$ is an expectation over $\pg$, so

$$V=\int\big[\pd(\bx)\log D(\bx)+\pg(\bx)\log(1-D(\bx))\big]\,d\bx$$

$D$ is free at each $\bx$: maximise pointwise. With $a=\pd(\bx)$, $b=\pg(\bx)$, $y=D(\bx)$:

$$f(y)=a\log y+b\log(1-y),\quad f'(y)=\frac ay-\frac b{1-y}=0$$
$$D^*(\bx)=\frac{\pd(\bx)}{\pd(\bx)+\pg(\bx)}$$

$f''=-a/y^2-b/(1-y)^2\lt0$: a maximum. $D^*$ needs only the ratio of the two densities, which a classifier can estimate from samples: the core trick of GANs.

Checked: a trained $D$ finds $D^*$

In the lab: freeze $G$ at iteration 200 (broad, far from the data), estimate $\pg$ from 2 million samples, train a fresh $D$ for 3000 steps, compare with the formula.

Mean $|D-D^*|$ = 0.010 on the 90 grid points that have samples (max 0.039).

At $x=1$ there is almost no data and many fakes: $D^*\approx0$. At $x=2$ data outnumber fakes: $D^*\gt0.5$. The classifier learned the density ratio without being told any density.

Grey: no samples, $D^*=0/0$. There $D$ is unconstrained and arbitrary. In high dimensions most of the space is like that.

The game minimises Jensen–Shannon

Put $D^*$ into $V$, with $M=\tfrac12(\pd+\pg)$, so that $\frac{\pd}{\pd+\pg}=\frac12\frac{\pd}M$:

$$V(G,D^*)=\int\pd\log\frac{\pd}{2M}+\int\pg\log\frac{\pg}{2M}$$
$$=-\log4+\KL(\pd\,\|\,M)+\KL(\pg\,\|\,M)$$
$$V(G,D^*)=-\log4+2\,\JS(\pd\,\|\,\pg)$$

$0\le\JS\le\log2$, and $\JS=0$ only if $\pg=\pd$. So the global optimum is $\pg=\pd$, with $D^*=\tfrac12$ and $V=-\log4=-1.386$.

Lab, $G$ of iteration 200: $\JS=$ 0.1552, so $-\log4+2\JS=$ −1.0759; Monte Carlo with the trained $D$: −1.0769.

With an optimal $D$ at every step, training $G$ would minimise JS. In practice $D$ takes one step per $G$ step, so this is only the target the game aims at.

Why the minimax loss saturates

Write $D=\sigma(a)$ on a fake, with $a$ the logit:

loss for $G$$\partial/\partial a$when $D\to0$
minimax $\log(1-D)$$-D$$\to0$: stalls
non-saturating $-\log D$$-(1-D)$$\to-1$

Early on the fakes are bad and $D$ rejects them: $D(G(\bz))\approx0$, exactly where the minimax loss is flat. Lab: $D(G(\bz))=$ 0.007, gradient 100 times larger with $-\log D$.

Same fixed point, strong gradient when $G$ is bad: every practical GAN trains $G$ with $-\log D(G(\bz))$, the BCE with the fake labels flipped to 1.

The price: no longer zero-sum. With $D^*$ it minimises $\KL(\pg\|\pd)-2\JS$ (Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017): a reverse KL, which is mode-seeking. Part C.

Mode collapse on a ring of 8 Gaussians

8 Gaussians, std 0.05, on a circle of radius 2. A sample is good if within 3 std of a mode; a mode is covered if it holds ≥ 1% of the samples.

Same networks for both (2-layer MLPs, 128 units, $d_z=256$), Adam $10^{-3}$, 2,000 generator steps.

Vanilla GAN: one mode at a time, and the mode keeps changing: mode hopping. At step 2000: 0 of 8 modes, 0.00 of the samples good.

WGAN-GP (later in this part): 8 of 8 modes, 0.58 good.

Mode collapse is partly luck: with other seeds the vanilla GAN sometimes finds all the modes. But its loss has no force against collapse; the Wasserstein loss does.

Why the generator collapses

1 · The loss is mode-seeking. $\KL(q\|p)=\int q\log\frac qp$ is huge where $q\gt0$ and $p\approx0$ (fakes where there is no data) and small for missing modes. Maximum likelihood, $\KL(p\|q)$, is the opposite: it covers everything, and blurs.

2 · $G$ chases a moving target. $G$ moves its mass to wherever the current $D$ says "real"; $D$ learns to reject that spot; $G$ jumps to the next. Nothing in one step rewards covering several modes at once.

3 · Minimax ≠ maximin. The theory needs $D$ optimal for each $G$. Alternating single steps are closer to $\max_D\min_G$, where collapsing onto $D$'s favourite point is the best reply.

Cures: losses with a useful gradient everywhere (WGAN), regularised $D$ (gradient penalty, spectral norm), minibatch features that let $D$ see diversity, unrolled $D$ steps.

Two players do not descend one hill

The smallest game: $\ \min_x\max_y\ xy$. Equilibrium $(0,0)$. Gradient steps for both, simultaneously:

$$x\leftarrow x-\eta y,\qquad y\leftarrow y+\eta x$$
$$(x-\eta y)^2+(y+\eta x)^2=(1+\eta^2)(x^2+y^2)$$

The cross terms cancel: the distance grows by $\sqrt{1+\eta^2}$ at every step, for every learning rate. The vector field rotates; each player's descent feeds the other's.

Alternating: determinant 1, an orbit. Extragradient: shrinks by $\sqrt{1-\eta^2+\eta^4}$; optimistic GD: $x_{t+1}=x_t-2\eta y_t+\eta y_{t-1}$, converges.

In GANs: oscillating losses, cycling samples. Remedies: small $\eta$, $\beta_1=0.5$, different rates for $D$ and $G$ (TTUR), optimistic Adam, gradient penalties, averaging $G$'s weights (EMA).

No overlap, no gradient

$p=\mathcal U[0,1]$, $q_\theta=\mathcal U[\theta,\theta+1]$. If $|\theta|\ge1$ the supports are disjoint: where $p\gt0$, $q=0$, so $M=p/2$ and $\KL(p\|M)=\log2$. Hence

$$\JS(p\|q_\theta)=\log2\ \text{ for all }|\theta|\ge1$$

A flat loss: zero gradient, nothing tells $G$ which way to move. The perfect $D^*$ is 1 on the data, 0 on the fakes, and flat around both.

In high dimensions this is the normal case: data and early fakes lie on thin sets that almost never meet.

Wasserstein-1: $W_1(p,q_\theta)=|\theta|$. It grows with the distance and always points the way. The lab checks both curves against SciPy.

The earth mover's distance

$p$ is a pile of earth, $q$ a hole of the same volume. $W_1$ = the least total work, mass × distance, to fill the hole:

$$W_1(p,q)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(p,q)}\E_{(x,y)\sim\gamma}\,|x-y|$$

$\Pi(p,q)$: transport plans, joint distributions with marginals $p$ and $q$.

In 1-D the best plan never crosses itself, and $W_1$ is the area between the CDFs:

$$W_1=\int|F_p(t)-F_q(t)|\,dt$$

$p=(0.4,0.3,0.2,0.1,0)$, $q=(0,0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4)$: $W_1=2.0$ bins. On average every grain travels two bins.

The Wasserstein GAN

The best plan is out of reach between image distributions. The Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality turns $W_1$ into a maximisation over functions:

$$W_1(\pd,\pg)=\sup_{\|f\|_L\le1}\ \E_{\pd}f(\bx)-\E_{p_z}f(G(\bz))$$

Over 1-Lipschitz $f$: $|f(\bx)-f(\mathbf y)|\le\|\bx-\mathbf y\|$. A network can do the sup.

Critic $f_w$: no sigmoid, a real-valued score. It maximises $\E f_w(\text{real})-\E f_w(\text{fake})$; $G$ minimises $-\E f_w(G(\bz))$. $k=5$ critic steps per $G$ step.

The critic's value estimates $W_1$, a loss that decreases as samples improve, unlike the GAN loss. Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017.

On the disjoint uniforms: $D^*$ is flat at the fakes (no gradient), while the critic $f(x)=1.5-x$ has slope 1 everywhere and pushes the fakes towards the data.

Keeping the critic 1-Lipschitz

Without a constraint the critic blows the difference up to infinity. Three ways to keep $\|f\|_L\le1$:

Weight clipping (WGAN): clip every weight to $[-0.01,0.01]$. Crude: the critic becomes almost linear, gradients explode or vanish with the clip value.

Gradient penalty (WGAN-GP, Gulrajani et al., 2017): the optimal critic has gradient norm 1 between coupled points, so add

$$\lambda\,\E_{\hat\bx}\big(\|\nabla_{\hat\bx}f_w(\hat\bx)\|-1\big)^2,\quad\hat\bx=\epsilon\bx+(1-\epsilon)G(\bz)$$

Spectral normalisation (Miyato et al., 2018): divide each weight matrix by its largest singular value, so every layer is 1-Lipschitz. Cheap; works with the standard loss too.

Lab, ring of Gaussians, $\lambda=0.1$: gradient norm on the interpolates 1.45, all 8 modes found. With clipping instead: 0 of 8 after 2,000 steps.

Upsampling: the transposed convolution

A generator goes from a small tensor to an image. A transposed convolution upsamples with learned weights: each input pixel multiplies the kernel, and the copies are pasted stride pixels apart; overlaps add up.

$$H_{\text{out}}=(H_{\text{in}}-1)\,s-2p+k$$

DCGAN: $k=4$, $s=2$, $p=1$ gives $H_{\text{out}}=2H_{\text{in}}$: $7\to14\to28$.

The name: it is the transpose of a strided convolution, $\langle\mathrm{conv}(\mathbf y),\bx\rangle=\langle\mathbf y,\mathrm{convT}(\bx)\rangle$, the very operation backprop uses to send gradients through a convolution. The lab checks it.

Uneven overlaps ($k$ not a multiple of $s$) give checkerboard artefacts; resize + convolution avoids them.

DCGAN: the lab's two networks

  • no pooling: strided convolutions in $D$, transposed in $G$
  • BatchNorm in both, except $G$'s output and $D$'s input
  • ReLU in $G$, tanh output (images in $[-1,1]$); LeakyReLU 0.2 in $D$
  • Adam, $\eta=2\cdot10^{-4}$, $\beta_1=0.5$; weights from $\mathcal N(0,0.02^2)$
  • no big fully connected hidden layers
  • Radford, Metz and Chintala, 2016: the recipe that made convolutional GANs train reliably

DCGAN samples during training

MNIST, 3 epochs, $m=64$ (2,811 iterations), the same 24 noise vectors at every checkpoint. CPU: about 45 s per epoch.

In a healthy run neither player wins: in the last epoch mean $D(\bx)=$ 0.72 and $D(G(\bz))=$ 0.28.

Latent walks: $G((1-t)\bz_a+t\bz_b)$ for $t=0,\dots,1$ morphs one digit into another, not a cross-fade:

A smooth latent space makes GAN inversion possible: find the $\bz$ with $G(\bz)\approx$ a real image, then edit by moving $\bz$.

Conditional GANs: digits on demand

Give the label $y$ to both networks (Mirza and Osindero, 2014): $G(\bz,y)$, $D(\bx,y)$. $D$ now asks "a real image of class $y$?", so $G$ must produce the right class to fool it.

Lab: the DCGAN code with a one-hot $y$, 2 epochs. A classifier that never saw a fake agrees with the requested digit for 90.1% of 10,000 samples.

Each column of the grid uses the same $\bz$: the label sets the content, $\bz$ the style (thickness, slant). pix2pix conditions on a whole image instead of a label, text-to-image models on a sentence.

Image-to-image translation

pix2pix (Isola et al., 2017): a conditional GAN whose condition is an image. U-Net generator; $D$ judges (input, output) pairs on $70\times70$ patches; an L1 term keeps the output close to the target.

It needs paired data: the same scene as a sketch and as a photo, a map and its aerial image.

CycleGAN (Zhu et al., 2017): no pairs, only two collections. Two generators $G:X\to Y$, $F:Y\to X$, a discriminator per domain, and cycle consistency: $F(G(x))\approx x$.

Without the cycle, $G$ could map every horse to the same zebra and still fool $D_Y$. The cycle forces it to keep the content.

StyleGAN, in one slide

StyleGAN (Karras et al., 2019–2021), the GAN behind the famous fake faces:

  • mapping network: 8 FC layers turn $\bz$ into a style code $\mathbf w$, a less tangled latent space
  • the synthesis network starts from a learned constant; $\mathbf w$ modulates every resolution (AdaIN, later weight modulation): coarse layers set pose, fine layers colour
  • per-pixel noise inputs for stochastic detail: hair, pores
  • grown resolution by resolution, $4^2\to1024^2$ (ProGAN, 2018)
  • truncation trick: sample $\mathbf w$ closer to its mean, quality up, diversity down

GAN inversion: optimise $\mathbf w$ so that $G(\mathbf w)$ matches a real photo; then edit the photo by moving $\mathbf w$ (age, smile, lighting).

Evaluating a generator without a likelihood

No $\log p(\bx)$ to report. We compare features of real and generated images, from a classifier trained on real data.

Inception Score: $\exp\big(\E_{\bx}\KL(p(y|\bx)\,\|\,p(y))\big)$: confident classes and balanced classes. It never looks at real images.

FID (Heusel et al., 2017): fit a Gaussian to each feature set,

$$\mathrm{FID}=\|\bmu_r-\bmu_g\|^2+\tr\big(\bSigma_r+\bSigma_g-2(\bSigma_r\bSigma_g)^{1/2}\big)$$

In 1-D: $(\mu_r-\mu_g)^2+(\sigma_r-\sigma_g)^2$, a content term and a diversity term.

2-D by hand: $\bmu_g=(1,0)$, $\bSigma_g=\mathrm{diag}(4,1)$ vs $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$: $1+\big[(1+1)+(4+1)-2(2+1)\big]=2$, as the lab asserts.

Quality, coverage and memorisation

FID mixes two failures into one number. A collapsed GAN has high precision and low recall; a blurry model (a VAE) the opposite.

A cheap extra check: the class histogram of the samples, by the classifier. Mode dropping shows up as a missing class.

Memorisation: a GAN that copies its training images looks perfect and is useless, and for synthetic data it is a privacy leak. Check: each sample's nearest training image.

Lab: median distance to the nearest training image 5.96 for samples, 4.38 for real test digits. The samples are no closer than new real data; the closest ones are simple 1s, and different 1s from their neighbours: no sign of copying.

Not a privacy proof: a sample can leak a detail (a face, a plate) without being a copy. Formal guarantees need differentially private training.

The three are often combined: Stable Diffusion runs diffusion in the latent space of an autoencoder trained with a KL term and an adversarial loss.

Diffusion is today's default for images

Why diffusion won (2021–2022): it trains like a regression (no game, no collapse), covers the modes, and scales with data and compute. "Diffusion models beat GANs on image synthesis" (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021) closed the debate for ImageNet.

Its weak point is speed: many denoising passes per sample.

Where GANs still win: one forward pass. Real-time super-resolution and video enhancement, on-device filters, face editing; GigaGAN (2023) showed that GANs scale too.

Adversarial ideas live on: the VQGAN autoencoder under latent diffusion, and adversarial distillation (SDXL Turbo, 2023) that turns a diffusion model into a 1–4 step generator with a discriminator.

The topic in one slide

A · The game

  • implicit model: $\bx=G(\bz)$, a push-forward of noise, no density
  • $\min_G\max_D\E\log D(\bx)+\E\log(1-D(G(\bz)))$
  • alternating steps; $G$ learns only through $D$'s gradient

B · The theory

  • $D^*=\pd/(\pd+\pg)$: a density-ratio estimator
  • $V(G,D^*)=-\log4+2\JS(\pd\|\pg)$; optimum $\pg=\pd$, $D=\tfrac12$
  • minimax loss saturates; train $G$ with $-\log D(G(\bz))$

C · Failures and fixes

  • mode collapse and hopping; rotation of the two-player field
  • disjoint supports: JS $=\log2$, no gradient
  • WGAN: $W_1$ by duality, a 1-Lipschitz critic (clipping, GP, spectral norm)

D · Architectures and evaluation

  • DCGAN: (transposed) strided convolutions, BN, LeakyReLU
  • conditional, pix2pix, CycleGAN, StyleGAN
  • FID, precision and recall, memorisation; diffusion leads today

Jensen–Shannon
$\frac12\KL(p\|M)+\frac12\KL(q\|M)$, $M=\frac{p+q}2$; at most $\log2$.
transposed conv.
learned upsampling, the adjoint of a strided convolution.
earth mover's, $W_1$
least work to move one distribution onto another.
critic
WGAN's real-valued, 1-Lipschitz replacement for $D$.
gradient penalty
$\lambda(\|\nabla f(\hat\bx)\|-1)^2$ on interpolates.
spectral norm
weights divided by their largest singular value.
DCGAN
the convolutional recipe: strides, BatchNorm, LeakyReLU.
conditional GAN
$G(\bz,y)$ and $D(\bx,y)$: generation on demand.
cycle consistency
$F(G(x))\approx x$ in CycleGAN.
truncation trick
sampling near the mean latent: quality over diversity.
GAN inversion
finding the latent code of a real image.
FID
Fréchet distance between Gaussians fitted to features.
precision / recall
quality of the samples / coverage of the data.
