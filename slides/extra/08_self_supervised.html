Self-supervised and contrastive learning

imanolgranada@gmail.com

Where we are

Every model of the course learned from labels: a price, a class, a box. Labels cost money and expert time; raw data piles up for free. Most of the photos, documents and recordings an organisation owns have no label at all.

Yet the course already trained networks without labels: word2vec, BERT, GPT and the autoencoder hide part of the data and predict it from the rest. The targets come from the data itself.

Self-supervised learning (SSL): pretrain a network on unlabelled data with a task whose targets are computed from the data, then use a few labels to read the answer out of its features.

Four parts: A learning without labels, B contrastive learning (SimCLR and the InfoNCE loss, with numbers), C methods without negatives and collapse, D masked modelling, CLIP and practice. It ends with the Extra 8 lab.

Labels are expensive, data are cheap

1 · Pretrain

On all the unlabelled data, with a self-supervised loss. The expensive step, done once.

2 · Probe or fine-tune

Linear probe: freeze $f$, fit a logistic regression on $\bh=f(x)$. Fine-tune: train everything, starting from $f$.

3 · Compare

Against the same network trained from scratch on the same few labels, for several label budgets.

Example. A car insurer stores millions of claim photos; a few thousand are labelled by an expert with the damaged part and the severity. Pretrain on all the photos, then train the damage classifier on the labelled ones. With 1 % of the ImageNet labels, SimCLR fine-tuning reached 48.3 % top-1, against 25.4 % for supervised training on the same labels (Chen et al., 2020).

You have already seen self-supervision

word2vec

input a word
target its neighbours in the text

Words used in the same contexts get close vectors: the embeddings of the RAG chapter's ancestors.

BERT

input a sentence, 15 % of the tokens masked
target the masked tokens

The encoder learns bidirectional context; fine-tuned for classification.

GPT

input the first $t$ tokens
target token $t+1$

Every position of every text is a training example: trillions of free labels.

Autoencoder

input an image
target the same image, through a bottleneck

Extra 3: the code keeps what is needed to rebuild the input.

The common recipe: hide or corrupt part of the data and predict it from the rest. The task is a means; the features learned on the way are the goal.

Text was easy: tokens are discrete, so "predict the missing one" is a softmax over the vocabulary. Pixels are continuous and redundant, and predicting them exactly wastes capacity on details. Images needed other ideas: pretext tasks, then contrastive learning, then masked modelling done right (MAE).

Pretext tasks for images

Transform the image in a known way, ask the network to undo or name the transformation. The label is free.

  • Rotation (Gidaris et al., 2018): 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°.
  • Jigsaw (Noroozi and Favaro, 2016): 3 × 3 tiles, a fixed set of permutations.
  • Colourisation (Zhang et al., 2016): grey in, colour out.
  • Inpainting (Pathak et al., 2016): context encoders.

Why they are limited. The features specialise in the pretext: the last layers know about rotations, not objects. And networks find shortcuts: predicting the position of patches, a network learned the chromatic aberration of camera lenses instead of shapes (Doersch et al., 2015).

Linear probes on these features stayed far below supervised features. Contrastive learning (2020) closed most of the gap.

Two views of the same image

Sample two augmentations $t,t'\sim\cT$ and apply them to the same image: two views $\tx=t(x)$, $\tx'=t'(x)$.

Contrastive learning: views of the same image (positives) must get similar embeddings; views of different images (negatives) must not.

No label anywhere: "same image" is known by construction. The other images of the batch are the negatives.

A person reads the same digit in both views. That is what we ask of the encoder: the same vector whatever the crop, angle, contrast or missing patch.

The pipeline is the lab's: crop 50–100 % of the area, ±15°, jitter with probability 0.8, erasing with probability 0.25. One batched affine warp: a few milliseconds for 256 digits.

SimCLR in one picture

Encoder $f$: any network; ResNet-50 in the paper, a 3-layer CNN in the lab. Its output $\bh$ is the representation we keep.

Projection head $g$: a small MLP, $\bz=g(\bh)$. Only the loss sees $\bz$; the head is thrown away after pretraining.

Loss: on the cosine similarities of the $\bz$ of all $2N$ views of a batch. Both branches share the same weights.

SimCLR: Chen, Kornblith, Norouzi and Hinton (2020). A simple framework: no memory bank, no special architecture; just augmentations, a head, a large batch and the NT-Xent loss. ResNet-50: 69.3 % ImageNet top-1 with a linear probe.

The similarity matrix of a batch

A toy batch: $N=4$ images, two views each, 3-D projections. Views ordered $1a,\dots,4a,1b,\dots,4b$.

Cosine similarity of every pair:

$$s_{ij}=\frac{\bz_i^\top\bz_j}{\norm{\bz_i}\,\norm{\bz_j}}\in[-1,1]$$

View $i$ has one positive $p(i)$ and $2N-2=6$ negatives. Image 4 resembles image 1 ($s_{1a,4a}=0.83$): a hard negative.

Each row becomes a classification: which of the other 7 views is my partner? Row 1a gives its partner 1b only 0.318, because 4a takes 0.241.

The mean loss is 1.284, below chance ($\log 7=1.946$) but far from 0. Training moves the $\bz$ to push every positive's probability towards 1.

The InfoNCE loss is a cross-entropy over the batch

$$\ell_i=-\log\frac{\exp(s_{i,p(i)}/\tau)}{\sum_{k\ne i}\exp(s_{ik}/\tau)},\qquad \cL=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\ell_i$$

Called NT-Xent in SimCLR (normalised temperature-scaled cross-entropy); a form of InfoNCE (van den Oord et al., 2018).

Row $i$ of $\mathbf S/\tau$, diagonal removed, is the logit vector of a $(2N-1)$-way classifier whose correct class is $p(i)$. So $\ell_i$ is a softmax cross-entropy, and the code is one line:

z = F.normalize(torch.cat([z1, z2]), dim=1)
logits = z @ z.T / tau             # all s_ij / τ
logits.fill_diagonal_(-float("inf"))  # k ≠ i
targets = torch.cat([torch.arange(N, 2 * N),
                     torch.arange(N)])  # p(i)
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, targets)

Gradient. With $P_{ik}=\mathrm{softmax}_k(s_{ik}/\tau)$:

$$\frac{\partial\ell_i}{\partial s_{ik}}=\frac{1}{\tau}\big(P_{ik}-[k=p(i)]\big)$$

Proof: $\ell_i=-s_{i,p(i)}/\tau+\log\sum_{k\ne i}e^{s_{ik}/\tau}$; the log-sum-exp has derivative $P_{ik}/\tau$. The softmax cross-entropy gradient of the course.

The positive is pulled with weight $(1-P_{i,p(i)})/\tau$; each negative is pushed with weight $P_{ik}/\tau$. The negatives that already look like the anchor are pushed hardest.

Mutual information. $\cL$ bounds the information shared by the two views: $I(\tx;\tx')\ge\log(2N-1)-\cL$. More negatives allow a tighter bound: one reason for large batches.

Lab §2: the loop, the cross-entropy and autograd give the same 1.2836 and the same gradient.

The temperature $\tau$

$\tau$ scales every gap between similarities before the softmax. The push on two negatives is in the ratio

$$\frac{P_{ik}}{P_{ik'}}=e^{(s_{ik}-s_{ik'})/\tau}$$

Small $\tau$: the loss looks only at the hardest negatives. Sharp features, but unstable, and it also pushes away false negatives (another image of the same class).

Large $\tau$: every negative is pushed about equally; the loss cannot go far below $\log(2N-1)$ and learns slowly.

Typical values: 0.1 in SimCLR with batches of 4,096, 0.07 in MoCo, 0.5 for small batches (the lab). CLIP learns $\tau$ as a parameter.

Pull and push on the hypersphere

Because the $\bz$ are normalised, they live on the unit hypersphere. Here a toy in 2-D: 6 images × 2 views, each view a free point on the circle, gradient descent on NT-Xent with $\tau=0.5$.

First the pull dominates: each view runs towards its partner (dashed lines shrink).

Then the push: the pairs spread out, here 60° apart, the most uniform arrangement of 6 points on a circle.

A real encoder cannot place every view freely: it must map similar images to nearby points. Spreading the images while keeping views together forces it to group images by content.

Alignment and uniformity

Wang and Isola (2020): on the normalised projections $\hz=\bz/\norm{\bz}$,

$$\cL_{\text{align}}=\E\,\norm{\hz-\hz^+}^2\qquad\cL_{\text{unif}}=\log\E\,e^{-2\norm{\hz_x-\hz_y}^2}$$

Alignment (lower is better): positives together; $\norm{\hz-\hz^+}^2=2-2s$. Uniformity (more negative is better): images spread over the sphere; all at one point gives 0.

The two terms of the loss map onto them: $\ell_i=\underbrace{-s_{i,p(i)}/\tau}_{\text{alignment}}+\underbrace{\log\textstyle\sum_{k\ne i}e^{s_{ik}/\tau}}_{\text{uniformity}}$. With infinitely many negatives the second term becomes exactly a uniformity loss.

Both are needed. Alignment alone is solved by mapping everything to one point; uniformity alone by ignoring the image. Good representations score well on both.

Lab §7–8, 2,000 test digits with two views each. Random network: alignment 0.002 but uniformity −0.005, every digit in nearly one direction. SimCLR: 0.172 and −2.968. Without stop-gradient: 0.004 and −0.018, collapsed.

The augmentations decide what is learned

The loss asks $f$ to be invariant to whatever $t$ changes, and nothing else. Choosing $\cT$ is choosing what the representation ignores.

SimCLR's finding: of all pairs of augmentations, random crop + colour distortion matters most. With crops alone, two crops of a photo share their colour histogram: the network matches histograms, a shortcut, and never looks at shapes.

Lab ablation (300 steps each): without crop and rotation the 10-label probe falls from 89.0 % to 83.2 % (kNN: 95.0 → 93.1 %), while the pretext task gets easier: contrastive accuracy 0.94 instead of 0.46. Crop + rotation alone: 89.6 %.

The flip side: an invariance you build in is information you lose. Colour jitter is wrong if colour is the label (ripe fruit, rust on a car); rotation is wrong if orientation matters (a 6 and a 9).

The projection head: probe $\bh$, not $\bz$

The loss makes $\bz$ invariant to the augmentations, so $g$ learns to throw away whatever distinguishes two views: position, size, stroke darkness. Some of that may be useful later.

The layer before the head, $\bh$, keeps more. In SimCLR, a non-linear head with the probe on $\bh$ beats no head by more than 10 points on ImageNet.

Lab, all 60,000 labels: linear probe 98.1 % on $\bh$ against 97.3 % on $\bz$; raw pixels 92.1 %, the random encoder 90.5 %.

Keep the head small and discard it. Later methods (BYOL, SimSiam, DINO) keep the same structure: encoder, head, loss on the head.

Batch size and negatives

More negatives: a better estimate of the denominator, more hard negatives, a tighter bound $\log(2N-1)-\cL$. SimCLR trains with $N=4{,}096$ images (8,190 negatives per view) on 128 TPU cores, with the LARS optimiser.

MoCo (He et al., 2020) decouples the negatives from the batch: the keys of past batches wait in a queue of 65,536. They come from a momentum encoder, a slow moving average of the query encoder, $\theta_k\leftarrow0.999\,\theta_k+0.001\,\theta_q$, so old keys stay comparable.

With a queue, a batch of 256 on 8 GPUs is enough. The momentum encoder reappears in BYOL and DINO.

The lab uses $N=256$ (510 negatives per view) on a CPU: enough for digits.

Collapse

Keep only the positives: minimise $-\cos(\bz,\bz^+)$. A constant encoder, $f(x)=\mathbf c$ for every image, gets $\cos=1$ for every pair: the global minimum, and useless.

Complete collapse: every image at one point. Dimensional collapse: the embeddings use only a few directions of the space.

Measure: the standard deviation of $\hz$ over a batch, averaged over the dimensions. Spread over the sphere of $\R^{64}$: about $1/\sqrt{64}=0.125$. Collapsed: 0.

Lab §8, 300 steps each: without the stop-gradient the spread falls from 0.079 to 0.008 and the loss reaches −0.9975, its best value; with it (SimSiam) the spread stays at 0.103 and kNN reaches 81.0 %.

BYOL and SimSiam: a predictor and a stop-gradient

SimSiam (Chen and He, 2021): no negatives, no large batch. One branch adds a small predictor $q$; the other is a target that gets no gradient:

$$\cL=\tfrac12D\big(\bp_1,\sg(\bz_2)\big)+\tfrac12D\big(\bp_2,\sg(\bz_1)\big),\quad D(\bp,\bz)=-\frac{\bp^\top\bz}{\norm{\bp}\norm{\bz}}$$

BYOL (Grill et al., 2020), earlier: the target branch is a separate network, an exponential moving average of the online one ($\xi\leftarrow0.996\,\xi+0.004\,\theta$). 74.3 % ImageNet linear probe with a ResNet-50, without a single negative.

Why no collapse? Subtle: the constant solution is still a minimum of $\cL$. The stop-gradient changes the dynamics: training alternates, like $k$-means or EM, between fitting $q$ to the current targets and moving the targets. Remove the stop-gradient and it collapses (the paper: kNN accuracy 0.1 %).

Barlow Twins and VICReg: decorrelate the dimensions

Instead of negatives, regularise the statistics of the batch, dimension by dimension.

Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al., 2021): the cross-correlation matrix $\mathcal C$ of the two branches' standardised $\bz$ should be the identity:

$$\cL_{BT}=\sum_j(1-\mathcal C_{jj})^2+\lambda\sum_j\sum_{k\ne j}\mathcal C_{jk}^2$$

VICReg (Bardes, Ponce and LeCun, 2022), three named terms on a batch $\mathbf Z$:

  • variance: $\frac1d\sum_j\max(0,1-\mathrm{std}(z_j))$, against collapse
  • invariance: the mean squared distance of positives
  • covariance: $\frac1d\sum_{j\ne k}C_{jk}^2$, against redundant dimensions

Weights 25, 25, 1. No negatives, no stop-gradient, no momentum encoder: the anti-collapse term is explicit.

Masked autoencoders: BERT for images

MAE (He et al., 2022): cut the image into patches (ViT tokens), mask a random 75 %, reconstruct their pixels.

  • the encoder (a ViT) runs on the visible patches only: 4 times fewer tokens, training 3 times faster or more
  • a light decoder gets the encoded patches plus mask tokens and predicts the pixels
  • loss: mean squared error on the masked patches only

Why 75 % and not BERT's 15 %? Images are redundant: a missing patch can be filled from its neighbours by interpolation. Only a high ratio forces the network to understand the object.

Results: excellent after fine-tuning (ViT-H: 87.8 % on ImageNet), weaker with a linear probe than contrastive features. The decoder is thrown away, like the projection head.

DINO: a student imitates a teacher

DINO (Caron et al., 2021): self-distillation with no labels. A student and a teacher with the same architecture output a softmax over $K$ "prototype" classes; the student must match the teacher (cross-entropy).

Teacher = moving average of the student (as BYOL). Multi-crop: the teacher sees 2 large crops, the student also several small ones: "predict the global view from a local one".

Against collapse: the teacher's outputs are centred (a running mean is subtracted, so no class dominates) and sharpened (a low temperature, so the output is not uniform).

With ViTs, the attention maps segment the objects without ever seeing a mask. DINOv2 (2023), trained on 142 million curated images, gives general-purpose features used frozen.

CLIP: contrastive learning across modalities

CLIP (Radford et al., 2021): an image encoder and a text encoder, trained on 400 million image–caption pairs from the web. The positives are the true pairs; the other captions of the batch are the negatives.

Loss: InfoNCE on the $N\times N$ matrix, along the rows (image → text) and the columns (text → image); a learned temperature; batches of 32,768.

Zero-shot classification: write one prompt per class, "a photo of a {class}", embed them, and pick the class whose prompt is most similar to the image. No training example of the task: 76.2 % on ImageNet.

The same shared embedding space powers text-to-image search: the RAG retrieval of chapter 6, with images as documents. Insurance: "rear bumper dent" retrieves the matching claim photos.

Evaluating a representation: the few-label curve

Linear probe: logistic regression on frozen $\bh$: are the classes linearly separable? kNN: cosine neighbours on centred, normalised $\bh$, no training: do neighbours share the class? Fine-tuning: train everything from $f$. Transfer: the same on another dataset.

The lab, MNIST, $m$ labels per class, test on 10,000 digits: with 10 labels per class the probe on SimCLR features gets 92.5 %, the CNN trained from scratch 88.0 %, logistic regression on pixels 73.2 %. With 1,000 per class: 97.8 % against 96.5 %.

The gain is largest where labels are scarce and shrinks as they grow: self-supervision substitutes for labels, it does not add information that many labels would not give.

What the encoder learned without labels

t-SNE of $\bh$ for test digits, coloured by the true digit, which the encoder never saw.

Random weights: only the 1s form an island. After 1,200 steps of SimCLR, ten clusters. A linear probe on these 64 numbers reaches 98.1 % with all labels.

Nobody told the network that there are ten classes. They emerge because two views of a "4" must be close while different images must spread: the easiest way to satisfy both is to group the images by shape.

The contacts follow the shapes: 4 touches 9, 3 touches 5.

When self-supervision pays off

situationwhat to do
many unlabelled in-domain images, few labelsthe sweet spot: pretrain on your own data (SimCLR, MoCo, DINO, MAE), then probe or fine-tune. Claim photos of damaged cars, X-rays, satellite tiles.
general images, little computedo not pretrain yourself: start from a public foundation model (DINOv2, CLIP) and train a linear probe on frozen features.
your domain differs from web photoscontinue the pretraining on your unlabelled data, starting from the public model, before fine-tuning.
categories that change oftenCLIP-style zero-shot with prompts, or kNN on frozen features: a new class needs a few examples, no retraining.
choosing augmentationslist what must not change the answer (lighting, framing, background) and what must (colour of rust, orientation). Augment only the first.
checking for collapsetrack the std of the normalised embeddings and the kNN accuracy of a small labelled monitor set during pretraining.
many labels alreadysupervised training is hard to beat; SSL pretraining still helps robustness and transfer.

Libraries: lightly and solo-learn (SimCLR, MoCo, BYOL, SimSiam, VICReg, DINO in PyTorch); open_clip and the timm / Hugging Face hubs for pretrained weights. The lab builds everything from scratch.

The topic in one slide

A · Learning without labels

  • pretext task: targets computed from the data
  • pretrain on unlabelled data; linear probe, kNN or fine-tune with few labels
  • word2vec, BERT, GPT, autoencoders are self-supervised

B · Contrastive learning

  • positives: two views $t(x)$, $t'(x)$; negatives: the batch
  • $\ell_i=-\log\frac{e^{s_{i,p(i)}/\tau}}{\sum_{k\ne i}e^{s_{ik}/\tau}}$: cross-entropy over $2N-1$ candidates
  • $\tau$ weights hard negatives; alignment + uniformity; probe $\bh$, not $\bz$

C · Without negatives

  • positives only $\Rightarrow$ collapse (complete or dimensional)
  • BYOL, SimSiam: predictor + stop-gradient (or moving-average target)
  • Barlow Twins, VICReg: variance, invariance, covariance

D · Masks, CLIP, practice

  • MAE: mask 75 % of patches, reconstruct pixels; DINO: self-distillation
  • CLIP: image–text InfoNCE, zero-shot by prompts, text-to-image search
  • gains largest with few labels and much in-domain data
