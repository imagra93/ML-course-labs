imanolgranada@gmail.com

Where we are

The course built models that predict well: boosted trees, forests, CNNs. A prediction alone is often not enough. An underwriter, a regulator, a customer and the data scientist who trained the model all ask why.

They ask different questions. Local: why this price for this policy? Global: what drives the prices of the whole portfolio? Debugging: did the model learn something it should not?

Explainable AI (XAI): methods that describe how a trained model turns inputs into outputs, for one prediction or for the model as a whole.

Four parts: A why and what to explain, a running example with a known truth; B global methods (importance, PDP, ICE, ALE, surrogates); C local methods (Shapley values, SHAP, LIME); D neural networks, counterfactuals and practice. It ends with the Extra 9 lab.

Why explain a model?

Debugging

A model can be right for the wrong reason: a "wolf" classifier that detects snow, a pneumonia model that reads the hospital's stamp. In the lab a random policy ID gets more credit than past claims.

Trust

Underwriters and claims handlers act on a score only if it makes sense to them. A fraud flag with "3 claims in 6 months, same garage" gets used; a bare 0.87 gets ignored.

Regulation

GDPR (art. 13–15, 22): "meaningful information about the logic involved" in automated decisions. EU AI Act: risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance and credit scoring are high-risk (annex III), with transparency duties and a right to an explanation (art. 86).

Insight

What drives claims? Which customers react to a price change? An explanation of a good model is a hypothesis about the world: one to test, not a proof.

Explanation ≠ causation. An explanation says how the model uses its inputs. "Power raises the predicted cost by 181 €" does not mean that a smaller car would cause 181 € fewer claims. Causal questions need causal inference (Extra 11).

Fairness is a fifth reason: since 21 December 2012 EU insurers may not price on sex (Test-Achats ruling). A model that never sees sex can still use a proxy of it; explanations help to find out.

A map of the methods

Global: the model as a whole (which features matter, how the prediction moves with each one). Local: one prediction.

Model-agnostic: only needs $f(\bx)$, works for any model. Model-specific: uses the inside (tree structure, gradients).

Interpretable by design (green): linear models, GAMs, small trees. The model is its own explanation. Post-hoc: a second method explains a black box after training; the explanation is an approximation that can be wrong.

Global from local: average many local explanations (mean $|\text{SHAP}|$, beeswarm plots).

Libraries: sklearn.inspection (permutation importance, PDP, ICE), shap, lime, alibi (anchors, counterfactuals), captum (gradients in PyTorch). The lab writes every method from scratch.

Running example: a portfolio with a known truth

6,000 fictional motor policies, 9 features. Simulated, so the true expected cost $\mu(\bx)$ is known and every explanation can be graded:

$$\begin{aligned}\mu=400&+300e^{-\text{lic}/2}+250e^{-(\text{age}-18)/6}+5(\text{age}-60)_+\\&+1.8(\text{kW}-100)+4(\text{kW}-100)_+\mathbb 1[\text{age}\lt 25]\\&+8(\text{km}-14)+120\,\text{urban}-0.15\,\text{ded}+90\,\text{claims}\end{aligned}$$

Observed cost $y=\mu+\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,120^2)$. Even the truth only reaches test $R^2=0.721$.

  • age and licence_years: correlation 0.987, both matter
  • car_value: correlation 0.863 with power, no effect
  • vehicle_age: no effect; power × young: an interaction
  • sex: not an input, but men drive 108.7 kW, women 94.3 kW

Policy A, explained again and again: 21 years old, 2 years of licence, 160 kW, 22,000 km a year, urban, one past claim, no deductible. True expected cost 1,284 €, the average is 507 €.

Interpretable by design

Linear: $\beta_j$ = change per unit of $x_j$, all else fixed. GAM: $f=\beta_0+\sum_jg_j(x_j)$, one free curve per feature (splines): the model is its plot. Small tree: a few if–then rules.

The linear model gets the sign of experience wrong: $\beta_{\text{licence}}=+5.51\pm0.89$ €/year. With correlation 0.987, "one more year of licence at the same age" never occurs, so the two coefficients trade off.

The GAM is as accurate as boosting and closer to the truth: try interpretable models first (Rudin, 2019). We still explain the boosting model from now on: in real portfolios with many interactions it usually wins.

Impurity importance and its bias

Impurity importance (MDI, feature_importances_): the total decrease in squared error of all splits on feature $j$, summed over the trees, normalised to 1. Free, but computed on the training data.

The lab adds a random policy_id (4,000 distinct values) to a forest of fully grown trees. Impurity: 0.043, 8th of 10, ahead of prior_claims (0.032), a real +90 € effect. car_value, no effect, gets more than the deductible.

Why: the deepest splits fit the noise of tiny leaves, and a column with many distinct values offers the most ways to do it. The same bias hits continuous features against binary ones.

On test data, permutation importance: policy_id $-0.1$ €, vehicle_age $-0.1$ €, car_value 0.6 €. Noise does not help on new data.

Permutation importance

How much does the model need feature $j$ on held-out data (Breiman, 2001)?

  1. test error $e_0$ of the fitted model
  2. shuffle column $j$ of the test set, error $e_j$; repeat $R$ times
  3. importance$_j$ = mean of $e_j-e_0$

No refit. With the RMSE the importance is in euros. The lab's version matches sklearn to 0.71 €.

Correlated features. Shuffling age creates impossible rows (85% of the test set: 25 years old, 40 years of licence). And a twin can replace the column: refit without age and the error rises by only 3.2 € (permutation: 29.7 €); without power, 4.0 € (32.6 €), as car_value takes over.

Permutation = how much this model relies on the column. Drop-column (orange bars) = how much information the column adds. Neither is the importance in the world.

Partial dependence and ICE curves

Partial dependence: set feature $j$ to $z$ for every policy, predict, average.

$$\PD_j(z)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nf(z,\bx_{i,-j})$$

If $f=g_j(x_j)+h(\bx_{-j})$ then $\PD_j=g_j+\text{const}$: for an additive model the PDP is exact.

ICE (individual conditional expectation): keep the $n$ curves $z\mapsto f(z,\bx_{i,-j})$. Parallel curves: no interaction. Fanning out: an interaction with another feature.

Lab, 120 → 200 kW: under 25, 3.92 €/kW; 25 or older, 1.16 €/kW; the PDP averages them to 1.50 and hides the interaction (truth: 5.8 and 1.8). Ours = sklearn to $2\times10^{-12}$.

Tree models give staircases: their "slope at a point" is zero. Read effects over ranges.

Correlated features: the PDP extrapolates

$\PD_{\text{age}}(22)$ evaluates every policy with its age overwritten by 22 and its own licence years kept: a 22-year-old with 35 years of licence.

With a correlation of 0.987, 85% of these rows lie outside the data. There the model was never trained and its predictions are arbitrary.

Lab: from age 19 to 40 the PDP of gradient boosting drops by 177 €, the truth by 257 €. At "22 years, 35 years of licence" the trees apply the branches learned for experienced drivers, and the average is pulled down.

The PDP of the truth is right only because $\mu$ is defined, and additive in age, everywhere. A fitted model has no such guarantee off the data. Permutation importance suffers from the same impossible rows.

ALE: accumulated local effects

Apley and Zhu (2020). Cut $x_j$ at its quantiles $z_0\lt\dots\lt z_K$. In bin $k$, use only its own policies, moved to the two edges:

$$\Delta_k=\frac1{n_k}\sum_{i\in\text{bin }k}\big[f(z_k,\bx_{i,-j})-f(z_{k-1},\bx_{i,-j})\big]$$

$\ALE_j(z_K)=\sum_{k\le K}\Delta_k-\text{const}$ (centred on the data).

Each row moves only within a small bin, so it stays a real policy. For a linear model every $\Delta_k/(z_k-z_{k-1})=\beta_j$: checked in the lab.

Effect of being 18, lab: truth 244 €. Boosting: ALE 184, PDP 165. Forest: ALE 139, PDP 64.

ALE fixes extrapolation, not attribution: at correlation 0.987 the data barely say whether youth or inexperience is to blame, and both models give part of the age effect to licence years.

Global surrogate: a readable copy

Fit an interpretable model to the predictions $f(\bx_i)$ of the black box, not to $y$, and read it instead.

Fidelity: $R^2$ between surrogate and black box on new data. Low fidelity = an explanation of a different model.

Lab, depth 3: fidelity 0.657. Depth 5: 0.767, depth 8: 0.862, no longer readable. The trade-off is unavoidable.

The rules make sense: the most expensive leaf is licence ≤ 1.4 years, power > 124.8 kW, age ≤ 26.1: 1,185 € for 85 policies. The young-novice-powerful-car interaction, found without knowing the truth.

Good for a slide to management; a third of what the model does is not in it.

Shapley values: features as players

Shapley (1953): $M$ players earn a payoff together; split it fairly. Players = features, payoff of a coalition $S$ = the expected prediction when only $\bx_S$ is known:

$$v(S)=\E\big[f(\bx_S,\bX_{\bar S})\big],\qquad v(\varnothing)=\E[f(\bX)],\quad v(\text{all})=f(\bx)$$

Let the features join one by one in a random order; $\phi_j$ = the average gain when $j$ joins:

$$\phi_j=\sum_{S\not\ni j}\frac{|S|!\,(M-|S|-1)!}{M!}\big[v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)\big]$$

The weight is the share of the $M!$ orders in which exactly $S$ arrives before $j$. Lundberg and Lee (2017) named these attributions SHAP values.

A worked example with three features

Toy pricing model, with an interaction:

$$f=300+200Y+2(P-100)+2Y(P-100)+100U$$

The four axioms

Efficiency

$\sum_j\phi_j=f(\bx)-v(\varnothing)$: the attributions add up to the prediction minus the average. Policy A: 580.1 € of SHAP values = 1,108 − 528. Checked for all 2,000 test policies to $5\times10^{-13}$.

Symmetry

If $v(S\cup\{i\})=v(S\cup\{j\})$ for every $S$, then $\phi_i=\phi_j$. Two features that always contribute the same get the same credit ($x_1x_2$ at $(2,2)$: $2+2$).

Dummy

A feature that never changes $v$ gets 0. With the truth $\mu$ as the model, car_value and vehicle_age get exactly 0; the boosting model gives them $-2.6$ and $-9.7$ €: fitted noise.

Additivity

The values for $f+g$ are the sums of the values for $f$ and $g$. A forest's SHAP values are the average of its trees'. Linear model: $\phi_j=\beta_j(x_j-\E X_j)$, checked in the lab.

Uniqueness (Shapley, 1953): the Shapley value is the only attribution with these four properties. Any other split of $f(\bx)-v(\varnothing)$ breaks at least one of them.

Price: $2^M$ coalitions, each an average over a background sample. With $M=9$ and 100 background rows: 51,200 predictions, 0.1 s. With $M=50$: impossible exactly, hence KernelSHAP and TreeSHAP.

Interventional or conditional value function?

Interventional $v(S)=\E[f(\bx_S,\bX_{\bar S})]$: fill the missing features from the background, ignoring $\bx_S$. Same impossible rows as the PDP.

Conditional (observational) $v(S)=\E[f(\bx_S,\bX_{\bar S})\mid\bX_S=\bx_S]$: fill them with values plausible given $\bx_S$ (lab: 50 nearest neighbours).

Lab, policy A, the truth $\mu$ as the model: car_value gets 0 € interventional, 50.3 € conditional; power falls from 203 to 164 €. $\mu$ never reads car_value, but a 35 k€ car reveals a powerful one.

Interventional = true to the model: auditing, debugging, a regulator asking what the model uses. Conditional = true to the data: what information signals risk, but a feature gets credit without being used.

shap: interventional with a background by default; TreeSHAP's "tree path dependent" mode approximates the conditional one.

KernelSHAP and TreeSHAP

Encode a coalition as $\mathbf z\in\{0,1\}^M$. Fit $g(\mathbf z)=\phi_0+\sum_j\phi_jz_j$ to $v(\mathbf z)$ by weighted least squares with the Shapley kernel:

$$\pi(\mathbf z)=\frac{M-1}{\binom{M}{|\mathbf z|}\,|\mathbf z|\,(M-|\mathbf z|)}$$

with $\phi_0=v(\varnothing)$ and $\sum\phi_j=f(\bx)-v(\varnothing)$ imposed. With all coalitions the solution is the Shapley values (Lundberg and Lee, 2017).

Lab: all 510 coalitions, exact to $2\times10^{-13}$. Sampled: 16 coalitions, 29.8 € error; 64, 2.45 €; 1,024, 0.52 €. Small and large coalitions get the weight: they isolate single features.

TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al., 2020): exact SHAP values of tree ensembles in polynomial time, by walking each tree once. Milliseconds per prediction for XGBoost, LightGBM, forests: the default in practice.

Reading SHAP: the waterfall of one policy

Policy A with the boosting model, 512 coalitions, 100 background policies. The format to show an underwriter or a customer: in euros, from the average to this price.

Grey column: the same attribution with the truth as the model. Same story, same order; the model is 176 € below the true cost (1,108 vs 1,284 €), mostly in power (+181 vs +228) and age (+163 vs +226): it learned only part of the young-driver interaction.

SHAP explains the model, errors included: vehicle_age −10 €, car_value −3 € are noise the trees fitted.

Order matters for the picture, not for the numbers: the values do not depend on the order of the bars.

From local to global: beeswarm and dependence plots

Beeswarm: one dot per policy and feature, at its SHAP value, coloured by the feature value. Spread = importance, colour = direction: high deductibles (red) lower the cost, the few novices (blue licence) add up to 300 €.

Mean $|\text{SHAP}|$ ranks licence years, urban, power, mileage, age. Permutation ranks power and age higher and urban lower: moving predictions is not the same as reducing error.

Dependence plot: SHAP value of power against power. Above 100 kW the drivers under 25 lie above the others: the vertical spread at one value is the interaction.

Exact values for all 2,000 test policies in the lab (background of 20): under a minute. The additivity check holds for every one of them.

LIME: a local linear surrogate

Ribeiro, Singh and Guestrin (2016): a linear model that only has to be right near $\bx$.

$$g=\arg\min_g\sum_k\pi_\bx(\mathbf z_k)\big(f(\mathbf z_k)-g(\mathbf z_k)\big)^2+\Omega(g)$$

$\pi_\bx(\mathbf z)=e^{-\norm{\mathbf z-\bx}^2/\sigma^2}$. Perturb, predict, weight, fit: the slopes of $g$ are the explanation.

Lab, policy A: age slope $-24.3$ €/year at $\sigma=0.75$ (truth $-25.3$), $-11.4$ at $\sigma=10$. Curved effects have no single slope, and LIME does not say which $\sigma$ to use.

Unstable: at $\sigma=1$ with 200 samples, 20 seeds give 4 different top-3 feature sets; with 5,000 samples, one.

Gradients and Integrated Gradients

Saliency $|\partial f_c/\partial x_j|$: one backward pass to the input. Gradient × input $x_j\,\partial f_c/\partial x_j$.

Both fail where the network saturates: $f=\tanh(x_1+2x_2)$ at $\bx=(1,1)$ has gradient $(0.0099, 0.0197)$, yet the inputs moved $f$ from 0 to 0.995.

Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017): average the gradient on the path from a baseline $\bx'$:

$$\IG_j=(x_j-x'_j)\int_0^1\frac{\partial f(\bx'+\alpha(\bx-\bx'))}{\partial x_j}\,d\alpha$$

Completeness: $\sum_j\IG_j=f(\bx)-f(\bx')$, the efficiency axiom. Lab (CNN, MNIST): 0.11% error with 64 steps, 0.02% with 256.

The baseline is a choice: black image, blurred image, average policy. IG explains $f(\bx)$ relative to it.

Grad-CAM, and a sanity check

Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017): weight each feature map $A^k$ of the last convolution by its average gradient, $\alpha_k=\frac1{HW}\sum_{u,v}\partial f_c/\partial A^k_{uv}$, and show $\mathrm{ReLU}(\sum_k\alpha_kA^k)$ upsampled. Coarse, class-specific.

Sanity check (Adebayo et al., 2018): randomise the layers from the top down. A map of the model must change.

Lab, all layers random, rank correlation with the trained maps: saliency 0.15, Grad-CAM 0.09, but gradient × input and IG 0.99: the black background is zero in both. On the digit pixels alone: saliency $-0.01$, IG still 0.28.

A map that looks like the digit is not evidence that the model looks at the digit. Run the test before trusting a method.

Counterfactual explanations

"What is the smallest change that flips the decision?" (Wachter et al., 2017). Refer to underwriting if $f(\bx)\gt\tau=900$ €:

$$\xcf=\arg\min_{\bx'}\ \lambda\big(f(\bx')-\tau\big)_+^2+\sum_j\frac{|x'_j-x_j|}{s_j}$$

Actionable: only power, mileage, deductible may move; never age or past claims. Plausible: within the data, valid levels (no 159 € deductible). L1 gives sparse changes (widget: policy B, rural, no claims, true cost 1,074 €).

Lab: on the MLP, power 160 → 100 kW and mileage 22 → 17 (899 €). A grid search on boosting: a car of 110 kW, nothing else (862 €). Keeping the car: no solution.

Recourse: "with a car of at most 110 kW you would not be referred". Never "be older". Anchors (Ribeiro et al., 2018) give the dual: a rule that keeps the decision, e.g. "licence ≤ 1 year and power > 125 kW ⇒ referred".

Pitfalls, and a fairness check by group

  • Correlated features: impossible rows (PDP, permutation, interventional SHAP), credit split arbitrarily between twins. Look at groups; use ALE; report drop-column too.
  • Model ≠ world: an explanation of a wrong model is a correct description of a wrong model. Check accuracy first; never read SHAP as causal effects.
  • Instability: LIME seeds, KernelSHAP samples, background choice. Repeat and report the spread.
  • Fairness by group: sex is not an input, yet the model predicts 21 € more for men (truth 22 €). SHAP gaps add up exactly to the gap: +28.7 € through power.

Here power has a real effect; had the gap come through a feature with none (car_value), it would be proxy discrimination. Explanations locate a gap; judging it is a legal and ethical decision.
