We can compute every gradient. Three questions remain:

  1. How much data per step? Whole dataset, one example, or a mini-batch.
  2. How to turn a gradient into a step? Vanilla gradient descent is slow and fragile on non-convex losses: optimizers (momentum, RMSProp, Adam) and learning-rate schedules.
  3. How to avoid overfitting? Weight decay, dropout, data augmentation, early stopping; and layers that make training stable (BatchNorm).

All of it is "knobs" on the same loop: forward, loss, backward, update. None of it changes the model's formula.

Ends with the layer zoo, a debugging recipe and a cheat-sheet of defaults.

Gradient Descent for NN:

To optimize the weights of a neural network we apply gradient descent, but:

  • Multi-layer NN are not convex.
    • Small NN → seed it at random points.
    • Large NN have more freedom and will likely escape from potential local-minima traps (empirical observation).
    • Optimizers → different from vanilla gradient descent.
  • Some activation functions are not differentiable.
    • Only at some points (e.g. 0 for ReLU).
    • We still have sub-derivatives.

Why does gradient descent work so well on non-convex nets? · ReLU and backprop

In high dimensions most flat points are saddle points, not bad local minima: some directions go up, others go down. Noise in the gradient (mini-batches) and momentum help to leave them.

Vocabulary: epoch, mini-batch, iteration

mini-batch
a few examples (16–256) processed together, as one matrix.
iteration / step
one gradient update, computed on one mini-batch.
epoch
one full pass over the training set: $m/|\mathcal B|$ iterations.

Example: 50,000 images, batch 64 → 782 iterations per epoch; 20 epochs → 15,640 updates.

In PyTorch: DataLoader(dataset, batch_size=64, shuffle=True) does the shuffling and cutting; the inner for xb, yb in loader is one epoch.

Gradient Descent variations:

  • Batch gradient descent: feed the entire dataset, average the cost, calculate derivatives and update. Used in LinReg and LogReg (convex problems).
  • Stochastic gradient descent: feed 1 sample, calculate the cost and derivatives, and update. Many more updates, not always towards the minimum (which can be good with non-convexity).
  • Mini-batch gradient descent: feed a mini-batch of samples, average the cost, calculate derivatives and update.
    • The same benefits as stochastic, but the calculation for the mini-batch is vectorized: speed.
    • Typically 16, 32, 64 or 128 (computers are prepared to parallelize this).
$$\bth:=\bth-\alpha\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal B|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}\nabla_{\bth}\ell\big(\bx^{(i)},y^{(i)};\bth\big)$$

The mini-batch gradient is an unbiased but noisy estimate of the full one: its variance shrinks like $1/|\mathcal B|$.

The learning rate is the most important knob

Too low: the loss creeps down; you run out of patience. Too high: it oscillates, or explodes to NaN.

How to pick it: try powers of 10 ($10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}$), or run a range test (right plot): increase the rate every step and watch the loss; use the value just before it stops falling steeply.

Defaults that usually work: Adam $10^{-3}$, SGD with momentum $10^{-1}$ (with a schedule). Fine-tuning a pre-trained net: 10–100× smaller.

Optimizers - Idea: momentum

Mini-batch updates are noisy, and valleys are narrow in some directions and flat in others. First idea: use momentum!

$$\bm\leftarrow\beta\,\bm+(1-\beta)\,\nabla_{\bth}J,\qquad\bth\leftarrow\bth-\alpha\,\bm$$

What is $\bm$? Unroll it: $\ \bm_t=(1-\beta)\sum_{k=1}^t\beta^{t-k}\bg_k$. An exponential moving average of the past gradients, over roughly the last $\frac{1}{1-\beta}$ steps ($\beta=0.9$ → 10 steps).

Components that keep the same sign (the flat direction) add up; components that flip sign (the zig-zag across the valley) cancel out. Like a heavy ball rolling downhill: it keeps going through small bumps and plateaus.

Optimizers - RMSProp and Adam

RMSProp divides each parameter's step by a running RMS of its gradient: every parameter moves at a similar rate, whatever its gradient scale.

$$\bs\leftarrow\beta_2\bs+(1-\beta_2)\,\bg\odot\bg,\qquad\bth\leftarrow\bth-\alpha\frac{\bg}{\sqrt{\bs}+\varepsilon}$$

Why it matters: a bias of the last layer and a weight of the first layer can have gradients 1000× apart. One learning rate cannot suit both. RMSProp gives each its own.

Adam = momentum + RMSProp + bias correction:

$$\bm_t=\beta_1\bm_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)\bg_t,\quad\bs_t=\beta_2\bs_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)\bg_t^2$$
$$\hat\bm_t=\frac{\bm_t}{1-\beta_1^t},\quad\hat\bs_t=\frac{\bs_t}{1-\beta_2^t}$$$$\bth_t=\bth_{t-1}-\alpha\frac{\hat\bm_t}{\sqrt{\hat\bs_t}+\varepsilon}$$

Defaults: $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$, $\alpha=10^{-3}$. The most popular method.

Why the bias correction? Both averages start at 0, so early on they are too small: $\E[\bm_t]=(1-\beta_1^t)\,\E[\bg]$ (a geometric series). Dividing by $1-\beta_1^t$ fixes the first steps; after ~100 steps the factor is 1.

Rule of thumb. Start with Adam, α = 10⁻³, batch 32–64. If it trains, try halving / doubling α once. If a paper you are reproducing uses SGD + momentum, copy its schedule.

Bigger batches need bigger learning rates (the gradient is less noisy): a common recipe scales α linearly with the batch size. And fine-tuning a pre-trained network needs a much smaller α than training from scratch.

Learning-rate schedules and the training loop

Large steps early to move fast, small steps late to settle. Warm-up avoids huge first updates while Adam's statistics are still poor (standard for transformers). Reduce on plateau divides α when the validation loss stalls.

model = MLP()
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(),
                        lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1e-2)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(
            opt, T_max=epochs)
for epoch in range(epochs):
    model.train()                    # dropout / BN on
    for xb, yb in train_loader:      # mini-batches
        loss = loss_fn(model(xb), yb)
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()              # backprop
        opt.step()                   # update
    sched.step()
    model.eval()                     # dropout off, BN stats
    with torch.no_grad():
        val_loss = evaluate(model, val_loader)

Every layer is a function tensor in → tensor out. To design or debug a network, write the shape after each layer: the first number is always the batch size.

Overfitting vs underfitting in NN:

In neural networks we should always verify that the model is generalizing well. It is quite easy to overfit your data: plot the learning curves (train and validation loss per epoch).

If your model under-fits your data:

  • Make your model deeper (or wider).
  • Try new architectures.
  • Train longer, tune the learning rate.

If your model overfits your data:

  • Use more data (also data augmentation).
  • Simplify your architecture (depth or number of units).
  • Apply L1 and/or L2 regularization to the weights; apply dropout; early stopping. Combine many of the above.

Regularization: weight decay, augmentation, early stopping

Add $\frac\lambda2\norm{\bth}^2$ to the loss. The gradient gains $\lambda\bth$, so every SGD step first shrinks the weights:

$$\bth\leftarrow(1-\alpha\lambda)\,\bth-\alpha\,\nabla_{\bth}J$$

Same as Ridge in the ML part: a Gaussian prior on the weights, smoother functions.

AdamW. With Adam, adding $\lambda\bth$ to the gradient gets rescaled by $1/\sqrt{\hat\bs}$, so large-gradient weights are barely decayed. AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights, separate from the adaptive step. It is the standard for transformers.

Data augmentation. Random, label-preserving transformations of the training inputs each epoch (flips, crops, rotations, colour jitter; noise for audio). The model rarely sees the same input twice: like having more data, and it builds in the invariances. Pictures in chapter 4.

Only use transformations that don't change the label: a horizontal flip is fine for clothes, not for digits.

Early stopping. Monitor the validation loss, keep the best checkpoint, stop after patience epochs without improvement. Training time acts as a regularizer.

Dropout:

0.30
units off: –

Inactivate some neurons in training (helps with regularization), controlled by a "rate" $p$. All neurons are used in validation / prediction!

Inverted dropout: survivors are scaled up so the expectation is preserved:

$$\tilde a_j=\frac{r_j}{1-p}\,a_j,\qquad r_j\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-p)$$$$\E[\tilde a_j]=\frac{1-p}{1-p}\,a_j=a_j$$
  • A unit cannot rely on any other unit being present: more robust, redundant features.
  • Like training a huge ensemble of thinned networks that share weights (compare bagging).
  • Typical $p$: 0.1–0.5, on dense layers; rarely on conv layers. Validation loss below training loss? Normal with dropout: training runs a handicapped network.

Batchnorm layer:

Helps correct training that is slow or unstable. In gradient-based algorithms it's a good idea to put all your data on a common scale; maybe also normalizing inside the network would be better!

A batch-normalization layer looks at each batch as it comes in, first normalizing it with its own mean and standard deviation, then putting it on a new scale with two trainable parameters per feature:

$$\mu_{\mathcal B}=\frac1m\sum_iz_i,\qquad\sigma^2_{\mathcal B}=\frac1m\sum_i(z_i-\mu_{\mathcal B})^2$$$$\hat z_i=\frac{z_i-\mu_{\mathcal B}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{\mathcal B}+\varepsilon}},\qquad y_i=\gamma\hat z_i+\beta$$
  • Higher learning rates, faster training, mild regularization (batch noise).
  • Train vs eval: batch statistics in training; running averages at test time (model.eval()). Forgetting eval() is a classic bug.
  • Where? Usually Linear/Conv → BatchNorm → ReLU. In CNNs, one $\gamma,\beta$ per channel.

BatchNorm vs LayerNorm: which numbers are normalized?

BatchNorm: for each feature, mean and variance over the mini-batch. Needs a reasonably large batch and behaves differently in train / eval. Standard in CNNs.

LayerNorm: for each example, mean and variance over its own features. Independent of the batch and of the other examples, identical in train and eval. Standard in RNNs and transformers (chapters 5–6), where the "batch" is a variable-length sequence.

Both end with the same learnable $\gamma\hat z+\beta$. For images, BatchNorm2d normalizes each channel over batch × height × width.
